Measuring With Your Own Hands同身寸: The Cun System

Classical locations read like riddles: two cun above the wrist, three below the knee. What is a cun, and where do you buy a ruler marked in them? You already own one. This guide explains the body-proportional system behind point location — finger measures, landmark tables, and the habit of measuring with your own hands.

“You already own one.”

By the Acupoints editorsAug 31, 20267 MIN readReviewed Aug 26, 2026
七則

Seven short parts

One idea each.
  1. 一一寸究竟有多長

    How Long Is a Cun?

    Open almost any classical point description and you meet the same style of direction: two cun above the wrist crease, three cun below the outer eye of the knee, four above the navel. To a newcomer this reads like a riddle. No shop sells rulers marked in cun, and even with one in hand, the numbers seem to assume you already know where the counting starts. The riddle unties itself the moment you learn what sort of unit a cun really is.

    Cun (寸) — pronounced cùn, a falling fourth tone — takes its character from an old Chinese unit of length, once used for cloth, land, and carpentry. Point location borrowed the word and quietly changed its nature: in classical practice, a cun stopped being a fixed stretch of ruler and became a proportion of the person being measured. The same character, a different idea entirely.

    This site’s glossary states it plainly: cun is a body-proportional unit used to locate acupoints, roughly the width of your own thumb at the knuckle. That one sentence carries the whole system. Every “so many cun” in a location string describes a share of your body, counted off in your own hands and bones — which is why the same instruction serves a tall reader and a small one without changing a word.

  2. 二為什麼身體本身就是尺子

    Why Your Body Is the Ruler

    The logic shows clearest by imagining the alternative. Suppose points were found with an ordinary ruler, a fixed span above every wrist regardless of whose it was. Bones do not grow to a common template, so a measurement suited to broad forearms would wander badly on a child’s. Classical practice met this problem long ago and answered it with economy: make the body its own reference.

    The tradition even gave the idea a name — tóngshēncùn (同身寸), the “same-body inch”: measurements drawn from the very body they will be used on. Your hands measure your points; a friend’s hands measure theirs. Because the unit grows and shrinks with the person, a location written down centuries ago still fits a body today. What travels across time is the ratio, never the distance.

    A quieter benefit hides in the arrangement. Measuring yourself in your own proportions turns locating points from memorising coordinates into reading your own geography. Many beginners say that learning to count cun was the moment acupressure stopped feeling like a foreign subject and began to feel like literacy — the page of directions suddenly made sense.

    同身寸the same-body inch
  3. 三手指同身

    The Finger Measures

    For daily self-care, the tradition keeps a set of ready-made rulers folded into your hands. The most quoted: one cun is the width of your own thumb at its knuckle. Lay the thumb sideways across your arm and you hold a portable single-unit gauge. A close cousin works with the middle finger: bend it, and the span between the two skin creases showing at its side also counts as about one cun.

    Wider spans come from stacking. Two fingers held together — index and middle — reach roughly one and a half cun. Three fingers cover about two, which is why guides describe the inner-forearm point Neiguan (PC6) both as “two cun above the wrist crease” and “three finger-widths up”: two phrasings, one address. Four fingers held snugly together, sighted across the middle knuckles, span three cun — the measure old texts called a “fu”, and the most useful wide gauge in home practice.

    A few habits keep these measures honest. Stack fingers edge to edge and sight across the knuckles, not the tapering tips. Always begin from the named landmark — a crease, a bone tip — rather than wherever the hand happens to fall. And grant the results their dignity as approximations: finger measures deliver you to the neighbourhood; the gentle search described below finds the door.

  4. 四骨度分寸

    The Landmark Tables: Measuring by Bones

    Fingers swell with heat, tire by evening, and differ from neighbour to neighbour. So classical practice kept a second, more formal instrument beside them: the bone-length method, gǔdù fēncùnfǎ (骨度分寸法), which divides the distances between fixed skeletal landmarks into constant cun counts. Its insight is that bones scale faithfully — the run from elbow crease to wrist crease is always twelve cun, and those twelve divisions grow and shrink with the forearm itself.

    A handful of entries from these tables covers most of home practice. Twelve cun lie between the elbow crease and the wrist crease; nine between the armpit fold and the elbow. On the torso, the notch where the breastbone meets the abdomen sits eight cun above the navel, and the navel five above the top of the pubic bone. Across the chest, the span from nipple to nipple counts eight. Behind, sixteen cun run from the crease at the back of the knee down to the tip of the outer ankle.

    These numbers invite a little arithmetic. Half of eight is four, which sets Zhongwan (CV12) neatly midway between navel and breastbone-notch — the cleanest demonstration of a table turning into a finding. In home practice, halves and quarters matter far more than decimal places; the tradition’s own instinct was to fold a span in half, and in half again, long before precision instruments entered the story.

  5. 五上手一量

    Putting It to Work: Three Stops, Measured

    The theory settles into habit quickly with two familiar stops. Sit down and rest one forearm palm-up on a table. Find the wrist crease nearest the palm. Lay the three centre fingers of the other hand — index, middle, ring — snugly against the crease, edges touching. Beneath the far edge of the ring finger lies the ground two cun up; press gently between the two central tendons there and you are visiting Neiguan (PC6), whose fuller record waits in this site’s catalogue.

    Second stop, the lower leg. Seated and easy, find the small hollow just below the kneecap and toward its outer side — the old “eye of the knee”. Set the four fingers of the other hand snugly beneath it, aligned and pointing straight down the shin. Three cun below the knee-eye, in the muscle just outside the shinbone, your fingertips arrive at Zusanli (ST36); flexing the foot makes the muscle there firm up obligingly under your touch.

    A third stop reuses what you already know: Sanyinjiao (SP6) waits three cun above the tip of the inner ankle bone — the same four-finger span you just used at the knee, carried to the ankle — just behind the shinbone’s inner edge. Pregnancy rules this point out entirely — anyone expecting leaves it alone until after the birth. Work each stop for one to two minutes, breathing slowly; wrist and knee alike recur through this site’s condition guides, so these two measurements will keep serving you elsewhere.

  6. 六不必量的時候

    When Not to Measure at All

    For all this apparatus, some of the tradition’s best-loved points decline rulers altogether. Hegu (LI4) lives in the fleshy web between thumb and index finger — no cun required, only the flat of the opposite thumb slid toward the meeting of the bones until the muscle answers. Plenty of head and neck points behave the same way, announcing themselves under slow fingertip exploration. Measurement narrows the field; touch chooses the spot.

    Experienced teachers rank sensation above geometry, and beginners may simply adopt the ranking. When a careful count leaves you near the textbook address and the tissue there responds — a dull ache, a spreading heaviness, a texture unlike its surroundings — believe the response over the millimetre. Points come in symmetrical pairs, and one side often speaks more plainly than the other; the livelier side is traditionally the one to press.

    Consistency, finally, outranks precision. Returning to roughly the same place, pressed in roughly the same way, builds familiarity faster than any amount of worry over half-widths. Measure honestly, search gently, accept the finding, and come back tomorrow — the system asks for good faith, not callipers.

    Hegu is traditionally avoided during pregnancy. LI4: not inpregnancy
  7. 七誠實的度量能做什麼

    What Honest Measuring Can and Cannot Do

    Be clear about what the cun system promises. It promises repeatability: a road back to yesterday’s spot, and a shared vocabulary between you, this guide, and every book that writes “two cun above the wrist”. It does not promise outcomes. Accurate location is craft, not therapy — the tradition treats finding the point as the beginning of practice, never the effect itself.

    Hold expectations modestly and the practice tends to repay them: a few minutes of unhurried pressure, a slower breath, a body given attention. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and nothing among them promotes a well-measured fingertip into a treatment. Take any ease that follows a session as a pleasant companion to slowing down, not a result you ordered.

    While you measure, keep the safety lines bright. Press healthy skin only — never broken, irritated, or numb areas, and never into swelling you cannot account for. Stop at sharp, electric, or radiating sensation; ease away rather than push through. If you are pregnant or living with a chronic condition, ask a qualified practitioner before adding point work to your days. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

問

Frequently asked

Do I need a special tool to measure cun?
No — hands were the original instruments and remain the best ones for self-care. Thumb width, stacked fingers, and the bony landmarks of your own arms and legs cover nearly everything a beginner needs. Tape measures and elastic cun rulers do exist, but they carry back the very problem proportional measuring removes: a tool cannot know whose body it is lying on.
My hands are much larger than average. Won’t my points be measured wrongly?
No — that is the system working, not failing. Your hands scale with your frame, so measuring your arm with your own fingers lands where a smaller person’s smaller fingers would land them. If proportions still feel uncertain, lean on the landmark tables: elbows, wrists, and ankles divide into the same cun counts on every body.
Why do guides disagree — is PC6 two thumb-widths or three fingers above the wrist?
Both descriptions aim at the same two-cun mark. Thumbs and stacked fingers are different gauges for one span, each approximate, and translations round differently besides. Treat small disagreements as alternative routes to one address, press gently to confirm, and let the tissue’s reply settle the question.
Is a cun the same as an inch?
Relatives, not twins. The character took its name from an old Chinese unit of length, yet in point location a cun is a proportion of the person measured — a tall reader’s cun is longer than a slight reader’s. Never convert a location into inches or centimetres with a tape; the ratio is the meaning, and the tape drops it.
How precise does my measuring need to be?
Roughly right beats exactly wrong. Finger measures carry an honest slop of half a finger-width or so, and the tradition expects you to finish by feel: press gently around the counted spot and look for that dull, spreading responsiveness. Once found, coming back to approximately the same place matters far more than landing on the millimetre.
Can I measure points on someone else?
Yes, with their consent and their proportions: measure their body with their hand spans or with the bony landmarks, never with yours. Keep touch light, stay on healthy skin, and leave alone any point a practitioner has flagged for them. Remember, too, that several points are traditionally avoided during pregnancy — Hegu (LI4) among them — while Sanyinjiao (SP6) is left out entirely.
