Neck and shoulder strain from desk work

Long hours at a desk can leave the neck stiff and the shoulders heavy. Breaks, a better set-up and neck exercise come first; this page then adds three traditional points as an optional comfort pause, fengchi (GB20), jianjing (GB21) and quchi (LI11), with notes on what the research found.

急

Get medical help now if

  • numbness, tingling or weakness spreads into an arm or hand
  • your hands turn clumsy or your walking becomes unsteady
  • bladder or bowel control changes
  • fever or unexplained weight loss comes with the pain, or it never lets up at night
  • the pain followed a fall, an accident or a blow
  • you have dizziness or blackouts

The national nursing protocol describes it arising from slowly accumulated strain, from wind and cold settling in, and from age, and it sorts sufferers into patterns: wind-cold obstruction, with stiffness that worsens in a draught; blood stasis and qi stagnation, with pain fixed in one spot; phlegm and damp blocking the channels, with a heavy head; liver and kidney depletion; and depletion of qi and blood, with tiredness. Its everyday advice follows the frame: guard daily habits and keep the neck out of wind and cold.

Clinical research

  1. Randomised trial · Matsubara T et al. · 2011

    Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine, 2011

    In 33 women with chronic neck pain, hand pressure at shoulder points or at arm points eased pain, neck disability and anxiety compared with no treatment; only the shoulder points changed heart-rate measures. There was no sham pressing.

  2. Randomised trial · Kim M & Kim J · 2021

    Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine, 2021

    Middle-aged women with chronic neck pain who had acupressure twice a week for four weeks reported less pain and disability and moved the neck further than untreated women. The control had no sham pressing.

    Annals of Internal Medicine, 2024

一

Desk Neck, and What Comes First

The picture is familiar to anyone who works at a screen: by mid-afternoon the back of the neck feels tight, the tops of the shoulders feel loaded, and turning the head to check a second monitor takes more effort than it did at nine. The head has drifted forward, the chin has crept towards the keyboard, and the arms have been held out in front of the body for hours with nothing to rest on. It is ordinary strain, not a diagnosis, and it usually eases with movement and rest.

Before any point is pressed, three habits do most of the work. First, breaks: the 2026 nursing protocol of the China Association of Chinese Medicine asks desk workers to avoid long stretches of head-down work and to move the neck every one to two hours, by tipping the head back, resting it against the chair back, or turning it slowly side to side. Short, regular pauses are easier to keep than long, rare ones, and a phone timer is a perfectly respectable tool.

Second, the set-up. Let the forearms rest on the desk or on chair arms instead of hovering, so the shoulders can drop. Raise the screen so you look straight ahead rather than down, and set the chair so both feet reach the floor, as the same protocol advises. A laptop on a low table is the usual culprit; a stand and a separate keyboard often change more than any amount of pressing.

Third, strength. The protocol keeps returning to simple exercise for the neck and shoulders: shrugging, opening the chest, and pressing the head gently back against clasped hands while the hands push forward, done slowly and stopped short of fatigue. Strong, used muscles cope better with long days than rested, unused ones. Acupressure comes after all this, as an optional comfort measure, not a replacement.

二

What Chinese Medicine Reaches For

Chinese medicine files lasting neck and shoulder stiffness under the name xiangbi, a painful obstruction of the nape. The national nursing protocol describes it arising from slowly accumulated strain, from wind and cold settling in, and from age, and it sorts sufferers into patterns: wind-cold obstruction, with stiffness that worsens in a draught; blood stasis and qi stagnation, with pain fixed in one spot; phlegm and damp blocking the channels, with a heavy head; liver and kidney depletion; and depletion of qi and blood, with tiredness. Its everyday advice follows the frame: guard daily habits and keep the neck out of wind and cold.

The points on this page come from that tradition and from the site’s classical catalogue. Fengchi (GB20) is the one point the protocol itself names for nurse-led point massage in this condition, there for dizziness that accompanies the neck pain; the catalogue lists headache, dizziness and a stiff painful neck among its classical uses. Jianjing (GB21) is recorded for painful obstruction of the shoulder and back and for a stiff neck. Quchi (LI11) is recorded for painful obstruction of the arm, and the protocol includes it among the points warmed with moxa when the arm goes numb.

The set mirrors a habit the tradition is known for: work near and far. Fengchi and jianjing lie right where desk strain gathers, at the base of the skull and the top of the shoulder, while quchi works from further along the arm. The catalogue also records tianzhu at the nape and houxi on the side of the hand for a stiff neck; this page leaves them out because the sources opened for it did not name them for this complaint. In the site’s usual wording, these points were traditionally used for neck and shoulder discomfort, which is a report of historical practice, not a claim of proven results.

三

Finding the Three Points

Fengchi (GB20), “wind pool”, sits in the two hollows where the skull meets the neck, just outside the thick strap muscles that run down beside the spine. Tilt the head slightly forward, slide the thumbs up the back of the neck until they meet the bony ridge of the skull, then let them drift outwards into the soft depressions either side. The standard description places it below the occipital bone, between the upper ends of the two large neck muscles.

Jianjing (GB21), “shoulder well”, sits on the top of the shoulder, halfway along a line from the prominent bone at the base of the neck to the outer tip of the shoulder. Reach the opposite hand across the chest and over the shoulder, and the middle finger usually falls on the crest of the muscle there, often the tenderest spot after a long day. It carries the page’s pregnancy caution: tradition avoids it during pregnancy.

Quchi (LI11), “pool at the bend”, sits at the outer end of the elbow crease. Bend the elbow to a right angle, palm facing the chest, and find the end of the crease on the thumb side; the point lies just beyond it, halfway towards the bony bump on the outside of the elbow. It is easy to find and easy to reach while seated.

四

A Gentle Routine for a Desk Break

Do this inside a break, not instead of one. Stand up first, roll the shoulders a few times, turn the head slowly left and right, and look at something far away. Then sit back down with the feet flat, the back supported and the forearms resting, and take a few slower breaths before touching anything.

Start at the nape: thumbs into the fengchi (GB20) hollows, pressure steady and angled gently inward and upward, never digging, for half a minute to a minute. Move to jianjing (GB21), one side at a time, with the middle finger pad or a gentle squeeze between fingers and thumb, about half a minute a side; leave it out entirely if you are pregnant. Finish at quchi (LI11) on each arm, a thumb pad at comfortable pressure for half a minute to a minute, then release gradually. Once or twice a working day is plenty.

The stop rules matter more than the order. Stop at once on sharp, electric or radiating sensation, and stop if pressing at the neck makes you dizzy. Skip broken, inflamed or numb skin. If the elbow itself is sore, leave quchi out; this routine is not meant for elbow pain. Pregnancy, a known neck condition, or medicine that affects bleeding are all reasons to ask a qualified practitioner before starting.

五

What the Evidence Says

The Chinese authority behind this page is the China Association of Chinese Medicine’s 2026 set of nursing protocols, whose section on xiangbi covers cervical spondylosis with nerve-root symptoms. It is a nursing standard, not a trial: it lists point massage at fengchi for dizziness among the nurse-led techniques, done on a doctor’s order, and gives the desk-work advice quoted above. A clinical acupuncture guideline for the same condition exists, but this page could not open its content and does not rely on it.

Two small trials pressed points by hand in women with long-standing neck pain. One compared pressure on shoulder points, jianjing among them, or on arm points, quchi among them, with no treatment, and both groups reported less pain and disability. The other gave a month of acupressure and found less pain and more neck movement than in untreated women. Neither used sham pressing. A large trial in China needled points and found more improvement than with sham needling, by a margin below what patients would notice as clearly better.

A 2024 Cochrane review found little or no difference between massage for neck pain and placebo. A 2018 Cochrane review of office ergonomics found that a forearm support combined with an alternative mouse may help prevent neck and shoulder problems, and that extra breaks may also help. On this page, breaks and set-up keep the first place, and hand pressure stays an optional comfort measure.

六

When Neck Pain Needs More Than Self-Care

Some signs mean the neck needs medical assessment before any self-care. Seek prompt help for numbness, tingling or weakness spreading into an arm or hand; hands that turn clumsy, or walking that becomes unsteady; any change in bladder or bowel control; fever or unexplained weight loss alongside the pain; pain that never lets up at night; neck pain after a fall, a car accident or any blow; and dizziness or blackouts, especially when you turn or tip the head.

Beyond those warnings, neck and shoulder pain that lasts more than a few weeks, keeps returning, or stops you working deserves a proper assessment and a plan, often with a physiotherapist who can tailor the exercise. Registered acupuncturists and traditional medicine clinicians can join that conversation alongside conventional care rather than instead of it. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

七

Who This Page Can and Cannot Help

This page is written for a healthy adult with ordinary neck and shoulder stiffness from screen work, who has already started taking breaks and fixing the desk and wants a small, calm ritual to go with them. For that reader it offers a clear map of three points, firm boundaries, and an honest sense of what the tradition claimed and what research has and has not found.

It is not for wrist or elbow pain. The 2024 carpal tunnel guideline of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons found acupressure no better than placebo or control for carpal tunnel syndrome, and this page offers no points for elbow pain such as tennis elbow. Numbness or weakness in the hand needs a medical review. If your stiff neck tends to bring on a band-like headache, the site’s tension headache page covers that next step, and the catalogue pages for GB20, GB21 and LI11 hold the full location details.

停

Self-care limits

Educational content only — self-acupressure means fingers and palms, never needles. Keep every touch comfortable rather than firm, avoid broken or irritated skin, and stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation, or if pressing brings on dizziness. Skip jianjing (GB21) entirely during pregnancy. Numbness or weakness in the arm or hand goes to medical review first. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問

Frequently asked

Can pressing points stand in for breaks and a better desk?
No. The order on this page is deliberate: move the neck every hour or two, support the forearms, raise the screen, and keep up shoulder and neck exercise first. A few minutes of pressing can sit inside that break as a comfort ritual, but it does not undo eight hours of a bowed head.
Is jianjing (GB21) safe during pregnancy?
Tradition records jianjing among the points to avoid during pregnancy, and this site follows that caution: leave it out entirely while pregnant. Fengchi (GB20) and quchi (LI11) carry no such traditional bar, but pregnancy is still a good reason to talk with a qualified practitioner before starting any self-pressure routine.
Will these points help a sore wrist or a “mouse elbow”?
The 2024 carpal tunnel guideline of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons found acupressure no better than placebo or control for wrist pain from carpal tunnel syndrome, and this page offers no points for elbow pain such as tennis elbow. Numbness, tingling or weakness in the hand needs a medical review, not more pressing.
How often can I do the routine?
Once or twice a working day is plenty, ideally folded into a movement break rather than done hunched at the keyboard. Half a minute to a minute at each point, pressure kept comfortable, releases gradual. More frequent or firmer work is not better; soreness the next day means the pressure was too firm.
Why press an elbow point for a stiff neck?
Because the classical system worked near and far at once. Quchi (LI11) sits on the large intestine channel, which old charts ran from the hand up the arm to the shoulder and neck, and its recorded indications include painful obstruction of the arm. Pressing it here is a distant station for the neck and shoulder, not a treatment for the elbow itself.
My stiff neck often brings on a headache. Is there more on that?
Yes. A tight neck and a band-like headache often travel together after long screen days, and this site has a separate page on tension headache with its own small routine. Fengchi (GB20) appears on both pages; there is no need to press it twice in one sitting.
