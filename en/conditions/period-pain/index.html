Period pain

Cyclical period pain is among the oldest complaints traditional East Asian medicine wrote about, and its recorded answers lean on a handful of inner-leg and foot points. This page gathers what classical practice reached for, a gentle home routine built around Sanyinjiao (SP6) and Taichong (LV3), and the boundaries that keep self-care honest.

急

Get medical help now if

  • the pain is suddenly much worse than usual
  • it comes with fever or vomiting, or stays fixed to one side
  • there is significant belly pain at any chance of pregnancy
  • bleeding is unusually heavy or long, or you feel dizzy or faint

一

How Period Pain Usually Shows Up

Most people who have it know its signature better than any textbook could describe it: a dull, dragging weight, or a rhythmic gripping low in the belly during the first day or two of a bleed. For some the ache climbs into the lower back; for others it reaches down the inner thighs. As the flow settles, the discomfort usually ebbs with it. The picture shifts from person to person and from month to month — the same body can hand over a mild cycle one month and a demanding one the next.

This page speaks to discomfort that follows a familiar monthly rhythm in an adult who knows their own pattern. It is everyday-comfort territory, not diagnosis: pain that is brand new, sharply different in character, fixed to one side, or far heavier than usual steps outside self-care altogether, and the closing section of this page explains where that boundary sits. Nothing here replaces a conversation with a clinician about your own cycle.

二

What Traditional Practice Reached For

Classical writers read menstrual discomfort through images of movement. Qi and blood were pictured travelling their courses freely; when flow turned sluggish or constrained, the lower abdomen was where the stillness told — sometimes helped along, in the old framing, by cold gathering near the womb. The recorded responses follow from that picture: warmth, rest, and points along the channels that sweep past the lower abdomen and climb the inner leg, chosen to encourage what tradition calls the free movement of qi and blood.

In the licensed dataset behind this site, two names recur around this concern, and both anchor this page: Sanyinjiao (SP6) on the inner calf, where the spleen, liver, and kidney channels are said to meet, and Taichong (LV3) on the top of the foot, the liver channel’s source point. Alongside them sits local care for the lower abdomen itself — warmth and a gentle palm rather than any deep working. These pairings report what historical practice reached for; they are not statements about outcomes, and the point pages behind them carry the precise records.

三

Sanyinjiao (SP6): Where Three Yin Channels Meet

Locating the point starts at the ankle. On the inner side of the lower leg, measure up from the tip of the inner ankle bone about three cun — which, helpfully, is roughly the width of your own four fingers held side by side. Slip around the back edge of the shinbone and you arrive at a softly tender hollow: Sanyinjiao, the Three Yin Intersection. The name is a map. Classical teaching places the crossing of the spleen, liver, and kidney channels here, three yin lines climbing the inner leg, and the record shows the point reached for across an unusually wide range of women’s-cycle concerns.

One warning travels with this point wherever it appears, and this page repeats it without softening: pregnancy rules Sanyinjiao out entirely. Anyone who is pregnant leaves it alone — not lighter pressure, not shorter sessions; the point simply goes unused until after the birth. If you are trying to conceive, or pregnancy is possible, ask a qualified practitioner before adding it to anything. Away from pregnancy, expect a distinct achiness when it is pressed; ease in gradually with a finger pad rather than digging, and let the tenderness set the pace.

四

Taichong (LV3): Source Point of the Liver Channel

Turn to the top of the foot. Between the first and second metatarsal bones — the long ridges running up from the big toe and its neighbour — lies a valley, and about two finger-widths back from the web between those toes the fingertip meets the junction where the bones part: Taichong. Classical theory crowns it the liver channel’s yuan-source point, a spot said to open onto the channel’s own vitality. The liver, in the old writings, governs the smooth flow of qi and the evenness of temper, which is why the record pairs this point with menstrual discomfort, stress, irritability, and tension headaches all at once.

As terrain for self-care, the top of the foot is forgiving: soft ground, no delicate structures nearby, happy to tolerate a patient hold. Its pregnancy note is milder than Sanyinjiao’s but worth reading in full — the record deems brief, gentle contact generally acceptable, yet calls it a strongly moving point. Anyone pregnant does best with feather-light, momentary touch at most, and clears anything longer with a qualified practitioner first.

五

A Gentle Routine with Clear Stop Rules

Choose a quiet evening, a warm room, and hands that are not cold. Begin with the lower abdomen: rest a warm palm flat over it, breathe slowly, and trace unhurried circles for a few minutes — a hot water bottle tucked against the belly belongs to the same old-fashioned family of comforts. Then move to the points. Settle a finger pad on Sanyinjiao and let the pressure grow to distinct-but-comfortable; hold through several slow breaths, roughly half a minute to a minute per leg. Give Taichong the same unhurried treatment on each foot. On uncomfortable days, once or twice a day is plenty — home practice rewards steadiness and gentleness, never enthusiasm.

The stop rules are short and absolute. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation ends the session for the day, wherever it appears. Pressing that sharpens cramps rather than soothing them sends the leg points back to the shelf; keep only the belly’s warmth if that still feels kind. Broken, irritated, or infected skin closes a point until it heals. Feverish illness shelves the whole routine. And pregnancy changes everything: Sanyinjiao stays unused entirely, and any new point practice waits for a qualified practitioner’s go-ahead. Discomfort that lingers after a session, or anything that worries you, belongs with professional assessment rather than a firmer thumb.

六

Warmth, Rhythm, and Honest Expectations

Traditional seasonal advice for the days of flow was homely: keep the lower abdomen warm, rest while the flow is heaviest, favour warm food and drink, and let the whole month carry steadier sleep and gentle movement so the period does not arrive onto exhaustion. Modern daily life translates the same instincts — heat packs, easy walks, quieter evenings — and many people find them comforting regardless of any theory behind them. None of it amounts to a prescription, and none of it promises prevention.

Honesty about effect matters more here than enthusiasm. Reviews suggest pressing sanyinjiao can ease period pain for a few hours, while a Cochrane review could not say whether acupressure works. What survives scrutiny regardless is smaller and real: a few minutes of deliberate warmth, slowed breathing, and undivided attention are worth having on a hard day, whoever you are. This page offers them in that spirit — companions to a difficult couple of days, never remedies.

七

Clear Boundaries: Where Self-Care Stops

Some signals outrank any routine. Pain suddenly much worse than your usual; pain with fever or vomiting; pain fixed stubbornly to one side; significant abdominal pain at any chance of pregnancy; bleeding that is unusually heavy, unusually prolonged, or arrives between periods; dizziness or fainting — each of these belongs promptly with a doctor, not with a longer pressing session. The same is true of pain that has stopped following the old familiar script. Urgency is information; give it a professional reader.

Two further groups should walk past this page towards something better. First, anyone whose period pain interrupts school, work, or ordinary life month after month: recurring pain that disruptive deserves a clinician’s search for underlying causes, many of which can be treated once found. Second, anyone hoping this page can diagnose: it cannot. What it offers is cultural context, two well-recorded points, and a calm routine — company for familiar, manageable days. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

停

Self-care limits

Educational content only — self-acupressure means fingers on skin, never needles. Keep pressure light and comfortable, skip broken or irritated skin, and stop immediately on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. Do not press Sanyinjiao (SP6) at all during pregnancy; if you could be pregnant, ask a qualified practitioner before any new routine. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問

Frequently asked

Is it safe to press these points during my period?
For healthy adults who are not pregnant, gentle surface pressure is generally reasonable at any point in the cycle. Keep the pressure comfortably below painful, remember that heavy days can leave everything feeling rawer, and let a session shrink to a few quiet minutes if that suits the day better. All the standing stop rules apply: nothing sharp, nothing over broken skin, and an immediate halt on electric or radiating sensation.
How often can I work on Sanyinjiao and Taichong?
Once or twice a day, holding each point for roughly half a minute to a minute, covers everything home practice needs. More frequent or firmer work adds soreness, not benefit. If a point feels unusually tender one day, shorten the hold or skip it; the points will still be there next month.
Why is Sanyinjiao ruled out completely during pregnancy?
Tradition describes it as a strongly downward-moving point, and older practice reached for it in contexts around childbirth itself. The licensed record accordingly carries a categorical pregnancy warning, and home practice has no way to calibrate a safe amount against it. The responsible reading is simple: anyone pregnant leaves the point alone entirely, and anyone who could be pregnant asks a qualified practitioner first.
Where exactly are the two points found?
Sanyinjiao (SP6) sits on the inner calf, about three cun — a four-finger width — above the tip of the inner ankle bone, just behind the shinbone’s back edge. Taichong (LV3) sits on the top of the foot, about two finger-widths back from the web between the big toe and second toe, in the valley between the first two metatarsal bones. The catalogue pages for SP6 and LV3 add plates and step-by-step locating notes.
Will pressing these points take my cramps away?
No honest answer fits inside a promise, and this page makes none. Some people find a session calming and are glad of the warmth and stillness; others notice little. Effects vary from person to person and cycle to cycle, and pressure is comfort rather than therapy. Cramps that are severe, worsening, or different from your norm call for assessment, whatever the calendar says.
My period pain disrupts life every single month — is this page enough for me?
It is not, and it does not pretend to be. Recurring pain that regularly interrupts study, work, or sleep deserves medical investigation for underlying causes, which are often treatable once identified. Think of self-care here as a companion to that process — something kind to do on difficult evenings — never as its substitute. Bring the pattern you have noticed to the appointment; it is useful information.
