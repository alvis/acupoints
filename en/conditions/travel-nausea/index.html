Travel nausea

Travel nausea — the queasy turn a winding road or a slow ferry can take — has a famous classical answer at neiguan (PC6) on the inner forearm, with zusanli (ST36) and sanyinjiao (SP6) close behind in tradition’s toolkit. This page covers how the complaint shows up, what practice reached for, and a gentle routine with clear limits.

證 · Where this comes from

Chinese sources name Neiguan, Zusanli and Sanyinjiao for nausea and vomiting, not travel sickness itself. Lab trials of wristbands and Neiguan pressure differ: some found less nausea, others no protection.

Clinical research

  1. Randomised trial · Hu S et al. · 1995

    P6 acupressure reduces symptoms of vection-induced motion sickness (Aviation, Space, and Environmental Medicine, vol. 66)

    Sixty-four people sat in a rotating striped drum. Those given Neiguan pressure reported less nausea than those given dummy-point, sham or no pressure, and showed less abnormal stomach rhythm.

一

How Travel Nausea Shows Up

Seasickness has cousins everywhere: the back seat on a winding coast road, the lower deck of a slow ferry, a small aircraft dipping through warm air, even a crowded bus lurching through evening traffic. The body usually announces itself early — a stomach that drifts uneasily, skin turning cool and clammy, a sheen of sweat, more saliva than usual, perhaps yawns or a dull headache settling in behind the eyes. A common explanation sketches the scene in rough outline: the eyes, the inner ears, and the muscles each report a different story about movement, and the disagreement registers as queasiness until the journey ends. Whatever the finer mechanics, the experience is old, ordinary, and human — classical writers knew it, and so does anyone who has ever regretted bringing a paperback onto a coach.

It behaves differently from person to person. One traveller crosses oceans unbothered while another feels unsteady stepping onto a riverboat; the same person may read comfortably on a train yet go pale in a car. Anticipation joins in too — for some people the queasiness begins at the ticket gate, before any wheel turns. None of this amounts to a diagnosis, and this page offers none: the descriptions here simply sketch shared experience so you can recognise your own version of it, and the suggestions that follow are comfort measures for ordinary journeys, not remedies for anything a doctor should be seeing.

二

What Traditional Practice Reached For

Traditional practice reached first for neiguan (PC6) on the pericardium channel, and it remains the name most travellers half-know — the point behind the motion-sickness wristbands displayed at pharmacy counters. Its two characters picture an “inner pass”: guan evokes a gate across a route, and tradition set this one on the inner forearm, two cun above the wrist crease, treating it as a doorway where the chest’s affairs could be quietly addressed. Classical filing gives it two titles at once — luo-connecting point of its channel, and one of the eight confluent points, traditionally paired with the yin wei vessel that travels the front of the body. Recorded indications gather around the middle of the trunk: nausea, vomiting, hiccups, and stomachache sit beside palpitations, and generations of case-notes reach for the spot whenever travel or pregnancy stirred the stomach.

Alongside it stand two leg points from the condition’s own territory. Zusanli (ST36), a hand’s breadth or so below the kneecap toward the outer shin, carries the stomach channel’s he-sea title and a long standing reputation as a command point of digestion and general vitality; folk memory hangs a charming saying on its name — san li means “three li”, the old distance measure, as if pressing the point lent strength for three more li of walking. Sanyinjiao (SP6), on the inner leg above the anklebone where three yin channels were said to meet, belongs to the same digestive register. One caution belongs in the same breath: the dataset records SP6 as contraindicated in pregnancy, and this page keeps that boundary without exception.

三

A Gentle Routine for the Road

The journey-ready routine centres on neiguan. Turn one palm upward and lay three fingers of the opposite hand across the wrist, fingertips resting at the crease; just beyond them, in the soft channel between the two tendons that rise when you curl your fingers, sits the point. Settle a fingertip there, press only as firmly as feels pleasant, and stay for roughly a minute while the breathing slows — several easy breaths are enough to judge the pressure. Swap wrists and repeat, and during a journey return to it every few minutes as needed. Many travellers find it worth beginning before departure, holding the point calmly in the queue rather than waiting for the first bend to announce itself.

At home, or on a long stopover with room to sit, the two leg points join in. For zusanli (ST36), cup the kneecap in the palm of one hand so the fingertips rest just below it, then slide one finger-width toward the outside of the shin and press into the slight hollow there. For sanyinjiao (SP6), stack four fingers above the tip of the inner anklebone and settle a thumb just behind the shin bone’s back edge — the spot is often tender, so arrive gradually rather than digging. The stop rules outrank every technique: leave broken, inflamed, or numb skin alone; release at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation; and during pregnancy keep to gentle pressure at neiguan only, leaving sanyinjiao out altogether. Anyone unsure where they stand asks a qualified practitioner first.

四

Habits That Help Before You Travel

Habits around the journey itself carry quiet weight, and most of them cost nothing. A forward-facing seat with the horizon in view steadies many travellers; fresh air through a cracked window, regular pauses to stand and walk, and water taken in small sips all earn their keep on long days. Before setting out, a light simple meal tends to sit better than a heavy or greasy one, and strong smells — diesel, perfume, packed lunches gone warm in the sun — repay a wide berth. Reading or watching a screen while the vehicle moves asks for trouble in a queasy-prone passenger; looking down the road does the opposite. None of this is prescription, merely accumulated travel sense that traditional wellness writing happily endorses.

Hold all of it modestly. Seat choice and fresh air are ordinary sense; finger pressure is a comfort practice wrapped in centuries of tradition; neither replaces your own judgement about your body. For travel sickness itself, small laboratory trials of wristbands and neiguan pressure have disagreed with one another. That candour is not discouragement — it is permission to try the routine on a short local journey first, notice what actually happens, and keep whatever proves pleasant without promising yourself relief.

五

When Nausea Belongs With a Professional

Certain signs move nausea out of self-care’s jurisdiction entirely, whatever the journey was meant to be. Vomiting that will not settle, or that keeps fluids from staying down, belongs with a doctor promptly; so does vomit containing blood, severe abdominal pain, fever riding alongside, dizziness strong enough to affect standing, or sickness following a knock to the head. Chest pain always speaks for itself. During pregnancy, vomiting that goes well beyond ordinary morning queasiness deserves professional attention rather than patient endurance, however cheerfully the folklore insists otherwise.

Recurring or long-standing nausea tells a similar story: it needs assessment first, and self-applied pressure can accompany good care but never substitute for it. The same holds for children and older relatives whose sickness keeps returning — patterns that repeat are information, and information belongs with someone trained to read it. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

六

Who This Page Can Help

This page serves several kinds of reader well: the traveller assembling a calm pre-departure ritual; the curious bystander wondering what those pharmacy wristbands actually press; and learners meeting the forearm’s tendon corridor for the first time. Everything asserted here is either recorded in the catalogue — the locations, recorded indications, and cautions for neiguan (PC6), zusanli (ST36), and sanyinjiao (SP6) — or attributed to general classical framing, and the distinction is kept visible throughout.

What the page cannot do is equally plain: it cannot assess anyone, treat anything, or replace the clinicians who manage recurring digestive trouble, balance disorders, or pregnancy complications. Licensed practitioners, including registered acupuncturists and East Asian medicine clinicians, do their best work alongside conventional care, coordinating with your regular doctors rather than substituting for them. When you want the fine print, the catalogue pages for PC6, ST36, and SP6 hold the exact locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions that this overview deliberately leaves to them.

停

Self-care limits

Educational content only — acupressure here means fingers and palms, never needles or devices. Keep pressure light and comfortable. Stay on intact skin, and skip any spot that is broken, inflamed, or numb. Leave sanyinjiao (SP6) out entirely during pregnancy. Stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問

Frequently asked

Does pressing neiguan (PC6) really help on a bumpy journey?
An honest answer refuses the podium. The point’s modern fame rests on decades of wristbands sold for exactly this purpose, and many travellers would hesitate to board a ferry without one; whether any given ride stays comfortable varies with the person, the weather, and the road. Try the routine gently on a short local trip first, and let your own experience — not a promise made anywhere, least of all here — decide how much weight it carries.
Where exactly do I find neiguan (PC6)?
Turn the palm up and place three fingers of the opposite hand across the wrist, fingertips touching the crease. Neiguan waits just beyond them, in the valley between the two tendons that stand up along the middle of the forearm. Settle a fingertip into that valley and test lightly; a comfortable, slowly spreading heaviness suggests you are in the right place.
Are these points safe to use during pregnancy?
Tradition treats neiguan (PC6) kindly here — the dataset records it as commonly used around morning queasiness, pressed gently rather than deeply. Sanyinjiao (SP6) sits firmly off-limits for the duration, being contraindicated in pregnancy. Zusanli (ST36) is recorded as acceptable. Any routine built during pregnancy still deserves a word with a qualified practitioner first.
Why does sanyinjiao (SP6) carry a pregnancy warning?
Classical filing treats it as a strongly downward-moving point, and history put that quality to work supporting labour. Pressing it during pregnancy was therefore judged too stimulating, and the dataset preserves the contraindication unchanged. The warning costs a traveller very little: the routine stands complete on the forearm alone, and the leg can rejoin it another time.
How long should each hold last?
About a minute is a fair measure — several slow breaths at comfortable pressure, then a swap to the other side. During a journey you can return to the point every few minutes as needed; at home, gentler and less often beats harder and constantly. Traditional teaching prizes consistency over force, and soreness afterwards is a signal to lighten everything next time.
Wristband or fingertip — does it matter which I choose?
Both address the same patch of forearm, so the choice is mostly practical. A band supplies steady, hands-free pressure and forgets itself nicely on a long drive; a fingertip adjusts instantly, goes wherever you go, and tunes the pressure second by second. Whichever you pick, the old boundaries still govern: gentle pressure, intact skin, and a firm stop on anything sharp or radiating.
