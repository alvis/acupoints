一

Where Yishe Stands on the Back

Yishe takes its place along the lower stretch of the rib-bearing back, where the spine is preparing to meet the waist. The dataset locates it precisely: on the back, below the spinous process of the eleventh thoracic vertebra, 3 cun lateral to the posterior midline. Unpacked for hands rather than rulers: begin at the base of the neck, where the prominent knob at the junction of neck and shoulder marks the seventh cervical vertebra, and let the twelve ridges of the thoracic spine be counted patiently below it. Beneath the eleventh ridge lies a soft step, and from that step the search moves outward three cun — well away from the central seam — onto the broad column of muscle that runs parallel to the spine. There, mirrored on both sides, sits Yishe.

A cun is the traditional measuring unit of Chinese point-location: proportional lengths taken from the person being examined rather than from a ruler, so a cun at the waist of a broad-framed adult differs from a cun on a slight teenager. For a distance of three cun, the rendering passed along through self-location teaching is the width of all four fingers pressed together — helpful arithmetic, worth trusting only alongside the bony landmarks. The terrain here is friendly: the spot rests on the long muscles that flank the spine, usually well padded, and the deeper structures of the trunk lie far beyond anything gentle fingertip contact could ever reach. The tips of the lowest ribs may announce themselves farther out toward the flank; the address itself belongs to the muscle column, and knowing where that boundary lies is part of reading the ground well.

The neighborhood completes the map. One ridge higher, on the same outer road, sits Yanggang (BL48) beneath the tenth thoracic vertebra; one ridge lower stands Weicang (BL50), the stomach’s station, beneath the twelfth. Directly inward, half the distance from the midline, waits Pishu (BL20), the spleen’s own transport point — inner office and outer lodging sharing one vertebral level. Classical teaching drew the bladder meridian as the longest pathway in the body, sixty-seven stations strong, beginning at the inner corner of the eye, sweeping over the head, and descending the whole back to the little toe. Down the spine it travels in paired vertical routes, an inner road one and a half cun from the midline carrying the transports of the organs, and this outer road three cun out carrying their companions — the road Yishe stands on.

二

A Self-Palpation Walkthrough

Sit upright on a firm chair, feet flat, shoulders heavy, because hunching reshapes the terrain about to be read. Bow the head slightly and find the great knob at the base of the neck, then sort the landmarks with the movement test: as you nod slowly, the ridges at and above the knob travel under your fingertip, while the first ridge beneath it stays quietly anchored — that anchored ridge is the first thoracic, and it opens the count. Now comes the long part: walk straight down the centre seam and count ridges, patiently, to eleven. Many people find it easier to count in quiet groups — three, six, nine, then two more — and worth counting twice, because a long tally invites drift. A familiar aid from self-location teaching places the lower tip of the shoulder blade near the seventh ridge when the arms hang relaxed, offering a mid-course check. Rest the pad of a finger in the soft step beneath the eleventh ridge, slide outward across the muscle until you have crossed roughly the width of four pressed-together fingers, and settle into the shallow hollow of the muscle column. Repeat the whole search on the other side.

Confirmations cost nothing. Touch feather-light and compare sides: many people register a faint, dull awareness at the genuine spot and little sensation a thumb-width away. Check the horizontal position too: the true spot keeps respectful distance from the spine, out on the muscle column, yet nowhere near the soft flank — three cun lands the search squarely on the cord of muscle, not on bone and not on the looser tissue farther out. Lean on the neighbors as witnesses: whoever has found Yanggang one ridge above or Weicang one ridge below can trust Yishe to sit exactly between them, and whoever has located Pishu on the inner road can step outward from the same level twice and arrive by a second route. If bare bone meets the fingertip, the search has wandered onto a rib edge or the spine itself; ease back onto the muscle column and begin again.

Four slips account for nearly every mislocation. Misjudging the starting knob comes first — if the landmark taken for the seventh cervical is really the first thoracic, every subsequent count lands one ridge low, which is why the movement test earns its keep. Losing the place during the long count comes second; slowness and a second pass mend it better than confidence ever will. Drifting outward past the muscle column into the soft flank comes third; the correction never changes — return toward the spine until the searching finger rides the muscle again. Forgetting the mirror pair is fourth — there are two Yishe, and neither substitutes for the other. When doubt outlives every test, resist guessing from wherever the wandering ended; go back to the base of the neck, run the movement test once more, and recount slowly from the top.

三

Reading the Name: House of Thought

The name is a small building with one quiet resident. Yi 意 means thought, intention, mindful attention — the silent mental work of turning something over. Written out, the character sets yin 音, sound, over xin 心, heart, a composition readers have long found poetic: thought as sound arising within the heart. She 舍 means house, lodging, inn — the same character that lives inside the modern word for dormitory, sushe 宿舍. Together the name reads as the house of thought: the place where the mind’s quiet labor lodges on the back. Classical teaching held that each organ shelters one aspect of the inner life, and it assigned yi — thought and intention — to the spleen, whose work of transformation was imagined to extend to the slow digesting of experience itself.

Yishe does not lodge alone. Classical teaching lined the bladder meridian’s outer road with addresses for the whole company: high in the back, Pohu (BL42) keeps quarters for the po, the lung’s corporeal aspect; Shentang (BL44) opens a hall for the heart’s shen, the shining spirit; Hunmen (BL47) hangs a gate for the liver’s hun, the wandering soul; at the eleventh vertebra Yishe houses the spleen’s yi; and far below, at the second lumbar vertebra, Zhishi (BL52) keeps a chamber for the kidney’s zhi, the steadfast will. Read together, they sketch a doctrine rather than an anatomy: the tradition imagining each organ’s inner face given a room along the back, level with that organ’s transport point on the inner road. Whether taken as physiology or poetry, it explains why this stretch of back mattered so much to the old writers — and why the spleen’s house stands exactly beside the spleen’s office.

Pinyin renders the name Yìshè, and both syllables share the same melodic fate: yi falls on the fourth tone, a firm downward stroke, and she carries the fourth tone as well. The fourth-tone she matters here because the character 舍 keeps two readings in the dictionary — shě on a dipping third tone when it means to give something up, and shè on the falling fourth tone when it means a dwelling — and the point-name uses the dwelling. The hanzi pair 意舍 stays legible at a glance — thought, house — and like many points on this road named for rooms and gates and halls, the metaphor performs real work. It tells a reader that the tradition treated this spot as somewhere something lives, not merely a milestone something passes.

四

Traditional Indications, Explained

The recorded indications read: fullness of the hypochondrium with vomiting, abdominal distension, borborygmus, and diarrhoea. Unpacked: the hypochondrium is the flanking region where the lower ribs curve away toward the waist, and fullness there describes a subjective swelling, oppression, or stuck quality at the sides of the trunk — recorded classically alongside rising nausea and vomiting. Abdominal distension is the belly feeling blown up and tight. Borborygmus is the formal word for gurgling — the audible churn and rumble of gas and fluid moving through the intestines. Diarrhoea needs no translation. Grouped together, the list sketches a single scene: a middle burner laboring, food and fluid lingering instead of moving on, the abdomen vocal about its discontent.

Classical practice read the scene through the spleen. In the traditional physiology the spleen governs transformation and transportation — receiving food and drink, separating the clear from the turbid, sending essence upward as qi and blood and waste downward for the bowels to finish. When that work stalled, tradition expected precisely this portfolio: distension from stagnation, rumbling from unruly movement, loose stools from unfinished transformation, and fullness spilling toward the ribs where qi could not pass freely. Because Yishe lodges the spleen’s yi beside the spleen’s transport point, classical discussions of such complaints naturally reached along this stretch of the outer road. That is the heritage’s own logic, offered to show how the old system connected one point to one organ’s duties — not a claim about what pressing anything achieves.

It bears repeating plainly: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats disease, and nothing in this guide replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Digestive complaints especially deserve that humility, because ordinary bloating and gurgling shade gradually into conditions that need real diagnosis. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition lightly, and hold the safety rules firmly.

五

Self-Care Acupressure at Home

Reaching the mid-back with one’s own hands takes a little arrangement. Seated on a firm chair, reach the opposite hand across the body and around the side; most people find the right hand settles more easily on the left-side point and the left hand on the right, with the finger pads arriving on the muscle column rather than the spine. Where reach falls short, asking a partner for light fingertip contact works far better than straining — or simply choosing another day. Begin with contact alone for a few breaths, then add small, slow circles, letting the skin glide over the muscle beneath rather than dragging across it. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or mild warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, matched where possible to slow exhales; then swap hands and give the other side the same courtesy.

Quality matters more than duration. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; hard jabbing teaches the body nothing and risks bruising a muscle that did nothing wrong. Warm hands help, because chilled muscle guards. Choose a quiet corner over a screen, since the practice doubles as a pause: breathe slowly and feel the breath widen the back under still fingers — this low station stirs far less than the chest, and noticing that stillness is part of the pleasure — then add gentle pressure on the long exhale. People who keep a routine often anchor it to an existing habit, morning or evening, because consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Treat the exercise as a small ceremony of attention rather than a treatment session. Perhaps the tight band along the spine feels less guarded afterward; perhaps the belly quiets while you rest; perhaps nothing shifts except that you spent a few unhurried minutes attending to your own back. Every one of those outcomes fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a result, especially anywhere digestion is actively unhappy. When curiosity pulls that way, return to gentleness or simply stop for the day, and let food, sleep, and time do the heavy lifting they were always going to do.

六

Cautions and Safe Practice

The licensed record for this point is quiet: the dataset lists no special caution entry alongside Yishe’s identity, and this page echoes that silence exactly. Silence, though, is not an invitation to force. An empty caution field means the compiled sources recorded nothing specific to flag — not that unlimited pressure becomes wise. Every back point shares the same plain physics: skin, then muscle, then structures best left to trained professionals, and home acupressure stays entirely within the first layer. Its neighbor Weicang, one ridge below, does carry a do-not-needle-deeply note in the same record — a reminder that the sources treat this corridor of the back with respect even when a particular entry is silent.

For fingertip practice the rules are the house rules. Keep pressure light and superficial, riding the muscle rather than driving through it; use finger pads, never elbows, knuckles, or tools. Skip the point during feverish illness, acute abdominal pain, or any digestive upset bad enough to send you to bed; avoid broken, irritated, or numbed skin, and be cautious over areas of recent injury or surgery until ordinary activity has been cleared. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once and do not try again that day. Pregnant readers should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice, and anyone managing chronic illness should treat a new self-care habit the same way — as something worth mentioning to the people who manage their care.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for persistent vomiting, blood in vomit or stool, severe or worsening abdominal pain, fever alongside digestive illness, unexplained weight loss, or any symptom that frightens you. Persistent complaints, digestive or otherwise, belong with a licensed practitioner, whether Western or East Asian medicine suits the complaint. The image of a quiet house where thought lodges is lovely, but the safest gift Yishe offers most people is ten unhurried minutes of acquaintance with their own back, treated kindly.

提要
In short

Yishe, the House of Thought, stands low on the back, three cun out from the spine beneath the eleventh thoracic vertebra. Classical teaching lodged yi — the thought-and-intention aspect of the spleen — here, and reached for this point when fullness, distension, or rumbling bowels unsettled the middle. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it gently.

限Self-care limits

Self-care here means gentle fingertip contact on the back muscles only: no tools or devices, no pressing through sharp pain, none during fever or acute abdominal upset, none over broken skin. Stop at once on sharp or radiating sensation. Pregnant readers should consult a qualified practitioner first, and persistent digestive symptoms belong with a licensed clinician — this page is educational only. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I find Yishe without memorising a count of eleven vertebrae?
答Lean on shortcuts and witnesses. The lower tip of the shoulder blade, when the arms hang relaxed, sits near the seventh thoracic ridge in most people — four ridges more brings the search to eleven. Or find Pishu (BL20), whose level matches the widely taught eleventh-vertebra station, then step outward the width of four pressed-together fingers. Neighbors help too: Yanggang one ridge above and Weicang one ridge below bracket the address. Whatever route you take, confirm the result against the bony landmarks rather than trusting wherever your hand happened to land.
問Is pressing Yishe myself reasonable when the record lists no caution?
答Gentle fingertip pressure on the back muscles is generally reasonable self-care for healthy adults, and the absence of a recorded caution reflects what the compiled sources flagged, not an assurance of any kind. The practical protection is depth discipline: stay in skin and superficial muscle, use pads rather than tools, and treat sharpness, radiating sensation, or worsening discomfort as immediate stop signals. Anyone pregnant, or living with significant illness, should clear new point practices with a qualified practitioner first — silence in a dataset never overrides personal medical advice.
問What did classical practice use Yishe for?
答The traditional record lists fullness of the hypochondrium with vomiting, abdominal distension, borborygmus, and diarrhoea. Classical texts describe it as the lodging of yi, the thought-and-intention aspect assigned to the spleen, standing on the outer road beside the spleen’s transport point — which is why digestive complaints dominate its historic portfolio. Those are traditional frameworks for understanding the body, documented for context — not evidence-backed treatment claims.
問Why does a spleen-related point stand three cun out instead of beside the spine?
答Classical teaching ran two roads. The inner road, one and a half cun from the midline, carries the back transports where each organ reports directly; the outer road, three cun out, carries companion stations the tradition associated with each organ’s inner life — doors and halls for po, shen, hun, yi, and zhi. Yishe is the spleen’s room on the outer road, level with Pishu, the spleen’s office on the inner road. Front-and-back pairing gets much of the attention, but the outer line preserved a second, more poetic geography of the organs.
問When should digestive trouble go to a doctor rather than a self-care routine?
答Promptly, whenever red flags appear: persistent vomiting, blood in vomit or stool, severe or worsening abdominal pain, fever alongside digestive illness, difficulty swallowing, or unexplained weight loss. And sooner rather than later whenever symptoms persist beyond a couple of weeks, recur often, or simply worry you. Self-acupressure is a comfort practice, not a diagnostic tool; it cannot tell ordinary indigestion from something surgical, and it was never meant to try. Daily self-care lives downstairs; clinical assessment keeps the consulting room upstairs.
