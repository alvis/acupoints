飛揚 Feiyang

“Soaring Flight” · BL58

For. Headache; low back and leg ache; weak legs.

一

Where Feiyang Sits on the Calf

The catalogue places Feiyang on the back of the lower leg, seven cun directly above Kunlun (BL60) — the hollow behind the lateral malleolus, the bony prominence on the outer side of the ankle — and one cun lateral and inferior to Chengshan (BL57), which holds the middle of the calf. A cun is the traditional measuring unit of Chinese point-location: proportional lengths taken from the person being examined rather than from a ruler, with the width of one’s own thumb serving as the classic rough equivalent. Seven cun above the ankle hollow therefore means seven of your own thumb-widths, climbing straight up the back of the leg, and “lateral and inferior” to Chengshan means one thumb-width below the calf’s midpoint, displaced toward the outer edge of the leg.

What lies under the fingertips is forgiving territory compared with many point locations. The upper calf is thick with muscle — the twin heads of the gastrocnemius that give the calf its curve — and this spot sits where that muscle begins narrowing toward the Achilles tendon, on the muscle’s outer flank rather than at its centre. No ribs, no lungs, no abdominal organs come anywhere near; the structures that matter most for self-care are simply muscle, tendon, and the skin over both. Small surface veins thread the region in some people, which is one more reason contact should stay light enough that the fingertips read texture rather than compress tissue.

A mental map of three neighbors makes the position unmistakable. At the crease behind the knee sits Weizhong (BL40), the channel’s gathering place at the popliteal fold. Midway down the calf, at the pointed depression that forms just below the belly of the gastrocnemius when the leg is extended or the heel lifted, lies Chengshan. And at the bottom, tucked into the hollow between the tip of the outer ankle bone and the Achilles tendon, rests Kunlun. Feiyang hangs a thumb-width below Chengshan and a thumb-width toward the outer side, squarely in the channel’s corridor down the back of the leg, just nudged toward the edge.

二

Finding Feiyang by Touch

Sit sideways on a firm chair and lay the ankle of the search leg across the opposite thigh, or stand and shift your weight fully onto the other leg so the calf softens. Begin low: slide your fingers up the back of the leg from behind the outer ankle bone until they settle into the hollow nestled between the bone’s tip and the cord of the Achilles tendon — that is Kunlun, your starting mark. From there, walk your thumb-widths straight up the back of the leg, counting as you go, until you have climbed seven. You should now be at the level where the peak of the calf bulge has passed and the muscle begins gathering toward the tendon.

A second route confirms the first. Rise onto the balls of both feet, or straighten the leg and draw the foot upward, and feel the belly of the calf bunch into shape; Chengshan sits at the sharp depression that appears just beneath that bunched muscle, near the midline of the leg. From Chengshan, drop one thumb-width down the leg and slide the same distance toward the outside edge. Where the two searches agree — below and outboard of the calf’s centre, seven thumb-widths up from the ankle hollow — you have found Feiyang. Repeat the entire sequence on the other leg, because the pair deserve equal attention.

Confirm before pressing anything. Touch feather-light and compare sides: many people notice a faint, dull awareness at the true spot and little sensation a finger-width away. Slowly point and flex the foot and feel the tissue glide beneath still fingers — the transition from muscle to tendon announces itself to patient hands. The classic mistakes run in both directions: searching too high lands you on the mid-bulge, too low drops you onto the tendon itself, and drifting inward loses the slight outward bias the word “lateral” asks for. When in doubt, return to the ankle hollow and climb again, slowly.

三

Reading the Name: Soaring Flight

The name is built from two characters of plain, vivid Chinese. Fei 飛 means to fly — the character began life as a picture of a bird lifting off, wings spread against the sky. Yang 揚 means to raise, to soar, to toss upward — the verb behind winnowing grain scattered on the wind and dust lifted by a passing cart; dictionaries group raising, fluttering, and spreading among its senses. Together they read as soaring flight — to take wing and rise — and the governing image is motion: something leaving the ground.

Classical commentators have long tied that airborne image to the point’s particular office. As the bladder channel’s luo-connecting point, Feiyang marks the place where, in traditional description, a branch vessel departs from the main channel to link with the kidney channel, its paired partner. A vessel taking leave of its course looks, in the old imagery, like something taking flight, and generations of teachers have heard that departure sounding inside the name. The gloss is traditional interpretation rather than anatomy; it has survived because it teaches the relationship in a way memory enjoys.

Pinyin renders the name Fēiyáng. The first syllable, fei, carries a first tone — a flat, even, high note held steady, the level tone dictionaries describe for 飛. The second, yang, climbs from mid-range toward the top, the rising second tone of 揚. The hanzi pair 飛揚 is short enough to memorise at a glance. Where many points take their names from architecture — gates, valleys, courtyards, storehouses — this one takes its name from motion, which suits a point whose classical role concerns traffic changing course.

四

The Luo Point: Where Channels Trade Roads

Traditional channel theory gives each of the twelve primary channels one luo point apiece, placed below the elbow or the knee. At such a point, the teaching goes, a smaller branching vessel — the luo, often rendered connecting vessel — splits from the main current of qi and blood and walks over to join the channel’s partner, the one bound to it as interior answers exterior. The bladder and the kidney form exactly such a pair: two water-phase channels, one tracing the body’s long outer circuit down the back to the little toe, the other rising from the sole along the inner leg. Feiyang is where the bladder’s branch sets out.

The office carried real weight in classical practice. Because the luo point stands at a junction, texts describe it as a place where a channel’s business could be addressed from both sides — matters belonging to its own channel, and matters arising from the partnership itself, met at the single spot where the two lines shake hands. Classical practice accordingly reached for luo points when complaints seemed to straddle the paired territories, and for steady work along a channel whose course runs long and laborious, as the bladder’s famously does, sweeping from the inner corner of the eye down to the outer edge of the little toe.

It bears stating plainly that this is traditional cartography, drawn centuries before modern anatomy and offered here as cultural context rather than physiological description. Nothing visible under the skin corresponds literally to the rivers and junctions the old texts describe. Yet the framework has organised East Asian practice for a very long time, and it explains why a point on the calf keeps company in the old records with complaints of the head — a leap that otherwise looks arbitrary. Hold it as one holds a map of an older city: useful, beautiful, and drawn to a different scale than today’s streets.

五

Traditional Indications, Explained

The recorded indications read: headache, dizziness, pain of the lower back and legs, haemorrhoids, and weak legs. Framed as tradition rather than promise, these are the complaint categories classical practice brought to this station. The first three follow the channel’s own geography: a pathway that sweeps over the head and descends the length of the back to the foot was understood to connect distant places, so unease reported at either end could be approached from the middle. A calf post on that road watched traffic moving toward both the head and the lower spine.

The remaining entries ask for a little more explanation. Haemorrhoids occupy a curious niche in the old literature: several lower-limb points of the bladder channel — the neighbor Chengshan among them — appear again and again in classical discussions of that complaint, an association the record preserves even where its reasoning is not spelled out in ways modern readers would recognise. Weak legs is a traditional umbrella for heaviness, fatigue, or unsteadiness in the lower limbs, understood as the channel failing to nourish the ground it crosses. And as the luo point — the place where the channel sends a helper toward its partner — Feiyang was read as a station concerned with the strength of the very leg it lives in.

These are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats any condition, and nothing in this guide replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Recurring headaches, dizziness that unsettles standing or walking, persistent bleeding, or progressive weakness all belong with professional care first — the tradition itself always assumed examination preceded treatment, and so should we.

六

Self-Care Acupressure at Home

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle comfortably: seated on the floor with the leg extended, or in a chair with the ankle crossed over the opposite knee so the calf faces you. Wrap one hand around the lower leg from behind and rest the pads of thumb and forefinger — pads, never tips or knuckles — on the spot. Begin with simple contact for a few breaths, then add small, slow circles, letting the skin travel over the muscle beneath rather than grinding against it. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or mild warmth, never sharpness. One to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, is a common home rhythm; matching the circles to slow exhales turns the minute into a breathing pause as well.

Quality outweighs duration. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; hard digging teaches the body nothing and bruises readily in a muscle this dense. Warm your hands first if they run cold, since chilled muscle guards itself. Some people like to pair the practice with a gentle calf stretch afterwards — straightening the knee, drawing the toes softly toward the shin, breathing slowly through the hold — because this territory responds kindly to being used gently. Whatever the accompaniment, keep the two sides symmetrical, and let regularity rather than force carry the ambition.

Treat the exercise as a few minutes of attention rather than a treatment session. Across weeks you might notice the area feeling less tight after long days of standing or desk-sitting, or simply come to enjoy the pause itself; every outcome within that range fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What falls outside it is escalation — pressing harder or longer to compel a result — and any soreness that grows instead of settling. When curiosity pulls toward force, return to gentleness, or close the day’s practice altogether. This point has kept its seat in the literature for centuries; it can easily afford your patience.

七

Cautions and Safe Practice

The licensed dataset records no cautions for Feiyang, and that absence tells its own story: unlike points high on the chest or the neck, where thin layers separate skin from vulnerable organs, the calf offers a deep cushion of muscle over uncomplicated ground. Gentle surface pressure disturbs nothing fragile there. But an empty caution column is not an invitation to force — it simply describes a narrower risk profile than some neighboring points carry, and the universal rules of careful self-care still apply in full.

The practical rules are short. Skip the point over broken, irritated, or infected skin, and over varicose veins that are prominent or tender. Be cautious after any recent calf injury, cramp, or surgery, and alongside circulatory conditions, until ordinary movement feels comfortable again. Use finger pads only, keep pressure light, and never press through pain or while holding the breath. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once, and try again another day rather than the same one. Pregnant readers, and anyone managing a chronic condition, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice to their routine.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical attention — not a longer pressing session — for a sudden severe headache, dizziness with fainting or near-fainting, headache after a head injury, weakness or numbness in a limb, changes in vision or speech, bleeding that persists, or a calf that becomes swollen, red, and warm. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner, medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. Feiyang’s gift, honestly rendered, is a modest one: a familiar landmark on the body’s longest road, best visited gently.

提要
In short

Feiyang — the name reads “to soar away” — is the bladder channel’s luo-connecting station on the upper calf, where the muscle begins its long slide toward the heel. Classical practice reached for it in cases of headache, dizziness, and aching lower backs and legs. This guide covers finding it by touch and pressing it gently.

限Self-care limits

Self-care here means gentle, purely surface contact: finger pads only, no tools, nothing over broken skin or over prominent or tender veins, and a pause during any acute calf injury. Stop at once on sharp sensation. Persistent headache, dizziness, or leg symptoms call for a licensed clinician — this page is educational only. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I find Feiyang without knowing anything about cun?
答Use the outer ankle as your ruler. Find the hollow behind the lateral malleolus — the bump on the outside of the ankle — then climb straight up the back of the leg seven of your own thumb-widths. Cross-check by finding the pointed dip just below the calf’s central bulge, dropping one thumb-width, and sliding one thumb-width toward the outer edge. Where both searches land, press lightly; a faint, dull familiarity usually confirms the spot.
問Is pressing Feiyang myself actually safe?
答For healthy adults, gentle fingertip pressure on the calf is generally reasonable self-care: the area is thickly muscled and holds none of the delicate structures that make some points cautious choices. The dataset records no cautions for this entry. Lightness still matters — avoid prominent or tender veins, broken skin, and recently strained muscle, and treat sharp or radiating sensation as an immediate stop signal.
問What did classical practice use Feiyang for?
答The traditional record lists headache, dizziness, pain of the lower back and legs, haemorrhoids, and weak legs. Classical texts describe the point as the bladder channel’s luo point — the junction where a branch sets out for the kidney channel — which shaped how the old literature talked about it. These are traditional frameworks documented for context, not evidence-backed treatment claims.
問Why would a point on the calf matter for headaches?
答In traditional reasoning, the channel is the unit of relevance, not the nearest body part. The bladder channel sweeps over the head before descending the back and leg to the little toe, so classical practice treated stations anywhere along that road as connected to places far away — an approach the tradition often summarises as treating the upper from the lower. Whether modern physiology agrees is another question; the logic belongs to the tradition, and it explains the old records without turning them into mechanism.
問Can acupressure at Feiyang replace seeing a doctor about recurring headaches?
答No. Self-acupressure is a comfort practice, not a diagnostic tool. Headaches or dizziness that recur, worsen, or arrive with red flags — sudden severity, fainting, vision or speech changes, limb weakness — need prompt professional assessment. Think of the two as neighbors on the same street: daily self-care lives in one house, clinical medicine in the other, and neither answers the other’s calls.
