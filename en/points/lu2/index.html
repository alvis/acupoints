一

Reading the catalogue location

The dataset describes Yunmen in one dense sentence: on the upper outer aspect of the chest, in the depression below the lateral end of the clavicle, under the coracoid process of the scapula, 6 cun lateral to the anterior midline. Clinical descriptions compress a lot of geography into few words, so it helps to unpack the landmarks one by one. Everything named there is something you can actually feel on your own body, which is exactly why locating this point well rewards a few quiet minutes of practice.

Start with the clavicle, the collarbone you can trace from the hollow at the base of the throat outward toward each shoulder. Run a finger along its top edge until the bone simply stops — that endpoint is the lateral end the description refers to. Directly beneath it, in most bodies, lies a soft hollow rather than bone, and that hollow is the neighbourhood the catalogue is pointing to. The phrase upper outer aspect sets the general territory: not the breastbone side of the chest, but the outer, shoulder-facing quadrant, high up where chest meets shoulder.

The cun measurement deserves a word of its own. A cun is a proportional unit in traditional point location, calibrated to your own frame rather than a fixed ruler; the width of your thumb at the knuckle is the classic rough equivalent of one cun. Six cun from the anterior midline — the vertical line down the centre of the front of the trunk — lands most adults roughly two thumb-widths outside a vertical dropped from the centre of the nipple. Treat these figures as orientation aids rather than surveying instruments; the tactile landmarks carry the final word.

二

Confirming the point with your own hand

Sit upright on a firm chair and let your shoulders drop away from your ears; tension in the shoulder girdle can bury the very hollow you are looking for. Rest your right hand on your left collarbone and slide outward until the bone ends. Drop your fingertip half a thumb-width straight down and press very lightly: you should feel yielding softness rather than rib or bone. Breathe slowly while you search — the hollow often becomes easier to feel on the exhale, when the chest wall settles.

The coracoid process gives a useful cross-check. Reach into the front crease of your shoulder, just below the outer part of the clavicle, and you may find a distinct knuckle of bone lying deep to the muscle — that is the coracoid process of the scapula named in the description. Pressing squarely on it feels hard and sometimes oddly tender. Yunmen sits in the soft space above and inboard of that knob, not on the knob itself. If your finger lands on bone, ease upward and inward until the quality of the tissue changes.

One neighbour helps confirm the address. Zhongfu, LU1, the channel’s first point, sits 1 cun below Yunmen, level with the first intercostal space — the gap between the first and second ribs. In practice the two form a small vertical pair: Yunmen higher, hugging the underside of the clavicle’s outer end; Zhongfu lower and slightly nearer the midline. Many people notice a faint achiness when fingertip pressure settles onto either point, a dull, spreading sensitivity that traditional practice has long read as a sign the location is right.

三

Why cloud gate

The Chinese name 雲門 is read Yúnmén in pinyin: yún means cloud, mén means gate or door. Classical writers pictured the lung as the canopy of the body, the uppermost organ whose qi disperses downward and outward the way weather moves beneath a vast sky. From that vantage, this point earned its poetic address: it is the gate through which the qi of the lung was imagined passing from the interior toward the surface, high on the chest where breath is felt most directly.

The image works as more than decoration. A gate suggests a threshold between outside and inside, and Yunmen does occupy exactly such a borderland — the seam where chest becomes shoulder, where the channel emerges onto the body’s surface before turning down the arm. Traditional commentators also relished the contrast between the grandeur of the name and the modesty of the place: a small hollow beneath the collarbone, barely a fingerprint wide, carrying one of the most evocative names on the whole channel. Names like this served as memory aids as much as poetry, letting students fix location and function in a single image.

四

What tradition assigns to the gate

The recorded actions for this point are cough, asthma, chest pain, shoulder and back pain, and painful obstruction of the throat. Framed properly, these are traditional indications: the purposes for which generations of practitioners reached for Yunmen, not claims of established outcome. Within classical physiology, cough and wheezing breathlessness belonged to the lung’s own domain, and a point positioned at the channel’s doorway was a natural candidate for helping rebellious or constrained lung qi settle back downward into its normal rhythm.

The shoulder-and-back listing surprises many newcomers, since the point sits on the front of the chest. Traditional reasoning ran along the channel rather than the joint: pathways were treated as connected corridors, and a gate high on the chest was considered relevant to tensions tracing toward the shoulder and upper back. Chest pain appears in the record as well, always understood in classical practice alongside careful diagnosis rather than as a stand-alone remedy — and persistent chest pain today is a symptom that belongs with a physician, whatever else one does.

The final recorded action, painful obstruction of the throat, reflects the old concept of bi — obstructed, uncomfortable flow — applied to the throat region. Classical texts describe the lung channel and its associates as intimately involved with the throat, and practitioners historically grouped throat discomforts with lung-channel points. As everywhere on this layer, the honest summary is: these are the traditional assignments, preserved and transmitted, offered here as cultural and historical information about the tradition’s map of the body.

五

Gentle self-acupressure, step by step

Self-care at this point means external fingertip pressure, nothing more. Sit comfortably, warm your hands if they tend to run cold, and settle into a slower breath before you begin. Place the pads of your index and middle fingers on the hollow you located earlier. Start with contact so light you are merely resting against the skin, then let the pressure grow gradually until it reaches the level of a firm, comfortable ache — the kind of sensation that invites you to lean in, never the kind that makes you flinch.

Use small, slow circles, letting the pressure deepen gently on the exhale and soften a little on the inhale. Continue anywhere from half a minute to two minutes per side, then rest briefly and repeat on the other side, noticing whether the two hollows feel different under your fingers. Short nails make the technique far more pleasant, and a relaxed shoulder lets the pressure come from a supported arm rather than a poking fingertip. If anything sharpens into true pain, back the pressure off or stop altogether.

Rhythm beats intensity in this practice. Many people fold a minute of work at each side into a morning routine, pairing the circles with five or six unhurried breaths; others prefer it as an evening wind-down. Whatever cadence you choose, treat the session as time spent paying attention to your breathing and posture rather than as a procedure aimed at fixing anything. Consistency and gentleness are the entire art here, and the habit of slow exhalation may prove the most valuable part of the ritual.

六

Cautions worth taking seriously

This point carries one of the weightiest cautions in the catalogue, and it is echoed verbatim in the panel above: do not needle deeply, because deep insertion may injure the lung and cause a pneumothorax. The reason is anatomy. Lung tissue rises high toward the root of the neck and reaches forward behind the clavicle, so the wall between skin and lung is thinner here than intuition suggests. Passing a needle too deeply at this site can breach that wall and collapse part of the lung — a serious injury requiring urgent hospital care. Any needling, at any depth, belongs to licensed professionals alone; everything on this page stays strictly on the surface.

For self-care readers the practical translation is simple: pressure, not penetration. Stay with fingertip contact, moderate force, and the superficial tissues. Stop at once if pressing produces sharp, stabbing, or electric sensations, and do not resume if soreness lingers. Because this region overlies the lung, treat any unexplained breathlessness, chest pain, or persistent cough as a reason to see a physician promptly — those symptoms deserve diagnosis, not self-treatment. Anyone living with asthma should continue their prescribed management exactly as directed and view acupressure, at most, as a companion habit.

A word about evidence keeps this page honest. Records of traditional use capture intent and practice, not measured outcomes, and the careful phrasing used across this layer applies here too: reviews suggest that acupressure may help with certain symptom experiences in some contexts, while the overall picture remains mixed and study quality varies. Nothing here promises relief, ranks this point against alternatives, or replaces professional assessment. When in doubt — pregnancy, frail health, recent chest surgery, children, or any active respiratory illness — ask a qualified practitioner before adding new self-care habits.

七

Where the gate stands on the channel

Yunmen is the second of the lung channel’s eleven points. The channel begins at Zhongfu, LU1, on the chest close beside it, crosses Yunmen here, then flows down the front of the shoulder and along the radial — thumb — side of the arm, past the elbow and wrist, ending at Shaoshang, LU11, at the base of the thumbnail. Standing at Yunmen therefore means standing at the channel’s summit on the trunk: the moment it tips over the ridgeline of the clavicle and begins its long descent toward the hand.

In self-care terms, that geography invites a simple sequence: some people begin with a minute at Yunmen and then draw slow fingertip strokes down the inner arm toward the thumb, following the channel’s course while breathing steadily. Whether or not one attaches meaning to the pathway, the gesture doubles as a shoulder-opening stretch and a pause for slow breathing. 提要
In short

High on the chest, tucked into the hollow just beneath the outer end of the clavicle, Yunmen — Cloud Gate — is where the lung channel gathers itself before beginning its long descent toward the thumb. This guide walks through finding the point by touch, the traditions attached to it, and gentle self-care.

忌Cautions from the classical sources

  • Keep insertion shallow; a deep needle can puncture the lung and cause pneumothorax.

限Self-care limits

Educational content only. External acupressure only — never attempt needling here; the dataset warns that deep insertion may injure the lung. Stop on sharp pain, dizziness, or breathlessness. Persistent cough, chest pain, or breathing difficulty calls for a licensed clinician, not self-care. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I tell Yunmen (LU2) apart from Zhongfu (LU1) beside it?
答By height and by neighbours. Yunmen sits directly under the lateral end of the clavicle; Zhongfu lies 1 cun lower, level with the first intercostal space, and slightly nearer the midline. Feel along the collarbone to its outer end, find Yunmen in the hollow beneath it, then slide down about a thumb-width and a little inward to meet Zhongfu. With the shoulder relaxed, the pair is easiest to distinguish.
問Is it safe to press this area myself?
答Gentle external fingertip pressure on intact skin is the self-care this layer describes, and it is a very different thing from the needling risk recorded in the catalogue. Keep the force moderate, avoid any sharp or electric sensation, and stop if soreness persists. Skip self-care entirely over broken skin, rashes, recent surgical sites, or unexplained lumps, and bring persistent respiratory symptoms to a clinician.
問How long and how often should I practise?
答Traditional home guidance favours short, regular sessions: roughly half a minute to two minutes per side, once or twice a day, is a common cadence. Let comfort govern. Pressure that leaves lingering ache, bruising, or fatigue is a signal to do less. There is no virtue in marathon sessions — a minute of unhurried circles with steady breathing is already a complete practice.
問Can acupressure here treat my cough or asthma?
答Treat is the wrong verb. Tradition assigned this point to cough and wheezing conditions, and this page presents that heritage as history, not promise. Asthma in particular needs ongoing professional management and prescribed medication; acupressure is at most a calming companion habit. If breathing symptoms worsen, seek medical care rather than more pressure.
問What should the right spot feel like?
答Many people describe a dull, spreading ache or heightened sensitivity when fingertip pressure settles onto the correct hollow, though some feel little at first, and that is equally ordinary. What you should never feel is sharp, catching, or electric pain — that is a cue to lighten off or reposition. Sensitivity varies widely between people, and even between your own two sides.
