秉風 Bingfeng

“Grasping the Wind” · SI12

Hand Taiyang · 12 of 19 · crossing-point

Where. Above the ridge of the shoulder blade, straight above Tianzong (SI11), where a dip shows as the arm lifts.

For. Shoulder-blade pain, a numb aching arm, trouble raising it.

Press. Two finger pads, slow circles, one minute each side.

一

Where Bingfeng Sits on the Shoulder Blade

Begin with a mental map of the upper back. The shoulder blade is a flat triangle of bone gliding over the rear of the rib cage, and a firm bony ridge — the spine of the scapula — runs across it from the inner border toward the shoulder. That ridge divides the back of the blade into two shallow basins: the supraspinous fossa above the ridge, and the larger infraspinous fossa below it. The dataset places Bingfeng in the centre of the upper basin, directly above Tianzong (SI11), which occupies the centre of the lower basin level with the fourth thoracic vertebra. Notice how completely the record locates this point by landmarks: a named basin, a named neighbour, and a movement test — no ruler and no measured distance appear anywhere in the entry.

What lies beneath the skin there shapes every safety note that follows. The upper basin is filled by the supraspinatus, a thick strap of muscle that runs outward beneath the bony shelf of the acromion and attaches at the very top of the arm bone; it is one of the muscles that starts the arm on its journey upward. Forward of the blade, beyond the reach of honest fingertip work, stand the ribs and everything they shelter. The dataset records a single caution for this point — do not needle deeply toward the chest cavity — and the warning reads plainly off the map: from this high perch on the blade, careless deep technique could travel toward the pleura. Professional training manages that risk with exact technique; home acupressure simply never leaves the muscle surface.

Neighbours confirm the address. Tianzong sits directly below, across the ridge in the lower basin, and the tissue there feels different under the fingers because the infraspinatus beneath it is broader and flatter. Toward the spine, at the inner end of the same upper basin, lies Quyuan (SI13), which shares the fossa but keeps to its medial corner. Bingfeng itself is the twelfth of the small intestine channel’s nineteen points, a channel classical texts trace from the little finger, up the arm, to the face — so this high shoulder station belongs to a pathway whose whole territory is the neck, shoulder, and ear. When all three neighbours seem plausible, the movement test below settles the question.

二

A Self-Palpation Walkthrough

Sit upright on a firm chair and drape the searching arm over the opposite shoulder, so the hand rests naturally on the far shoulder blade. First find the ridge: sweep the fingers across the back of the blade until they meet the firm horizontal cord of the scapular spine, then follow it outward toward the shoulder, where it flattens into the shelf above the arm joint. Next, slide the fingertips just above that ridge into the soft basin on its upper side. Wander the basin until you reach its middle — roughly midway between the inner border of the blade and the outer shelf — and let the pads rest quietly there. That central floor of the upper basin, directly above Tianzong, is where the dataset locates Bingfeng.

The movement test confirms it. Slowly begin to raise the searching-side arm out to the side and slightly forward, and watch what happens under the fingertips of the other hand: a gentle hollow appears in the basin, then softens away as the arm lowers and the muscles gather again. This is the behaviour the catalogue describes — a depression that shows itself when the arm is raised — and it works because the muscles filling the fossa change their tension as the arm moves. Compare sides with feather-light touch: most people notice a faint, dull awareness at the true spot and little sensation a finger-width away. If the finger is standing on bare ridge instead, slide either up into the basin or down past the ridge and search once more.

Three slips account for nearly every missed attempt. The commonest is dropping across the ridge into the lower basin, where a satisfying hollow waits under the fingers but belongs to Tianzong rather than Bingfeng. The second is drifting too far toward the neck, arriving at the inner corner of the upper basin where Quyuan keeps house. The third is searching with the shoulder hunched up beside the ear: a lifted shoulder buries the landmarks under tension, so drop the shoulder first and let the blade lie flat against the ribs. Whenever the picture muddles, return to the ridge, trace it outward again, and walk through the steps slowly one more time.

三

Reading the Name: Grasping the Wind

The elder of the two characters, 秉, is a small piece of pictorial writing: a hand closing around a sheaf of grain stalks, a picture still half-visible in the strokes. Its inherited meaning is to hold in the hand, to grasp, to carry. The second character, 風, is wind. Read together, Bingfeng is usually rendered Grasping the Wind or Holding the Wind — a name that pictures this high shoulder station catching something that passes on the air, the way an open hand closes around stalks.

Classical commentators took the image seriously rather than filing it as decoration. The top of the shoulder stands exposed in weather and in draught, and the tradition held that wind-borne influences gathered and assailed precisely such open heights; a point stationed there was read as the place that receives and holds what the wind brings. The category record adds a structural note: Bingfeng is listed as a crossing-point, and classical literature described it as a junction where the qi of neighbouring channels converged with the small intestine channel itself. Whatever one makes of the theory, the practical moral the tradition drew stayed consistent: this was a station where several lines met, so complaints of the shoulder and limb came to be discussed through it.

Pinyin renders the name Bǐngfēng: a dipping third tone on bing, then a high, level first tone on feng. Said slowly, the name carries a lifting-then-settling rhythm that suits a point found by raising and lowering an arm. Like the gates, valleys, courtyards, and storehouses elsewhere on the channels, the metaphor doubles as instruction: come here to hold, to collect, to steady. The hanzi pair 秉風 is compact enough to memorise at a glance, and once the picture is remembered — a closed hand, a passing wind — the location rebuilds itself more easily than any coordinate list could manage.

四

Traditional Indications, Explained

The recorded indications read: scapular pain; numbness and aching of the upper limb; inability to raise the arm; bronchitis. Framed as tradition rather than promise, the first three form one coherent cluster. Scapular pain covered the dull, stubborn ache of the blade region that follows long hours at a desk, heavy carrying, or cold nights. Numbness and aching of the upper limb belongs to the channel-surface way of reading the body: sensations travelling down the arm were mapped onto the course of the channel crossing this shoulder, so a shoulder station entered discussions of forearm and hand complaints as well. Inability to raise the arm speaks for itself — the stiff, guarded shoulder that refuses to lift — and it suits the point’s own situation, seated in the muscle group that begins the lift.

The last entry, bronchitis, looks like a stranger in such company until its era is restored. The records preserve the medical vocabulary of their time, and classical shoulder-and-back stations were repeatedly drawn into discussions of cough and congested chest; bronchitis names, in modern dress, the kind of chest complaint those discussions circled. Meeting it here is an exercise in historical understanding — evidence of how the tradition connected regions of the body — and nothing more: this page makes no claim that pressing the point influences any lung condition. Anyone living with a cough or a breathing complaint needs proper clinical assessment, not shoulder pressure.

It bears repeating plainly: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that self-applied pressure treats disease, and nothing in this guide replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Where modern research touches acupressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition lightly, and hold the safety rules firmly.

五

Self-Care Acupressure at Home

Settle in first: seated, spine tall but unforced, the working shoulder dropped away from the ear, the searching arm draped over the opposite shoulder so the pads of two fingers can rest in the basin found earlier. Contact precedes pressure — spend a few easy breaths with the pads merely resting — then begin small, slow circles that let the skin glide over the muscle beneath rather than dragging or grinding. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or mild warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, matched where possible to slow exhales so each circle rides the breath out. Swap hands and repeat on the other side, keeping the two sessions symmetrical.

Quality matters more than duration. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; hard jabbing teaches the shoulder nothing except to guard harder. Warm cold hands before starting, because chilled muscle braces. Choose a quiet corner over a screen, and let the practice double as a posture pause: as the circles continue, notice whether the shoulder wants to creep upward, and invite it to sink instead. Resting the idle forearm on a cushion keeps the blade flat and the landmarks honest. People who keep a routine often anchor it to an existing habit — morning washing, an afternoon stretch break — because consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Treat the exercise as a small ceremony of attention rather than a treatment session. Across weeks, observe honestly: perhaps the area feels less guarded, perhaps the arm travels a little more freely while you press, perhaps the only yield is a minute of quiet attention paid to a part of the body that usually works unthanked. Every one of those outcomes fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — pressing harder or longer to force the shoulder into obedience. When curiosity pulls that way, return to gentleness or simply stop for the day.

六

Cautions and Safe Practice

This point deserves more respect than most, and the reason stands written in the licensed record. The dataset cautions: do not needle deeply toward the chest cavity. Behind the plain words lies plain geography — from this high station on the shoulder blade, the ribs and the structures they shelter lie forward and beneath, and careless deep technique launched from here could approach the pleural space, an outcome that constitutes a medical emergency. Professional training manages that risk with exact technique; home self-care manages it differently and far more simply, by staying entirely on the muscle that lies over the blade.

For fingertip practice the rules follow from that picture. Keep pressure light and strictly on the surface: the intention lives in skin and the muscle of the upper basin, never driving inward past the blade or hunting between bones. Use finger pads only — no elbows, knuckles, or tools. Skip the point over broken or irritated skin, during feverish illness, and entirely after a fresh shoulder injury, a fall, a dislocation, or surgery, until a clinician clears ordinary activity. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once and do not try again that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with ongoing neurological or shoulder conditions, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for shoulder pain following an injury, progressive weakness or spreading numbness in the arm, a shoulder that turns hot or swollen or arrives with fever, or any symptom that frightens you. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. The wind imagery is poetic, but the most useful gift Bingfeng offers most people is a better acquaintance with the roof of their own shoulder, treated kindly.

提要
In short

Bingfeng, Grasping the Wind, rests at the centre of the shallow basin above the ridge of the shoulder blade, directly above Tianzong (SI11). Classical practice reached for it when scapular pain, numbness and aching down the arm, or a shoulder unwilling to lift made daily work uncomfortable. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it gently and safely.

忌Cautions from the classical sources

  • Do not drive the needle deep toward the chest cavity.

限Self-care limits

Self-care here means gentle fingertip contact on the muscle of the hollow above the shoulder-blade ridge: nothing driven past the surface toward the ribs, no tools, none over broken or irritated skin, none during fever, and none after a fresh shoulder injury or surgery before a clinician clears ordinary activity. Stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I find Bingfeng without measuring anything?
答Use the shoulder blade itself as the whole map. Find the firm ridge running across the back of the blade, follow it outward toward the shoulder, then slip the fingertips into the soft basin just above it and walk to the basin’s centre. Raise the arm slowly: the hollow that appears under the fingers marks the spot the dataset describes. No numbers are needed — the landmarks and the movement test carry the entire location.
問Is pressing Bingfeng myself actually safe?
答Gentle fingertip pressure on the muscle of the upper basin is generally reasonable self-care for healthy adults, because it stays on tissue lying over solid bone, far short of anything the recorded caution worries about. The danger the dataset flags concerns deep professional technique directed toward the chest cavity — a category home acupressure never approaches. Respect the stop signals: sharp pain, electric or radiating sensation, or discomfort that worsens all mean finish immediately.
問What did classical practice use Bingfeng for?
答The traditional record lists scapular pain, numbness and aching of the upper limb, inability to raise the arm, and bronchitis. Classical texts describe it as a crossing-point where neighbouring channels were said to converge, which is why shoulder-and-limb complaints dominate its historic portfolio. Those are traditional frameworks for understanding the body, documented for context — not evidence-backed treatment claims.
問Why does the hollow appear when I raise my arm?
答Because the muscles that fill the basin change their tension as the arm moves. Raising the arm recruits the very muscle lying in the supraspinous fossa, and the tissues shift enough to open a gentle depression at the centre — which is why the catalogue itself uses the raised-arm hollow as the identifying sign. It also says something about the point being well chosen: classical location built its tests from movements the body already knows.
問Can acupressure at Bingfeng replace treatment for a frozen shoulder?
答No. Self-acupressure is a comfort practice, not a diagnostic or therapeutic tool, and a genuinely locked shoulder — one that will not lift, keeps worsening, or followed an injury — needs professional assessment promptly. Think of the two as different floors of the same building: daily self-care lives downstairs, and clinical medicine keeps the consulting room upstairs that you visit when symptoms persist or escalate. Neither substitutes for the other.
