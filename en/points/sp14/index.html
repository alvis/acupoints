腹結 Fujie

“Abdominal Knot” · SP14

Foot Taiyin · 14 of 21

Where. On the belly, straight down from the nipple, about two finger-widths below the level of the navel.

For. Belly pain, loose stools, constipation.

Press. Gentle finger pads, slow circles, one minute each side.

Caution. Not on the belly in pregnancy.

一

Where Fujie Sits on the Lower Abdomen

On the lower abdomen, the dataset places Fujie 1.3 cun directly below Daheng (SP15), four cun lateral to the anterior midline. A cun is the traditional proportional unit of Chinese point-location — lengths taken from the person being examined rather than from any ruler, so a cun measured across a broad-framed adult differs from a cun on a slight teenager. The catalogue records its neighbour Daheng sitting level with the centre of the umbilicus, also four cun lateral; chain the two entries together and Fujie resolves neatly: find the navel, travel down the same vertical line a little more than a thumb-width, and your fingertip stands on the recorded spot. For most bodies, four cun from the midline keeps the point squarely on the front of the belly, well clear of the sides of the torso.

The terrain under the fingertips here is unlike that of the bony landmarks many famous points offer. The lower abdominal wall is soft muscle layered over membrane, and beneath it coil the loops of the intestine, which shift quietly with every breath and change of position. Nothing rigid lies close beneath the surface for the fingers to press against, and that shapes how the point should be approached: sensation travels through yielding tissue, so gentleness here is not mere politeness but the only method that works. A hand that pushes hard meets shifting coils and provokes guarding tension; a hand that rests lightly learns the slow tide of the breath lifting and settling the belly. At abdominal points, patience comes before force — always.

Fujie belongs to a vertical family of spleen-channel entries climbing the belly. Several neighbouring entries of the same channel record the same four-cun distance from the midline, so the channel ascends the abdomen along a single lane carried symmetrically on both sides of the body, with Fujie sitting low on that lane just beneath the navel’s horizontal. Locating it together with Daheng confirms placement: if your fingertips rest level with the navel they have found Daheng, and a thumb-width and a fraction further down brings them to Fujie. Both sides of the body carry the point equally, and any practice should give the two sides matching attention.

二

A Self-Palpation Walkthrough

Settle the body before searching for anything: lie on your back on a bed or mat with knees bent and feet flat, which lets the abdominal wall soften. Empty your bladder first — the lower abdomen sits just above it, and pressing over a full bladder produces misleading urgency rather than useful sensation. Find the centre of your navel, then measure straight out sideways using your own thumb-width as one cun: four thumb-widths fixes the vertical line the dataset describes. From there, travel down the belly until you sit roughly one-and-a-third thumb-widths below the navel’s level. Sweep feather-light in small circles until you notice a faint thickening, hollow, or dull sensitivity — the quiet signature traditional palpation listens for.

A few calm confirmations help before any real contact begins. Compare the two sides: most people find perception heavier and duller on the true line than a finger-width away from it. Let a slow exhale pass under your palm — the belly swells and settles while the spot stays pinned in place relative to the navel, which tells you your measuring has not drifted. Sensation here typically reads as deep, rounded pressure or a faint ache that blooms and fades, quite distinct from the sharpness that would mean stop. If you feel nothing at all, do not wander in search of feeling; return to the navel and walk the measurements again. Landmarks first, personal proportion second, sensation last — that order protects accuracy.

The classic mistakes are few and easily corrected. The first is landing too high: level with the navel is Daheng, not Fujie, so if your spot sits on the navel’s horizontal line, travel down another thumb-width or so. The second is drifting toward the hip as the hand slides down; keep to the plumb line measured out from the navel. The third is borrowing someone else’s hand to gauge your own proportions — the entire logic of the cun lives in your body, not theirs. When in doubt, start again at the navel and move deliberately: sideways four of your own thumb-widths, down one and a third. One slow repetition beats three clever guesses.

三

Reading the Name: Abdominal Knot

The characters tell the story plainly. Fu 腹 means the belly, the abdomen, written with the flesh radical that marks words of the body. Jie 結 means to knot, bind, or tie; the same character names the tangle in a cord and the lashings that fasten a bundle. Together, Fujie reads as the abdominal knot, or the binding of the belly. Traditional readings take the name two ways at once: as the place where the qi of the abdomen gathers and knots together, and as a direct portrait of what the records say the point traditionally addressed — bellies felt bound, knotted, congested, movement stalled inside. As with most classical names, geography and function fold into two spoken syllables.

Placement gives the name extra weight once the channel map is consulted. Classical teaching draws the spleen channel rising from the big toe, climbing the inner leg, entering the lower abdomen, and continuing upward to the chest; the registry describes the channel as governing digestion, blood, and women’s health. Along that ascent, the abdominal stretch is the channel’s passage through the territory of digestion itself, which is why so much of its recorded traditional work gathers around the belly. One honest observation belongs here: unlike some celebrated neighbours, the dataset records no special point class for Fujie — no mu office, no crossing status. Its standing rests simply on where it sits and what the records say was traditionally addressed there, and that plainness has its own dignity.

Pinyin writes the name Fùjié, and the tone marks matter. The first syllable falls through the fourth tone, a short decisive drop like a door closing; the second climbs the second tone, rising the way a question lifts at its end. Spoken aloud, the name has a compact, knotted rhythm suited to its meaning. The hanzi pair 腹結 is brief enough to hold at a glance, and like many points named for states of things — knots, bindings, meetings, gatherings — the metaphor does real work: it frames the tradition’s aim here as untangling, loosening what has gathered and stuck. Names in this literature are rarely decoration; they are compressed instructions about how the tradition thought.

四

Traditional Indications, Explained

The dataset records four traditional actions: abdominal pain, diarrhoea, hernia, and constipation. Read as tradition rather than promise, each opens a window onto classical practice. Abdominal pain in the old records usually meant dull or colicky discomfort centred around the navel or lower belly — ache from cold bellies, stagnant movement, or food at odds with digestion, sorted by warmth, motion, and rhythm rather than by laboratory values. Diarrhoea and constipation sharing one list looks contradictory until the traditional idea of regulation returns: classical practice treated the spleen channel as ordering intestinal movement and reached for these points when movement ran too loose or too slow alike, the stated goal always being restored order, whichever end of order had slipped.

Hernia deserves careful handling. In classical texts the term gathered a broad family of disorders sensed as something bulging, dragging, or descending at the groin or around the navel, grouped together long before modern medicine separated tissue defects from muscular strain. The listing is a historical fact about the records; it is not advice. An actual hernia involves tissue pressing where it should not, and no surface pressure changes tissue: new or worsening bulges, especially with pain, nausea, or discoloration, belong promptly with a physician. Holding both truths at once — respect for the historical record and clarity about its limits — is exactly the posture this editorial layer asks of its readers.

It bears repeating plainly: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats disease, and nothing in this guide replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition warmly and hold the boundary firmly; the two have never actually been in conflict.

五

Self-Care Acupressure at Home

With the point located, return to the reclined position from the walkthrough and warm your hands against each other first, because chilled hands teach the belly to guard. Rest the pads of two or three fingers — never the tips, elbows, or knuckles — on the spot and begin with contact alone for several slow breaths. Then add small, unhurried circles, letting the skin glide over the tissue beneath rather than grinding inward; the pleasant register is spreading warmth or a mild, satisfying ache, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, timed to slow exhales so each circle rides the breath out. Finish one side, swap hands, and give the other its exactly equal turn.

Quality outweighs duration at abdominal points more than anywhere else on the map. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; force teaches the belly only to clench. Choose a quiet corner over a screen — many people fold the practice into the evening wind-down, when the digestive day is already settling. Breathing low into the belly while the fingers rest turns the exercise into a short reset for the whole torso: inhale, feel the abdomen rise against still fingers; exhale, soften one degree further without adding any weight. Readers who keep routines often anchor the habit to something already dependable — brushing teeth, an afternoon tea — because consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Treat the minutes as attention rather than treatment. Across weeks you may notice the area feels less guarded, that digestion feels calmer on the days you practise, or simply that you spent three minutes caring for your own middle — every one of those fits the honest scope of acupressure. What does not fit is escalation: pressing harder, lingering longer through discomfort, or pressing through pain to force a result. If the belly feels tender beyond its usual dull awareness, skip the day without ceremony. And when curiosity pulls toward force, borrow the point’s own imagery and look elsewhere for the knot worth loosening — it was probably never in the abdomen.

六

Cautions and Safe Practice

Fujie arrives with an unusually clean slate: the licensed dataset records no cautions for this entry, and this page echoes that empty list verbatim. An empty caution field, however, is a fact about the record, not a licence for force. The lower abdomen houses loops of intestine and, just beneath this very region, the bladder; that architecture rewards no aggression whatsoever. Every general rule of gentle surface contact therefore applies here at full strength, and the missing printed warning simply means ordinary prudence must supply what the catalogue does not.

For fingertip practice the working rules stay simple. Keep pressure light enough that breathing stays easy and conversation remains possible; use finger pads, never tools or devices; skip the point entirely over broken, irritated, or infected skin. Leave it alone during acute digestive illness with severe features — violent pain, vomiting, fever — which need medical care rather than massage. After any abdominal operation, wait until a clinician clears ordinary activity before resuming any point practice. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once and do not try again that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone managing a chronic digestive diagnosis, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for severe or worsening abdominal pain, blood in the stool, bowel habits that change persistently without explanation, fever alongside belly pain, or any new lump at the groin or abdomen. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner, medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. The kindest reading of the knot imagery is permission to loosen habits gently; it was never an invitation to press harder.

提要
In short

Fujie, the Abdominal Knot, rests on the lower abdomen a little below the level of the navel, four cun out from the midline. Classical practice reached for it when the belly complained — cramping pain, loose bowels, sluggish movement, dragging sensations. This guide covers finding it by touch, pressing it gently, and the sensible limits of self-care.

限Self-care limits

Self-care here means gentle, purely surface fingertip contact on a relaxed abdomen: no tools, no force, none over broken skin, during acute digestive illness, or after recent abdominal surgery without medical clearance. Stop at once on sharp sensation. Severe or persistent abdominal symptoms call for a licensed clinician — this page is educational only. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I find Fujie without knowing anything about cun?
答Use the navel as the whole map. Measure straight sideways from the centre of the navel with four of your own thumb-widths to fix the vertical line, then travel down until you sit a thumb-width and a little more below the navel’s level. Light circular touching usually confirms a faint, dull sensitivity there. Cun measurements encode exactly this landmark sequence in proportional language — the thumb-width convention is simply the traditional way of carrying the ruler on your own hand.
問Is pressing Fujie myself actually safe?
答Gentle fingertip pressure held on the surface is generally reasonable self-care for healthy adults: the abdominal wall is built to yield, and light contact engages skin and superficial muscle far short of the organs beneath. The dataset records no specific caution for this point. The universal stop signals apply regardless — sharp pain, radiating sensation, or discomfort that worsens all mean finish immediately — and the rules covering pregnancy, surgery, skin, and acute illness appear in the safety section above.
問What did classical practice use Fujie for?
答The traditional record lists abdominal pain, diarrhoea, hernia, and constipation. Classical readings of the name describe abdominal qi gathering and binding at this spot, which is why belly complaints dominate its historic portfolio. Those are traditional frameworks for understanding the body, documented for context — not evidence-backed treatment claims. The hernia entry in particular records a historical category of complaint, not modern advice.
問Why do diarrhoea and constipation appear together among its traditional uses?
答Because classical practice thought in terms of regulation rather than stimulation. The spleen channel was understood to order intestinal movement, so a point on that channel was reached for when movement ran too loose or too slow, with the shared goal of orderly transit. One list holding both ends signals the regulating frame of traditional physiology, not a contradiction — and neither entry amounts to a modern clinical claim.
問Can acupressure at Fujie help a hernia?
答No. A hernia involves tissue pushing where it should not, and surface pressure cannot change that anatomy. The traditional listing reflects how old texts grouped dragging, bulging sensations under one name, and it appears here purely as historical context. New or worsening bulges, particularly with pain, nausea, or colour change, need prompt medical assessment. Respect the tradition as a story about how people once understood their bodies, and take real symptoms to real medicine.
