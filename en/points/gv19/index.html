一

Where Houding Sits at the Back of the Crown

Run a fingertip straight along the centre line of the scalp and it crosses country every point of this channel shares: firm bone beneath a thin, richly supplied layer of scalp, with no muscle bulk to soften the touch. The dataset locates Houding on the head, five and a half cun directly above the midpoint of the posterior hairline, and one and a half cun behind Baihui (GV20). Unpacked piece by piece: the posterior hairline is the hairline at the back of the head, and its midpoint is where that line crosses the body’s exact centre. “Directly above” pins the search to the midline seam so it cannot wander toward either ear. And the cun, as always in this literature, is the traditional proportional measure taken from the person being examined — roughly one thumb-width for most adults — so the recorded distances flex with the body they are measured on rather than with any ruler.

The second half of the location sentence does most of the practical work. The registry entry for Baihui describes it as sitting at the intersection of the midline of the vertex with a line joining the tips of both ears — the highest point of the skull where two arcs drawn ear-tip to ear-tip would cross. From that summit, Houding waits one and a half cun straight back along the seam, on the crown’s backward slope. The chain of midline points continues from there: Qiangjian (GV18), recorded midway between Naohu (GV17) and Baihui, lies farther down the back of the head, so a fingertip walking rearward from the summit meets Houding first and Qiangjian after. Reading the points as an ordered walk turns an abstract measurement into a journey with landmarks, which is how classical location teaching has always preferred to hand down the midline.

What rests under the fingertips here is architecture without cushion. Scalp is thin and snugly bound down to the skull, so every contour of the bone speaks plainly through it. Along the very top of the head a shallow groove can often be felt running front to back where the skull plates meet, though its depth varies from person to person, and hair whorls frequently swirl close by, adding texture but no true landmarks. Because nothing thick stands between skin and bone here, pressure registers quickly and honestly — which is one reason the points of the crown region are traditionally approached with a lighter hand than almost anywhere else on the body. The geography itself teaches the technique before any instruction is given.

二

A Self-Palpation Walkthrough

The ear-line method is the simplest way in. Sit comfortably in front of a mirror if you like, or simply relax and let your hands do the searching. Gently press the tips of both index fingers against the highest points of each ear and imagine a smooth arc connecting them over the top of the head; where that arc would cross the front-to-back centre line of the scalp lies Baihui, exactly as its registry entry describes. Many people feel a faint change under the finger at the summit — a subtle flattening or a faint tenderness — but the geometry alone is enough. Once the summit is settled, slide that fingertip straight backward along the midline, keeping contact with the scalp, through about a thumb-width and a half of travel. Where the finger comes to rest, on the seam and nowhere beside it, is the recorded site of Houding.

A second pass cross-checks the address from below. Find the midpoint of the hairline at the back of the head — easier to locate by touch than by sight: trace the hairline with a fingertip until you reach the point directly centred on the skull. Measuring the full five and a half cun upward in thumb-widths is a long accumulation, and small errors stack, so treat this route as confirmation rather than as the primary search: five or six successive thumb-widths up the seam should land you near the same spot the ear-line method found. Sensation offers a third, gentler check. Pressed feather-light, the true point tends to give a faint, dull awareness where skin a finger-width away feels unremarkable — though honesty requires saying some people feel little anywhere on the scalp, and absence of sensation proves nothing about the address.

Three classic slips account for most missed finds. First, drifting off the seam: without a visible line to follow, fingers wander toward a hair whorl or into the soft hollows beside the midline ridge, so pause now and then and confirm the fingertip still sits dead centre, where left and right halves of the head meet. Second, overshooting rearward and landing on Qiangjian, one more step back down the slope; if the spot feels too far back, return to the summit and re-walk the single distance. Third, measuring through dense hair, which makes distances feel longer than they are; part the hair or work with dampened fingertips so contact reaches skin. When the search refuses to resolve, stop measuring altogether: gentle contact anywhere on this stretch of midline is comfortable and safe, and a professional can confirm the exact level another day.

三

Reading the Name: Behind the Crown

The two characters sketch the point’s whole situation. Hou 後 means rear, behind, or after — the character pictures walking, someone following behind. Ding 頂 means crown or summit, the topmost place, and by extension anything that sits at or pushes up to a peak — the same character appears in words for the crown of the head and for propping something aloft. Joined, Houding reads naturally as Behind the Crown or Rear Vertex. The name is relational: it does not describe the point on its own terms but positions it against the great neighbor just ahead, Baihui, the Hundred Meetings whose registry entry crowns the whole channel. Naming a point by its stance toward a more famous companion is an old habit of the literature, and it quietly tells a student where to stand: find the celebrated summit first, and the modest point behind it announces itself.

That positioning carries meaning beyond cartography. The Du vessel, on which Houding rides, is described in classical teaching as running up the midline of the back and head and uniting all the yang meridians — the great collector through which every bright, active current of the body passes on its way to the summit. Classical texts describe the crown region accordingly, as ground where yang gathers and where disturbance shows quickly as headache, dizziness, or an unsettled spirit. A point standing just behind that gathering place was read as sharing its gravity while facing the quieter territory of the back of the head: less the marketplace of the summit, more the courtyard behind it. This is traditional reasoning about relationships among points, offered for orientation rather than as mechanism — but it explains why the old books grouped this stretch of midline together when headache and dizziness dominated a case.

Pinyin renders the name Hòudǐng: hou falls firmly away on the fourth tone, a decisive downward step, while ding dips and recovers on the third tone, the low-dipping note that gives spoken Chinese much of its melody. The hanzi pair 後頂 is compact enough to memorise at a glance, and it joins the long family of architectural and landscape names that run the length of the channels: gates, wells, courtyards, windows, summits. Here the metaphor works twice over — 頂 names both the physical crown of the head and the idea of a highest place, so the point’s name doubles as a map reference and a small poem about where importance lives. Names like this were mnemonic devices first and poetry second, and they still do their original job for anyone who lets them.

四

Traditional Indications, Explained

The recorded indications read: headache, stiff neck, dizziness, epilepsy, hysteria, and insomnia. Read within their own era, the first three form a coherent picture. Headache at the crown and across the top of the head was the complaint this stretch of midline existed to discuss; classical practice traditionally located such pain by region and matched the point to the territory. Stiff neck — the tight, reluctant nape that will not turn freely — belongs to the same upper-channel geography, since the Du vessel travels the full length of the spine’s centre and connects the crown to the neck it rises from. Dizziness gathered under one heading what modern readers would separate: lightheadedness, a swaying or spinning sense, the dimmed steadiness that classical texts attributed to yang rising unruly or failing to reach upward clearly.

The next two entries need historical translation before they mean anything to a modern reader. Epilepsy and hysteria, as classical category labels, collected a wide range of dramatic presentations — convulsions, sudden collapse, altered awareness, overwhelming emotional storms — under names the tradition used for pattern-matching, not diagnosis in today’s sense. It matters enormously to say plainly: anything resembling these events belongs with qualified medical care, promptly and always; self-applied pressure plays no role in managing a seizure or an acute psychiatric crisis, and nothing in the traditional record suggests otherwise. Insomnia, the last entry, meant disturbed sleep broadly — trouble falling asleep, restless broken nights — and classical teaching placed sleeplessness among the complaints of an unsettled spirit, which is why the calm, high, central territory of the crown appears so often in those discussions. These are frameworks for understanding, recorded for context.

It bears repeating without ornament: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats any condition, and nothing in this guide replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and heavily context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Anyone living with seizures, blackouts, psychiatric diagnoses, or persistent headaches owes themselves proper medical partnership first and self-care only alongside it, never instead of it. Hold the tradition lightly, and hold that ordering firmly.

五

Self-Care Acupressure at Home

With the point found by the walkthrough above, settle in: seated with the spine tall but unforced, or lying back with the head supported, whichever lets the shoulders drop. Rest the pad of one or both middle fingers on the spot — pads, never nails — and begin with simple contact for a few slow breaths. Then add small, slow circles, letting the scalp glide over the bone beneath rather than scrubbing the skin against the hair. Because there is no muscle padding here, the right amount of force feels like pleasant dullness or mild warmth almost immediately; anything sharper is the body declining the offer. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per session, once or twice daily, riding each circle on a slow exhale. If using both hands, keep the pressure symmetrical around the seam so neither side of the scalp does all the receiving.

Quality outranks duration, doubly so on the crown. The scalp answers light, patient, regularly repeated touch far better than force, and force buys nothing here except soreness — the bone is close and unforgiving. Warm cold hands first, because chilled fingertips make the whole scalp flinch. Work without tools of any kind: no combs, rollers, or gadgets belong in this practice, whatever their marketing says. Choose a quiet corner rather than a screen, since the session doubles as a thinking pause — eyes closed, breath slow, attention resting at the top of the head like sunlight. People who keep such routines usually anchor them to something existing: after washing the hair, after an afternoon stretch, before bed. Consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually request.

Treat the minutes spent here as a ceremony of attention rather than a treatment appointment. Across weeks, notice what genuinely changes: perhaps the scalp feels less tense under the fingers, perhaps the shoulders lower as the head eases, perhaps nothing shifts except that you spend two minutes a day tending yourself at the literal top of things. Every one of those outcomes fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What never fits is escalation — pressing harder, lingering longer, or hunting for stronger sensation to force a result. When curiosity pulls in that direction, return to gentleness, or simply close the practice for the day and let it wait. The point has sat behind the crown for as long as the literature remembers; it will keep.

六

Cautions and Safe Practice

The licensed dataset consulted for this entry record zero cautions for GV19, and honesty requires explaining what that empty record does and does not mean. It means the professional literature this dataset compiled lists no special warning attached to this particular point — no recorded danger zone beneath it comparable to the thin chest wall overlying lung tissue elsewhere on the body, and no recorded prohibition. It does not mean the point has been proven safe for everyone in every circumstance; absence of a recorded warning is a statement about the sources, not a guarantee about individuals. The practical reading is calm: this is anatomically straightforward country — skin, hair, bone — and gentle surface contact asks little of it, which is precisely why lightness remains the house rule even where no rule is written.

Everyday rules still apply in full. Skip the point entirely over broken, inflamed, or irritated skin, over wounds, burns, or fresh scars, and during any acute illness until you feel steady again. Wait until a clinician clears ordinary activity after any recent head injury, concussion, or surgery anywhere on the head. Stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation, and give the spot the rest of the day off. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with a neurological or psychiatric condition, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding new point practices of any kind — a caution made doubly sensible by the old associations this point carried. And keep the practice honest to its scale: fingertip pads on the surface, minutes not hours, comfort rather than conquest.

Finally, the wider rule stands over everything on this page: headaches especially carry signals that must never be massaged away. Seek urgent medical care — not a pressure routine — for the sudden worst headache of your life, any headache after a blow to the head, headache with fever and a stiff or rigid neck, headache with vomiting, blurred vision, speech difficulty, weakness, numbness, or any headache that simply feels different and wrong. Persistent or worsening headaches belong with a licensed practitioner promptly. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not diagnosis or therapy, and it never substitutes for assessment. Used within those bounds, the quiet gift of this small point behind the crown is a few honest minutes of attention at the top of a busy day.

提要
In short

Houding, Behind the Crown, rides the midline of the scalp a little way back from Baihui, the celebrated meeting point at the very top of the head. Classical practice reached for it in headache, dizziness, stiff neck, restless sleep, and certain old records of disturbed spirit. This guide covers finding it by touch and pressing this quiet stretch of scalp gently.

限Self-care limits

Self-care here means gentle fingertip contact on intact scalp: no tools, no pressing over broken or irritated skin, none after recent head injury or surgery until cleared, and stop at once on sharp sensation. Sudden severe headache or headache after a blow to the head calls for urgent medical care. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — this page is educational only. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I find Houding without knowing anything about cun?
答Use the ears and the summit as the whole map. Imagine an arc joining the tips of both ears over the top of the head; where it would cross the front-to-back centre line lies Baihui, the crown point its own entry describes. From there, walk straight back along the midline about a thumb-width and a half. The spot where the finger settles, still on the seam, is the location the dataset records. Cun measurements simply encode that same landmark sequence in proportional language.
問Is pressing Houding myself actually safe?
答Gentle fingertip contact on intact scalp is generally reasonable self-care for healthy adults: the structures involved are skin, hair, and bone, with no deep vulnerable territory beneath, and the dataset lists no cautions for this point. That empty record describes the sources, not a promise for every person. Keep pressure light, avoid broken skin and healing injuries, and respect the stop signals — sharp pain, radiating sensation, or worsening discomfort all mean finish immediately.
問What did classical practice use Houding for?
答The traditional record lists headache, stiff neck, dizziness, epilepsy, hysteria, and insomnia. In their own era the first three formed the point’s everyday work: crown-region pain, reluctant nape, unsettled balance. The middle entries are historical category labels for dramatic events that today call urgently for medical care, and insomnia referred broadly to disturbed sleep. Those are traditional frameworks for understanding the body, documented for context — not evidence-backed treatment claims.
問Why is Houding described relative to Baihui instead of on its own terms?
答Because the midline was taught as a chain. Its location also gives the hairline measurement — five and a half cun above the posterior hairline’s midpoint — but the practical anchor is Baihui, easily found with the ear-tip arc, sitting one and a half cun ahead of it. The name agrees with the method: “Behind the Crown” defines the point by its stance toward its famous neighbor. Landmark-to-landmark steps survive differences in bodies far better than absolute numbers ever did.
問Can acupressure at Houding replace seeing a doctor about my headaches?
答No. Self-acupressure is a comfort practice, not a diagnostic tool. Sudden severe headache, headache after injury, headache with fever, vomiting, vision or speech changes, weakness, or a character that feels wrong all require prompt medical evaluation, not massage. For ordinary tension-type days, gentle scalp contact may sit comfortably alongside whatever care a practitioner recommends — alongside, never instead. Think of the two as different rooms in one house of looking after yourself.
