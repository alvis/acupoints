天牖 Tianyou

“Heavenly Window” · TH16

Hand Shaoyang · 16 of 23

Where. On the side of the neck, behind and below the bone behind the ear, level with the jaw angle, at the back of the long neck muscle.

For. Stiff neck; blocked nose; sore throat.

Press. Feather-light fingertip touch, one side at a time, about a minute.

Caution. Never press on a pulse or both sides at once; in pregnancy, ask first.

一

Where Tianyou Sits on the Side of the Neck

On the upper part of the neck’s side, Tianyou occupies the shelf of muscle just below and behind the ear. The dataset places it directly below and behind the mastoid process — the rounded bone you can feel immediately behind your earlobe — level with the angle of the mandible, the corner where the jawbone turns upward toward the ear, and on the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid, the cordlike strap running from behind the ear down to the collarbone. Three landmarks, then: a bone, a corner of the jaw, and the rear edge of a well-known muscle. None requires instruments; all announce themselves to unhurried fingertips. In the channel’s own inventory Tianyou is the sixteenth of its twenty-three stations, but on the body it answers to those three touches, not to arithmetic.

What lies beneath deserves genuine respect, because the licensed record says so plainly: the dataset instructs practitioners to needle with caution here, since vessels and nerves of the neck lie nearby. The side of the neck is one of the body’s great corridors — arteries and veins carrying blood to and from the head travel close to the surface, bundled with nerves serving the face, the ear, and the shoulder. All of that traffic passes through a space narrower than two fingers. Professional training manages the density with anatomy and technique; home practice manages it differently and far more simply, by staying strictly on the surface.

A neighbour helps confirm the address. Directly behind the earlobe, in the hollow between the mastoid process and the angle of the mandible, sits Yifeng (TH17), the next point up the channel. Tianyou stands a step lower and farther back: level with the jaw corner rather than tucked against the earlobe, resting on the rear border of the great neck muscle as it climbs toward the skull. Locating the pair together settles most doubt — find the hollow behind the lobe first, then walk down and back onto the muscle’s edge, and the fingertip has travelled from Yifeng to Tianyou.

二

A Self-Palpation Walkthrough

Sit upright on a firm chair, both feet down, shoulders released, so the neck muscles soften before you search. Start from the earlobe and slide a fingertip straight back until it lands on the mastoid process, the rounded bone behind the ear. Next, cup your palm against your cheek, turn your head gently toward the opposite shoulder, and press lightly against your own palm: the sternocleidomastoid will stand out as a cordlike ridge. Follow its rear border upward with your fingertip until you reach the height of the jaw corner — bite your back teeth together lightly and feel the muscle at the corner of the jaw firm up, confirming the level. Where that height meets the muscle’s rear border, just below and behind the mastoid bump, lies Tianyou. Repeat the whole search with hands swapped, so the other side receives the same careful attention.

A few quiet confirmations settle the placement before any pressing begins. Compare sides with feather-light touch, noticing how the spot sits symmetrically. Feel for the pulse: if a soft beat arrives under your fingertips, ease them off it at once onto muscle — this single habit matters more than any other on this page. Nothing should ache at the true site; a faint dull awareness is already plenty. And keep every movement slow, because the landmarks here are small and the corridor busy.

The classic mistakes are easy to name. Drifting forward into the hollow right behind the earlobe lands you on Yifeng’s territory instead of Tianyou’s. Sliding down toward the throat leaves the muscle’s rear border entirely. Pressing hard to hunt for tenderness teaches nothing and can irritate a busy neighbourhood. If you lose the thread, return to the mastoid process, re-find the jaw-corner level, and measure once more, slowly — the sequence rewards patience far more than force.

三

Reading the Name: Heavenly Window

The two characters tell the story. Tian 天 means heaven or celestial — the highest register of the traditional map, which reads the head as the body’s heaven. You 牖 is the older Chinese word for a window, and specifically a window set through a wall: early character dictionaries describe it as an opening pierced in a wall and framed with crossed wood, and old usage kept it apart from chuang 窗, the opening cut in a roof — in the wall a you, on the roof a chuang. The character carries a second, lovely sense: because a window lets light in, you came also to mean to enlighten, to open someone’s understanding.

Why hang a window high on the neck? In the traditional frame the openings of the head are the body’s own windows, and classical practice read this spot as the one through which the ear hears clear and the eye sees clear — which is why its recorded indications centre on sudden deafness and blurred vision, the moments the window clouds over. The channel adds its own logic: the San Jiao pathway climbs from the ring finger up the outer arm, crosses the shoulder, and rises along the side of the neck precisely here before continuing behind the ear toward the temple, carrying qi upward to the head’s openings. A window set on that road serves everything travelling through it.

Pinyin renders the name Tiānyǒu: tian on a flat, high first tone, you on a dipping third tone. The hanzi pair 天牖 is compact enough to memorise at a glance. Like the gates, courtyards, valleys, and storehouses elsewhere on the channels, the architecture metaphor does real work: it says the tradition treats this spot as an aperture to be opened, not a junction things merely pass. The name is a small set of instructions — when hearing or sight feels shuttered, tradition reached for the window.

四

Traditional Indications, Explained

The licensed records gather eight traditional indications: sudden deafness, blurred vision, head wind, nasal congestion, sore throat, stiff neck, shoulder and back pain, and cervical scrofula. Read as history, they form a coherent portrait. Sudden deafness meant an abrupt muffling or loss of hearing, understood traditionally as qi failing to reach the ear; blurred vision likewise read as the eye’s brightness dimming. Both deserve a modern sentence beside them: a sudden change in hearing or sight is a reason to see a clinician promptly, whatever gentle self-care follows afterwards. Head wind is the old literature’s name for stubborn, recurring headaches that drift across the head like weather.

The remaining indications follow the map as neatly. Nasal congestion and sore throat belong to the same upward passage — a point standing on the road to the face was classically consulted when nose and throat clouded. Stiff neck needs little explaining from a point set where neck meets skull, and shoulder and back pain echoes the channel’s crossing of the shoulder region on its climb. Cervical scrofula is the historical term for nodular lumps strung along the neck, a condition old texts approached at neck points; today any new lump in the neck belongs with a doctor first, not under a fingertip.

It bears repeating plainly: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats disease. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition lightly, and hold the safety rules firmly.

五

Self-Care Acupressure at Home

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle into position: seated, spine tall but unforced, shoulders heavy. Curl your index and middle fingers together and rest the pads — not the tips — on the spot, beginning with contact alone for a few breaths. Then add small, slow circles, letting the skin glide over the tissue beneath rather than grinding against it. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or mild warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, matched where possible to slow exhales. Work one side at a time, then swap hands and give the other side the same unhurried attention.

Quality matters more than duration. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; hard jabbing causes harm and teaches the body nothing. Warm cold hands first, since chilled muscle guards. Choose a quiet corner over a screen and let the practice double as a pause: inhale through the nose, feel the neck soften under still fingers, press gently on the long exhale. People who keep a routine often anchor it to an existing habit — morning washing, an afternoon stretch — because consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask.

Treat the exercise as a small ceremony of attention rather than a treatment session. Across weeks you might notice the area feeling less guarded, the neck turning a shade more freely, or simply ninety seconds of your day spent attending to yourself. Every one of those outcomes fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — heavier hands, longer sessions, deeper pressure to force a result. When curiosity pulls that way, return to gentleness or simply stop for the day.

六

Cautions and Safe Practice

This point earns stricter language than most, and the reason stands written in the licensed record: the dataset instructs practitioners to needle with caution here, because vessels and nerves of the neck lie nearby. The corridor described at the top of this page is the practical shadow of that caution. Everything suggested here therefore stays in the skin’s outermost story — feather-light fingertip contact, taken one side at a time, never approaching the structures the warning names. That restraint is not timidity; it is exactly what makes surface practice reasonable for healthy adults at all.

Translated into household rules, the discipline is short. Use finger pads only, with pressure no heavier than a coin resting on skin. Work one side at a time and never press both sides of the neck together. Never rest weight on a felt pulse — if a beat arrives under your fingers, slide aside onto muscle. Skip the point entirely during feverish illness, on dizzy days, over broken or irritated skin, and after recent neck injury or surgery until a clinician clears ordinary activity. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation, dizziness, visual change, or palpitations mean stop at once and do not try again that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with heart, vascular, or neurological conditions, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any neck-point practice.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for a new lump in the neck, persistent hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, sudden changes in hearing or vision, a headache that worsens without relief, or any symptom that frightens you. Persistent complaints belong with a licensed practitioner, Western or East Asian as suits the complaint. The window imagery is beautiful, but the safest gift Tianyou offers most people is a kinder acquaintance with their own neck — touched gently, and left alone whenever in doubt.

提要
In short

Tianyou, the Celestial Window, sits high on the side of the neck where the great neck muscle climbs toward the skull, just below and behind the bony bump behind the earlobe. Classical practice opened this window when hearing, vision, or the head itself clouded over. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it safely.

忌Cautions from the classical sources

  • Needle cautiously; the neck's vessels and nerves are close by.

限Self-care limits

Neck self-care here means feather-light fingertip contact on one side at a time: never press on a felt pulse, never work both sides of the neck together, no tools or deep pressure, and none at all during fever, dizziness, pregnancy without practitioner guidance, or with broken skin. Stop immediately on sharp or radiating sensation, dizziness, or visual change. Sudden hearing or vision changes, a new neck lump, persistent hoarseness, or swallowing difficulty call for prompt medical assessment — this page is educational only. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I find Tianyou without knowing anything about cun?
答This page never asks you to measure in cun — the dataset locates the point entirely by landmarks. Find the rounded mastoid bump behind your earlobe, bring your fingertip down and back from it, turn your head gently toward the opposite shoulder to raise the sternocleidomastoid ridge, and settle where the muscle’s rear border sits level with the corner of your jaw. Three touches of everyday anatomy replace any ruler, and comparing both sides confirms the placement is even.
問Is pressing Tianyou myself actually safe?
答Feather-light fingertip contact on one side at a time is reasonable self-care for healthy adults, because it engages skin and superficial muscle and never approaches the vessels and nerves the caution record names. The documented dangers concern deep professional techniques — a category home practice never enters. Keep the habits that matter: stay off any felt pulse, never work both sides of the neck together, and stop at once on sharp pain, dizziness, or visual change.
問What did classical practice use Tianyou for?
答The traditional record lists sudden deafness, blurred vision, head wind, nasal congestion, sore throat, stiff neck, shoulder and back pain, and cervical scrofula. Classical commentary explains the pattern through the name itself: a window high on the neck, consulted when the head’s openings clouded over. Those are traditional frameworks for understanding the body, documented for context — not evidence-backed treatment claims.
問Why does a hand channel run all the way up to the neck?
答Classical channel theory draws the San Jiao pathway beginning at the ring finger, climbing the outer arm, crossing the shoulder, and rising along the side of the neck — passing Tianyou — before continuing behind the ear toward the temple. Discomfort along a route was traditionally met at points along it, which is how a point of a hand channel comes to live on the neck. It is the plainest expression of the channel idea: where the road goes, stations are set.
問When should a sore neck or troubled ear mean calling a professional instead of pressing?
答Whenever red flags appear: sudden changes in hearing or vision, a new lump in the neck, persistent hoarseness, trouble swallowing, fever riding with neck stiffness, dizziness, or pain that worsens instead of easing. Think of the two as different floors of one building: daily self-care lives downstairs, and clinical medicine keeps the consulting room upstairs for symptoms that persist, escalate, or frighten you. Knowing which stair to take is itself a self-care skill.
