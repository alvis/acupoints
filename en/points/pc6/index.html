內關 Neiguan

“Inner Pass” · PC6

Hand Jueyin · 6 of 9 · luo-connecting, eight-confluent point (opens the yin wei vessel)

Where. Three finger-widths above the wrist crease on the palm side, between the two tendons.

For. Nausea; palpitations; poor sleep.

Press. Gentle finger pressure between the tendons, about a minute each arm.

一

Where Neiguan Sits on the Forearm

On the palmar aspect of the forearm — the side the palm faces — Neiguan holds the centre line of the limb. The dataset places it on the line connecting Quze (PC3) and Daling (PC7): Daling marks the midpoint of the wrist crease, Quze sits at the crease of the elbow, and the line joining them simply traces the midline of the front of the forearm. Two cun above the wrist crease, the record continues, between the tendons of palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis. A cun is the traditional proportional unit of Chinese point-location, taken from the person being measured rather than from a ruler — roughly the width of the thumb at its widest — so two cun settles at a slightly different distance on every arm. That variability is precisely why the named landmarks matter more than any number: the tendons and creases belong to your body alone.

The two tendons the record names announce themselves easily once invited. Make a loose fist and gently bend the wrist inward, and two cord-like ridges rise at the base of the palm: the flexor carpi radialis heading toward the thumb side, and, in most people, the palmaris longus climbing straight up the middle. Some people lack the second tendon altogether — an ordinary variation of human anatomy, nothing more — and for them the search narrows to the soft strip just toward the little-finger side of the remaining tendon. Between the cords runs a shallow valley, and the valley is a busy passage: tendons, vessels, and nerves travel the forearm together through this corridor, which is reason enough for everything done here to stay feather-light.

Neiguan belongs to the pericardium channel, a route the classical texts draw beginning in the chest, descending along the inner aspect of the arm, and finishing at the tip of the middle finger — nine named stations in all, of which Neiguan is the sixth. It stands midway down that inner-arm stretch, two cun above the wrist, commanding the corridor where the channel passes. On the other side of the limb, on the back of the forearm at a comparable height above the wrist, lives Waiguan (SJ5), whose name means Outer Pass; the two face each other like gates set into opposite walls of one courtyard, and tradition reads them as an inside-and-outside couple.

二

A Self-Palpation Walkthrough

Settle upright on a firm chair with shoulders released and the forearm resting palm-up on a table or the opposite thigh. Begin by finding the wrist crease where the hand meets the forearm; its midpoint is Daling (PC7), the channel station just below. Now make a loose fist and gently flex the wrist so the two tendons stand clear of the surrounding softness, and take a moment to see the valley between them — that valley is the road Neiguan lives on. Trace it upward with a fingertip and you are walking the very line the dataset describes.

For the distance, use the three-finger method. Stack the index, middle and ring fingers of your free hand so that the bottom edge of the lowest finger just touches the wrist crease; the width of those three stacked fingers comes close to two cun, and Neiguan lies level with the top edge of the stack, dropped straight down into the groove between the tendons. Before any real pressing, run the confirmations: rest the pad of a finger there feather-light and compare with a spot a finger-width away — many people notice a faint dull heaviness at the true point and little next door. Let a slow breath move underneath; nothing here needs force to announce itself. Then repeat the whole search on the other arm, because symmetry is its own reassurance.

Three slips account for nearly every mislocation. The first is landing on the wrist crease itself, which is Daling’s territory — if the fingertip feels the crease folding beneath it, slide up the valley until the fold sits clearly behind. The second is drifting sideways out of the midline, toward the thumb side or the little-finger side; return to the two visible tendons and settle between them. The third is pressing onto a tendon rather than into the groove — a difference unmistakable once noticed: tendon pushes the pad back taut and round, while the groove receives it softly. Where the palmaris longus happens to be absent, remember the fallback: aim for the soft strip just toward the little-finger side of the flexor carpi radialis.

三

Reading the Name: Inner Pass

The hanzi carry the whole idea in two strokes of thought. Nei 內 means inner, within, belonging to the inside; guan 關 is a pass or gate — the checkpoint set where a mountain road narrows, the bar that closes a door. Together they read as the Inner Pass: a gate on the interior side, a place where movement between outside and inside is watched and managed. Point names in this tradition are rarely decoration; they compress a teaching about what a point does into characters short enough to hold in memory.

Why inner? The point turns its face toward the palm — the surface that folds toward the body’s own interior — and in the classical imagination it guards the doorway leading inward toward the chest, the domain of the heart and of its protective envelope, the pericardium. Its twin across the forearm makes the pairing explicit: Waiguan, the Outer Pass, faces the back of the body and the world beyond it. One watches the road going out; the other watches the road coming in. Hold the image lightly, but notice how much practical sense it gains once the connecting role described below enters the picture.

Pinyin renders the name Nèiguān. The syllable nei falls through the fourth tone, a firm downward pitch; guan rides the first tone, held high and level like a sustained note. The two characters 內關 are compact enough to recognise at a glance in older charts, and like the gates, courtyards and bridges scattered along every channel, the metaphor earns its keep: this is a crossing place, not a dead end — something passes through here, and the tradition built the name to say so.

四

A Bridge with Two Titles: Luo Point, Eight-Confluent Point

Each of the twelve main channels, in classical theory, keeps one designated station where a collateral departs to connect with its paired channel — the luo-connecting point. For the pericardium channel, that station is Neiguan, and the partner is the san jiao (Triple Burner) channel, its counterpart in the interior-exterior pairing. The oldest descriptions have the branch leave here, reach back up the arm toward the heart and pericardium region, and tie the two channels together in the same gesture. Whatever one makes of the underlying physiology, the picture is coherent: a bridge point, open to traffic from both directions.

The second title is grander. Eight points on the twelve channels — the eight-confluent points — were classically taught to communicate with the eight extraordinary vessels, deep reservoir-like routes lying outside the ordinary channel map. Neiguan is assigned the yin wei vessel, the Yin Link. Tradition teams it there with Gongsun (SP4) on the spleen channel, which opens the chong vessel, while its mirror Waiguan corresponds to the yang wei vessel on the opposite surface of the forearm. These are framework teachings of the classical literature, offered as orientation rather than mechanism.

Read together, the two titles explain the shape of the point’s historic portfolio. A connecting bridge between pericardium and san jiao sits astride the border between the chest and the middle burner; a confluence with the Yin Link ties it further into the interior web. Heart-and-chest concerns on one side, stomach-and-nausea complaints on the other — the double citizenship stops looking accidental and starts looking designed, at least within the tradition’s own architecture.

五

Traditional Indications, Explained

The recorded indications sort naturally into three clusters, and the first gathers around the heart and chest: cardiac pain, palpitations, and chest pain and tightness. In the traditional frame the pericardium is the heart’s envelope and guardian — the intermediary through which the heart conducts its affairs with the surface of the body — so disturbances of rhythm, and the felt weight of a pressed and oppressed chest, belonged naturally to this channel’s discussions, where Neiguan stands prominently. Chest tightness was understood in its own right as qi failing to move freely across the front of the trunk, quite distinct from the sudden, severe pain that has always called for urgent care rather than a pressure routine.

The second cluster is the stomach in revolt: nausea, vomiting, hiccups, and stomachache. Classical physiology wanted stomach qi to descend — food and drink travelling downward as a matter of course — and read queasy belching, retching, and hiccupping as the stomach’s momentum turning the wrong way, rebelling upward. Points on the pericardium channel were traditionally reached for in exactly these moments, on the reasoning that settling the channel settled the ascent. Whatever the mechanism, this is the cluster that carried Neiguan’s reputation furthest, and the one modern readers most often encounter — in talk of travel sickness, and in the acupressure bands sold for it.

The third cluster spreads wider. Insomnia appears in the record, read traditionally as the spirit lacking a settled harbour — the settling of the mind being part of this channel’s classical remit. Epilepsy appears too, a reminder that ancient casebooks catalogued convulsive illness alongside routine complaints; today a seizure, like a suspected stroke, belongs to emergency medicine, full stop, and nothing on this page alters that. Finally come numbness of the fingers and hemiplegia — the historical habit of reaching distally along a channel for troubles of the limb it crosses, and the way classical rehabilitation writing leaned on channel pathways after paralysis. These entries document how the tradition reasoned; they are not invitations to manage serious illness at home.

It bears repeating plainly: all of the above are traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, presented for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats disease. Where modern research touches Neiguan at all, reviews suggest possible modest benefit in some contexts — most explored around queasy stomachs of various origins — with evidence thin at the level of any single point and results varying from person to person. Hold the tradition warmly, and hold the safety rules firmly.

六

Self-Care Acupressure at Home

With the point located by the walkthrough above, arrange yourself comfortably: seated, forearm supported palm-up, hand soft. Rest the pad of the index or middle finger — never the tip, never a knuckle — in the groove between the tendons. Start with contact alone for a few slow breaths, then begin small circles, letting the skin glide over the tissue beneath. Aim for a pleasant, spreading dullness or mild warmth; anything sharp means easing off at once. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, timed where possible to long, unhurried exhales. Finish one side, then repeat on the other, keeping the sessions symmetrical.

Consistency beats intensity in every self-care tradition worth the name. Ten calm minutes spread across a week teach the body more than a single heroic session, and anchoring the habit to something already routine — the last minutes of a commute, the wind-down before sleep — works better than willpower alone. Many people reach for Neiguan specifically during queasy moments of travel or an unsettled stomach; as a traditional comfort practice, that is exactly the company it kept historically, and gentle pressing joined to slow breathing costs nothing and harms nobody who follows the stop rules. What harms is escalation: harder thumbs, longer sessions, deeper digging to force a result. The tradition never asked that of anyone.

Treat the practice as a few minutes of ordered attention rather than a treatment. Over weeks you may notice the forearm feels less guarded, that slow breathing comes easier while the finger rests there, or simply that you spent ninety seconds tending yourself — all of which sits comfortably inside what self-acupressure honestly offers. Keep a light notebook if it helps: which days, which sensations, what changed. Curiosity that documents itself tends to be satisfied; curiosity that escalates tends to reach for more pressure. When in doubt, do less, and enjoy doing it.

七

Cautions and Safe Practice

The licensed dataset records no specific caution entry for this point — unlike certain chest and neck locations, nothing about its geography places fragile structures immediately beneath the fingers. But an empty caution field is not permission to force. The corridor between the tendons carries nerves alongside everything else, and the forearm’s tissues respond far better to patience than to pressure. Every general rule of gentle self-care therefore applies here with full strength.

The working rules are short. Use finger pads only — no tools, no devices, no elbows. Keep pressure superficial and pleasant; sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stopping at once, and not trying again that day. Skip the point over broken, irritated or healing skin, during feverish illness, and whenever the arm is swollen or injured. Pregnant readers, and anyone managing chronic illness or taking regular medication, should ask a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice, so that it fits the whole picture rather than fighting it.

And the standing disclaimer holds over everything: this page is educational only, and acupressure here means self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Chest pain or pressure, breathlessness, palpitations that feel wrong, seizure activity, facial droop or limb weakness, fainting — these are moments for emergency services, never for a pressure routine. Persistent or worsening symptoms of any kind belong with a licensed clinician, whether medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. The Inner Pass is a courteous neighbour to have; treat it kindly, and let it remain what it is — a small daily ritual of attention, not a substitute for care when care is needed.

提要
In short

Neiguan, the Inner Pass, waits two cun above the wrist crease on the palm side of the forearm, tucked in the corridor between two tendons. Classical practice reached for it when the chest felt pressed and the stomach rose in protest — palpitations, chest tightness, nausea and hiccups stand in its old record. This guide shows how to find it by touch and how to press it gently.

限Self-care limits

Self-care here stays strictly superficial: finger pads only, no tools, no force driven into the tendon groove, none over broken skin, during fever, or on a swollen or injured arm. Stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. Chest pain, breathlessness, seizure, or stroke signs call for emergency care, and persistent symptoms call for a licensed clinician — this page is educational only. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I find Neiguan without knowing anything about cun?
答Use the three-finger method and skip the arithmetic entirely. Rest the bottom edge of a three-finger stack — index, middle and ring fingers together — against the centre of the wrist crease, then drop into the tendon groove level with the top edge of the stack. The tendons show themselves when you make a loose fist and gently flex the wrist. Cun measurements simply encode this landmark sequence in proportional language; your fingers reproduce it directly.
問Is pressing Neiguan myself actually safe?
答Gentle fingertip pressure at the surface is generally reasonable self-care for healthy adults, and the licensed dataset records no specific caution for this location. Unlike certain points high on the chest, nothing delicate sits immediately beneath this spot. The universal rules still apply: light contact only, stop on sharp or electric sensation, skip broken skin and feverish days, and consult a practitioner first if pregnant or managing chronic illness.
問What did classical practice use Neiguan for?
答The traditional record groups its uses in three families: heart and chest complaints — cardiac pain, palpitations, chest pain and tightness; stomach rebellion — nausea, vomiting, hiccups and stomachache; and a wider set including insomnia, epilepsy, numb fingers and hemiplegia. Its double title as luo-connecting point and opener of the yin wei vessel was the tradition’s way of explaining that breadth. Documented history and framework, not treatment promises.
問Why is Neiguan so often mentioned for travel sickness?
答Because the stomach-rebellion cluster — nausea and vomiting foremost — became the point’s best-known assignment, both inside classical medicine and in modern wellness culture; commercial acupressure bands worn on the wrist owe their placement to this spot. As for evidence, reviews suggest possible modest benefit in some contexts, with results varying widely and research on any single point still thin. Treat band and fingertip alike as comfort measures, not treatments.
問Can pressing Neiguan replace seeing a doctor about palpitations or chest pain?
答No, and the distinction matters more here than almost anywhere. New, severe, or worsening chest symptoms, breathlessness, fainting, or palpitations accompanied by dizziness are reasons to seek urgent medical assessment — a pressure point is not a triage nurse. Self-acupressure lives in the comfortable territory of daily maintenance and mild, familiar discomforts; anything frightening or persistent belongs with a licensed clinician. Neither role substitutes for the other.
