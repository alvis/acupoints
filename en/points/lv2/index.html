一

Where Xingjian Sits on the Foot

The dataset places Xingjian on the dorsum of the foot, between the first and second toes, at the junction of the red and white skin just behind the web margin. Each clause earns its place. The dorsum is the top of the foot — the side you see looking down. Counting from the inside, the first toe is the big toe itself, so the search happens in the very first cleft, the one right beside it. The web margin names the fold where those two toes flow together into the foot; just behind it means a finger’s breadth back toward the ankle, away from the nails. And the junction of the red and white skin is the old location term for the line where the paler, finer skin of the instep yields to the firmer, fleshier skin bound for the sole — a border most feet display as openly as they conceal anything else.

Beneath the fingertips the ground is kind. The first cleft is a shallow valley of pliable tissue between the two toe roots, padded and mobile, with nothing thin-walled or fragile anywhere near the surface. Toward the big-toe side a slender tendon stands up when the big toe lifts — the very cord that raises it — and the point keeps politely clear of that ridge, resting in the open groove. Spread the two toes gently and the valley opens to view; relax them and it closes again, softly cupping whatever rests inside. Of all the addresses on this channel, few announce themselves with less fuss: no counting, no deep hollows, just skin quietly changing its mind about texture under a patient pad.

Neighbours round out the map. Outward, toward the toenail, the channel holds its very first address: Dadun (LV1), at the lateral side of the end of the big toe beside the nail corner — the well point where classical teaching pictures the pathway beginning. Behind, back toward the ankle across the flat of the instep, sits Taichong (LV3), in the hollow on the toe-side of the junction where the first and second metatarsal bones meet. Between the three of them they own the whole first cleft. The registry counts fourteen stations along this channel, and nearly all of the journey — up the inner leg, across the lower abdomen, out to the ribs — waits beyond this little district. Learning the trio together fixes the gateway in memory: nail corner, cleft, hollow before the bone junction.

二

A Self-Palpation Walkthrough

Settle first: sit on a low chair and rest the searched foot on a folded towel atop a small stool, or draw it up cross-legged if that suits you better — a relaxed, unhung foot shows its folds honestly. Find the cleft between the big toe and the second toe and lay a fingertip in it. Glide slowly toward the sole until you feel the fold where the two toes melt into the foot; that crease is the web margin. Ease the pad just behind it, a finger’s breadth back toward the ankle, then hunt with feather-light strokes for the line where the skin’s grain alters — finer and paler above, denser beneath. Where that shifting line crosses the middle of the cleft, you have arrived.

Two gentle confirmations help before any pressing begins. Compare sides with the barest touch: plenty of people register a faint, dull awareness at the true spot and almost nothing a finger-width aside, though responses differ from body to body. Then let the toes rehearse. Curl them slowly downward and watch the web-margin crease travel forward; straighten them and it retreats. The point holds its post just behind that moving fold, so a fingertip riding the crease itself should slip back one small step. Some searchers also lift the big toe once to raise the tendon cord on the big-toe side, note where it stands, and confirm their chosen hollow lies clear of it in the open groove.

Missed searches stumble in familiar places. The commonest is settling on the crease instead of behind it; the address is one small step back. Overshooting lands you up the instep in Taichong’s handsome neighbourhood — worth knowing, but not tonight’s destination. And a cold, clenched foot buries every landmark: warm the foot, walk a few steps, let the cleft soften, and begin again. Because the location asks for no arithmetic at all, resist inventing any; one unhurried pass of one fingertip reads this little landscape more truly than ten careful reckonings.

三

Reading the Name: the Space of Movement

The characters carry the story. Xing 行 is the ordinary verb of going: to walk, to move along, to travel — the dictionary’s plainest word for setting one foot ahead of the other. Jian 間 names an interval or gap, a space between; in its older written form it pictures moonlight slipping through the leaves of a double door. Read together, Xingjian is the interval where movement passes — and commentators have long taken it two ways at once: literally, the space between the first two toes, crossed tens of thousands of times by anyone who walks; and in the channel sense, the narrow place where the qi passes through on its way. Name and ground agree with unusual neatness.

The point’s classical office adds a second layer. Old teaching sorts the points below the elbow and knee into five categories named for the manners of flowing water, and Xingjian belongs to the spring tier — the stage where qi, imagined slipping into the channel at the well by the toenail, begins to pool and gather like water at a stream’s birth. Five-phase commentary goes further and assigns fire to this spring of the wood-element liver channel, wood feeding flame in that antique arithmetic. The same tradition groups spring points with presentations of heat, a habit of thought that sits easily beside the record’s own heat-toned entries — burning eyes, a throbbing head.

Pinyin writes the name Xíngjiān: xing rides the second tone, a rising inflection, while jian holds the first tone — high, level, sustained. Verified one character at a time against dictionary records, the readings hold steady; the same graph jian also carries a fourth-tone reading reserved for senses of separation and crack, and the name takes the first tone, not that one. The catalogue prints it with its marks, Xíngjiān, and the two hanzi 行間 practically memorise themselves. Channel points lean heavily on nouns of landscape — streams, valleys, gates, courtyards — so a name built on a verb of motion stands out on the map: less a place than a passing-through.

四

Traditional Indications, Explained

The recorded indications read: headache, dizziness, red and painful eyes, facial paralysis, stroke, epilepsy, irregular menstruation, dysmenorrhea, uterine bleeding, retention of urine, hernia, and distending pain of the chest and hypochondrium. Framed as tradition rather than promise, the opening trio travels together naturally. Classical texts describe the liver pathway rising from the big toe, up the inner leg, and into the trunk, and they pair the liver system with the eyes — the organ’s vitality, the teaching says, pours upward into them. Aching heads, swaying dizziness, and smarting red eyes were therefore approached at stations along that road, and on so long a route the foot-end points made favourite handles in classical strategy.

The middle cluster gathers around the cycle and the lower abdomen. Irregular menstruation, painful periods, and uterine bleeding stand beside retention of urine and hernia within the same entry — and the grouping follows the classical map, which draws the liver channel wrapping the external genitals and crossing the lower abdomen on its climb, while assigning the liver the office of storing blood. Disorders of rhythm and flow in that quarter were thus read onto the channel’s own territory, and this spring point stood near the top of such discussions. Distending pain of the chest and hypochondrium closes the list: the tight, bloated weight across the rib-side, in country the pathway reaches at its upper end.

The remaining entries — facial paralysis, stroke, epilepsy — belong to the classical idea of wind stirring within, the tradition’s image for sudden, shaking, or lopsided disturbances of the body’s governance. Read them today as historical addresses, not home advice: a suspected stroke or seizure, a new facial droop, belongs to emergency medicine first and entirely. It bears saying plainly, too, that these are the traditional uses preserved in a licensed dataset and old literature, offered for cultural understanding. They are not claims that pressing the point treats disease. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and dependent on context, and evidence tied to any single point stays thin.

五

Self-Care Acupressure at Home

With the point found by the walkthrough above, get comfortable: seated, the searched foot supported, shoulders easy. Rest the joined pads of index and middle fingers — never the tips — in the groove behind the web margin. Begin with contact alone for several slow breaths, then add small, unhurried circles, letting skin glide over the tissue below rather than grinding on the small bones underneath. Aim for a pleasant dullness or mild warmth; sharpness means lighten or stop. A familiar home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, timed where possible to slow exhales, then swapped so the other foot receives the same attention.

Finesse outranks duration. Light, patient, often-repeated contact suits the wellness register this page practises; jabbing merely teaches the toes to flinch. Warm cold hands first, and give cold feet their due — a short stroll or a rinse in comfortable water loosens the cleft wonderfully. Leave the screen, choose a quiet corner, and let the minute double as a pause: breath through the nose, pressure riding each long breath out. Readers who keep the habit usually tether it to something already fixed — after the morning wash, before the evening wind-down — because what self-care traditions actually request is regularity, not heroics. A point this accessible travels well: chair, hand, minutes.

Hold the exercise as a small rite of attention rather than a session of treatment. Across weeks, notice: perhaps the feet tire less by evening, perhaps the jaw unclenches while you press, perhaps nothing changes except that you spend those minutes squarely inside your own body. All of that fits the honest reach of self-acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — harder, longer, deeper, chasing a result. When curiosity leans that way, come back to gentleness, or close the day’s practice there. The name offers its own counsel: movement, not force — a passage, not a push.

六

Cautions and Safe Practice

Xingjian’s licensed record carries no special caution entry — unlike the thinly roofed points of the chest wall, whose compiled files warn trained hands outright about depth, this file lists location, class, and indications with no warning attached. Absence deserves a careful reading. It reports what the compilers chose to note, not permission to bear down; professional judgement at any point belongs to trained hands; and home practice keeps the same surface-only discipline here as everywhere else. The terrain, happily, cooperates: a padded, mobile valley laid over sturdy small bones, about as forgiving as the foot gets.

The working rules are the household ones. Keep pressure light and strictly superficial; use finger pads only — no elbows, knuckles, pen caps, or gadgets. Skip the point altogether during feverish illness, over broken, irritated, or visibly changed skin, and over any recent injury to toes or forefoot until healing settles. Sharp, electric, or radiating feeling ends the session immediately and retires the point for the day. Pregnant readers do well to consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice, and anyone managing diabetes, reduced circulation, or nerve-related symptoms in the feet should ask first too — diminished sensation deserves extra gentleness, not less caution.

And the wide rule arches over all of it: this page educates; acupressure comforts; neither diagnoses nor treats. Seek prompt medical care — not a longer pressing session — for severe or unusual headache, sudden facial weakness, seizure, abrupt disturbance of vision, inability to pass urine, heavy or unusual bleeding, or a hot, swollen, painful foot. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. The name’s own image closes neatly: between every two strides there is an interval; leave room in yours for rest, and hand what will not settle to those equipped to settle it.

提要
In short

Xingjian — the Space of Movement, or simply the passage between — sits in the cleft between the big toe and its neighbour on the top of the foot. A ying-spring point close to the liver channel’s very beginning, it drew classical attention when the head throbbed, the eyes burned red, or a woman’s cycle lost its rhythm. This guide covers finding it by touch and pressing it safely.

限Self-care limits

Self-care at this point means gentle, purely surface fingertip contact. Do not use tools, and do not drive force inward. Pause during feverish illness, over broken or visibly changed skin, and on recently injured toes. Stop at once on sharp sensation. Persistent fever, sudden facial weakness, seizure, inability to pass urine, heavy or unusual bleeding, or worsening headache calls for a licensed clinician — this page is educational only. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問How do I find Xingjian without knowing anything about cun?
答You need none here — the address is all landmarks. Lay a fingertip in the first cleft, glide toward the sole to the fold where the big toe and second toe merge into the foot, step just behind that crease, and trace the faint line where the finer skin of the instep turns dense and fleshy. Where the texture-change meets the middle of the cleft stands the point the dataset describes. Proportional measurements would only translate the same scenery into another dialect.
問Is pressing Xingjian myself actually safe?
答For healthy adults, gentle fingertip pressure held on the surface is generally reasonable self-care: the site is pliable soft tissue over small, solid bones, remote from the delicate structures that earn some other points explicit warnings. Take the missing caution entry as a shorter file, not a licence for force. Keep the stop signals sacred — sharp pain, radiating sensation, anything that worsens — and the session ends there.
問What did classical practice use Xingjian for?
答The traditional record lists headache, dizziness, red and painful eyes, facial paralysis, stroke, epilepsy, irregular menstruation, dysmenorrhea, uterine bleeding, retention of urine, hernia, and distending pain of the chest and hypochondrium. Spring-point teaching tied the tier to heat presentations, and the liver channel’s map — eyes above, cycle and groin along the way — accounts for the rest. Those are frameworks for understanding, kept for context; they are not evidence-backed treatment claims.
問Why would a point between the toes be reached for headaches or for the cycle?
答Classical channel thinking treats the pathway as one continuous thread: from the big toe the liver channel climbs the inner leg to the trunk, and the teaching links the liver with the eyes and with storing blood, drawing the course around the genitals and across the lower abdomen. Trouble at either end could therefore be answered from the far handle. Hold it as a geometry of tradition rather than physiology — the map repays study even for readers who credit it as history alone.
問When should I skip this point and see someone instead?
答Skip generously: during feverish illness, over broken or visibly changed skin, over fresh injuries, whenever sensation turns sharp, and in pregnancy unless a qualified practitioner advises otherwise. Trade continued pressing for proper assessment when facing severe or unusual headache, sudden facial weakness, seizure, vision disturbance, inability to pass urine, heavy or unusual bleeding, or a hot swollen foot. Self-acupressure accompanies good care; it never replaces it.
