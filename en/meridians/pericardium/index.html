要 · In short

A guided walk along the hand jueyin Pericardium channel: its classical route from the centre of the chest to the tip of the middle finger, the old portrait of the heart’s watchful guardian, and calm, realistic ways to explore the line at home.

一Part 1

The Pericardium Channel at a Glance

In the classical map of East Asian medicine, twelve principal channels were traced over the body, and the pericardium channel — the hand jueyin channel — walks the middle path along the inner arm. Tradition gives it an unusual commission: it serves the xinbao, the protective envelope drawn around the heart, and older writers liked to picture it standing guard while the sovereign organ kept its own counsel. The line belongs to the fire element in the five-phase framework and forms a working pair with the san jiao channel, its exterior–interior partner. On this site the channel carries nine catalogued points numbered PC1 through PC9, beginning at tianchi on the chest and ending at zhongchong at the very tip of the middle finger.

This page is a whole-channel companion to the individual point pages. It follows the pathway as pre-modern sources described it, introduces the points and the roles tradition assigned them, and keeps every self-care suggestion inside the territory of light touch. Nothing here is medical advice, and nothing here teaches diagnosis or treatment. A note on wording: phrases such as “traditionally used for” or “classically associated with” report what historical practice reached for, not statements about demonstrated outcomes — the distinction matters, and the page holds to it throughout.

二Part 2

From Chest to Fingertip: Tracing the Course

Classical pathway descriptions start the hand jueyin channel deep in the torso: it arises in the centre of the chest, joins the company of the pericardium itself, and then slips downward through the diaphragm to visit the three burners in turn — an internal errand readers can take on trust rather than touch. The surface journey begins higher up, at tianchi (PC1), which sits in the fourth intercostal space, one cun beside the nipple. From there the pathway climbs toward the armpit, turns down the arm, and settles into the groove between the heads of the biceps at tianquan (PC2), two cun below the front fold of the armpit.

The descent continues along the inner aspect of the upper arm, where tradition slots this channel neatly between its neighbours: the lung channel keeps to the front line, the heart channel to the back, and the jueyin line walks the middle. At the elbow crease the channel arrives at quze (PC3), resting on the ulnar side of the biceps tendon. Below the elbow the route narrows onto the palm side of the forearm and threads between two familiar cords — the tendons of palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis. Three points share that corridor, counted upward from the wrist crease: ximen (PC4) at five cun, jianshi (PC5) at three, and neiguan (PC6) at two.

Past the wrist the channel crosses daling (PC7) at the midpoint of the wrist crease, enters the heart of the palm at laogong (PC8), and finishes at zhongchong (PC9), the centre of the middle finger’s tip. Two departures complete the geography. A slender branch leaves the palm at laogong and runs to the ring finger, handing the current to the san jiao channel, the paired exterior–interior line; and the main flow, having reached the fingertip, was classically understood to circle onward through the channels that follow. Nearly every landmark on this route — crease, tendon, palm-centre, fingertip — sits within reach of the opposite hand, which is why the channel lends itself so readily to unhurried tracing at home.

三Part 3

Five Points Worth Knowing First

Neiguan (PC6) is the channel’s best-known resident, and its name reads plainly: nei for inner, guan for a pass or gate — together they name a doorway on the inner forearm. Classical lists file it twice, as the luo-connecting point that ties this channel to its san jiao partner, and as one of the eight confluent points, traditionally paired with the yin wei vessel that sweeps the front of the body. Recorded indications cluster around queasiness and the stomach: nausea, vomiting, hiccups, and stomachache appear beside palpitations and unsettled sleep, and generations of practitioners reached for the spot whenever travel or pregnancy stirred the stomach. Its contemporary fame comes from the motion-sickness wristbands sold in pharmacies, which press precisely here.

Nearer the elbow, quze (PC3) closes the forearm’s work with a watery image: ze means marsh and qu the bend, so the he-sea point pictures channel flow pooling where the arm folds. Older records attach cardiac pain, palpitations, stomach unease, and vomiting to the place. Down at the wrist, daling (PC7) collects titles instead of water: it is the channel’s shu-stream point and its yuan-source point together, the latter classically read as a window onto the organ’s own vitality. Da means great and ling an embankment — the raised causeway of the wrist crease — and the recorded indications run to cardiac pain, chest and hypochondriac discomfort, stomachache, and sore throat.

Laogong (PC8), the ying-spring point, sits where the hand labours: lao means toil and gong a palace, so the characters sketch a palace of work in the palm’s centre — find it between the second and third metacarpal bones, closer to the third, exactly where the tip of the middle finger lands when you close a loose fist. Mouth ulcers and bad breath head its traditional register, beside palpitations. Zhongchong (PC9) ends the line at the fingertip as the jing-well point, where classical physiology imagines the flow surging outward; its oldest entries record fainting, heatstroke, and loss of consciousness — emergencies of history that belong with professional care, never with experiments at home.

四Part 4

Guardian of the Heart: Classical Associations

Why give a channel a bodyguard’s job? Classical physiology made the heart the sovereign of the organism — the seat of the shen, the animating spirit that shows in bright eyes and a steady manner — and ruled that so exalted an organ should not be approached directly. The xinbao, the membrane wrapped around it, took the front line instead: outside disturbances were said to meet the pericardium first, and agitation of the heart’s court showed itself as palpitations, restlessness, or a flustered tongue. Evening belonged to the channel too: tradition assigns it the xu watch, roughly seven until nine at night, when the day’s fires were thought to settle and the guard could ease its stance.

These pictures come from pre-modern sources and are offered here as cultural history, attributed generally rather than quoted from any text. They explain why classical discussions moved so freely between breathlessness, worry, and a racing pulse, and why later wellness writing borrowed the guardian image so willingly. They are not clinical claims about how hearts or minds work; modern medicine studies those questions with its own instruments, and nothing on this page stands in for it. Read the old imagery as poetry with a diagnostic habit — coherent, evocative, and entirely of its time.

五Part 5

Gentle Self-Care Along the Channel

The simplest exploration asks only for a quiet minute. Rest the flat palm lightly below the outer collarbone, near tianchi’s territory, let a slow breath out, and draw the hand in one soft line down the inner arm — past the elbow, along the tendon corridor, across the wrist and palm — to the tip of the middle finger. Repeat a few times on each side, letting the pace follow the breath rather than the clock. Keep the touch lighter than seems necessary; the goal is noticing, not kneading.

When you want to meet a single point, neiguan (PC6) rewards beginners. Turn the palm up, make a loose fist, and watch two cords rise in the midline of the forearm; settle a fingertip in the valley between them, two cun above the wrist crease, hold with only comfortable pressure for several slow breaths, then release gently. Daling (PC7), at the midpoint of the crease itself, and laogong (PC8), in the palm’s centre, invite the same patient style. People traditionally reached for these points around travel queasiness and an uneasy stomach. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, with study quality uneven and results unsettled for most uses — a fair summary to carry into any experiment of your own.

A few boundaries keep the practice kind. Avoid broken, inflamed, or numbed skin, and skip deep prodding altogether; the chest-area point answers to the lightest flat-palm contact or to none at all, and the ribs need never be pressed. If you are pregnant, live with a medical condition, or take medication that affects bleeding, ask a qualified practitioner before adding self-pressure routines. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once, wherever it appears.

六Part 6

Realistic Expectations

So what does channel-based self-care actually have to give? A pocket of deliberate slowing, to begin with: quiet attention, longer exhales, hands settled enough to be noticed. Some people find such routines calming through ordinary digestive grumbles or a restless evening, and there is no harm in enjoying that. What the practice does not offer is treatment: no point on this line replaces assessment, and a racing heartbeat or any new sensation in the chest is a reason to see a doctor promptly, not an invitation to press harder.

It helps to keep three frames separate. As heritage, the pericardium channel is a centuries-old way of organising observation about protection, feeling, and the heart’s sphere — worth understanding on its own terms. As self-care, tracing and gentle holding are low-cost relaxation habits some people enjoy. As medicine, neither asserts nor delivers clinical effect, and persistent symptoms call for licensed professionals. Keeping the frames apart is what lets the practice stay both pleasant and safe.

七Part 7

Who This Page Helps

This overview suits several readers: newcomers who want the whole channel before visiting individual points; students of acupressure learning to map the forearm’s tendon corridor; and anyone building a calmer evening routine around the wrist and palm. It equally suits the sceptical, because everything asserted here is either recorded in the catalogue or attributed to general classical framing. Warning signs outrank any routine: chest pain or pressure, breathlessness, fainting, palpitations accompanied by dizziness, or symptoms arising with a known heart condition call for urgent medical assessment.

For ongoing concerns — recurring palpitations, stubborn digestive unease, anxiety that will not lift — licensed practitioners, including registered acupuncturists and East Asian medicine clinicians, can offer channel-informed perspectives alongside conventional care, and they work best coordinating with your regular doctors rather than substituting for them. When you want the fine detail behind this tour, the catalogue pages for PC1 through PC9 hold exact locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

限Self-care limits

Educational content only. Self-acupressure uses fingers and palms, never needles or devices. Keep pressure light and comfortable. Stay on intact skin. Stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問Is it safe to press points on the pericardium channel at home?
答For most healthy adults, yes, provided the pressure stays light and comfortable. Keep to intact skin, treat the chest-area point with particular restraint, and stop immediately on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. If you are pregnant, manage a health condition, or take medication that affects bleeding, check with a qualified practitioner before starting.
問How do I find neiguan (PC6) on my own arm?
答Turn your palm upward and make a loose fist so two tendons rise in the middle of the forearm. Neiguan sits in the hollow between them, two cun above the wrist crease. Settle a fingertip into that valley, press only as firmly as feels pleasant, and breathe slowly while you hold.
問Why is the pericardium called the heart’s protector?
答Classical anatomy gave the heart a membranous covering — the xinbao — and classical reasoning then gave the covering a role: the sovereign organ was too important to meet disturbances directly, so its envelope stood in front. Palpitations, fright, and restlessness were read as stirrings at the gate. The image is historical framing, offered as context rather than physiology.
問Can acupressure on this channel ease palpitations or anxiety?
答An honest answer makes no promises. Gentle pressure is a comfort practice some people find steadying during an anxious stretch, and that is the whole of the claim this page makes. New, severe, or frequent palpitations — especially with chest pain, breathlessness, or faintness — need prompt medical assessment; ongoing anxiety deserves professional care, with self-care kept strictly complementary.
問What is laogong (PC8) for in a home routine?
答Laogong occupies the palm’s centre, arriving under the middle fingertip in a loose fist, and tradition filed it around mouth ulcers, bad breath, and palpitations. In a home setting it is simply a natural place to rest attention: cup the hand, settle the opposite thumb into the centre, and hold lightly while the breath slows.
問Where does the channel begin and end?
答The classical pathway arises in the centre of the chest, surfaces at tianchi (PC1) in the fourth intercostal space beside the nipple, descends the inner arm between the lung and heart lines, threads the forearm tendons past the wrist, and ends at zhongchong (PC9), the tip of the middle finger. A branch from the palm reaches the ring finger, where the san jiao channel takes over.
