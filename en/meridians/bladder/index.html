要 · In short

The taiyang Bladder channel is the longest line in the classical map: 67 catalogued points run from the inner corner of the eye, over the crown, down both banks of the spine and the whole back of the leg, and out along the outer foot to the little toe. This page walks the route, names its landmarks, and keeps every suggestion feather-light.

一Part 1

The Longest Line in the Map

In the classical geography of East Asian medicine, twelve principal channels were traced over the body like rivers drawn onto a landscape, and the bladder channel — the foot taiyang channel — is the longest expedition among them. It begins where few newcomers think to look, at the inner corner of the eye, sweeps over the crown of the head, walks down the whole back in two parallel lines, continues the length of the thigh and calf, crosses the outer ankle, and closes at the outer corner of the little toenail. Along the way it collects 67 catalogued points, numbered BL1 through BL67, more than any other channel on this site. The five-phase framework assigns it the water element and pairs it with the kidney channel, its exterior–interior partner; in the old telling, the two exchange couriers at the little toe, where one channel ends and the other begins beneath the nail.

This page is a whole-channel companion to the 67 individual point pages. It retells the pathway as classical sources understood it, introduces the stations tradition found most interesting, and gathers self-care ideas that stay firmly in the territory of light touch. It is educational material, not medical advice, and it teaches no diagnostic or therapeutic technique. Wording carries weight here: phrases such as “traditionally used for” and “classically associated with” report what historical practice reached for, not findings about what works. The registry spine of this site — codes, names, locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions — always owns identity; the prose below adds story and context, nothing more.

二Part 2

The Course: From the Inner Eye to the Little Toe

Start at jingming (BL1), the small depression slightly above the inner canthus, where classical texts gather several channels together. Next door sits cuanzhu (BL2), in the notch at the medial end of the eyebrow. From there, six points climb the forehead in single file along a line 1.5 cun lateral to the midline: meichong (BL3) sits 0.5 cun inside the anterior hairline, then qucha (BL4), wuchu (BL5), chengguang (BL6), tongtian (BL7), and luoque (BL8) step upward to 5.5 cun above the hairline’s midpoint. Over the crown the line reaches yuzhen (BL9), 2.5 cun above the midpoint of the rear hairline and 1.3 cun lateral, then drops to the nape at tianzhu (BL10), in the hollow at the outer border of the trapezius muscle — the pillar whose very name says it props up heaven.

At the base of the neck the channel divides its labour between two rails. The medial rail runs 1.5 cun beside the spinous processes and carries 18 stations, BL11 through BL28; the outer rail runs 3 cun out and carries 12, BL41 through BL52, before continuing across the buttock as BL53 and BL54. On the sacrum, four pairs of small openings — the posterior sacral foramina — host the liao family in descending order: shangliao (BL31), ciliao (BL32), zhongliao (BL33), and xialiao (BL34), with huiyang (BL35) beside the tip of the coccyx. Off the sacrum, the momentum pools at chengfu (BL36), the midpoint of the gluteal crease, and travels the back of the thigh through yinmen (BL37).

The crease at the back of the knee stages a small gathering: fuxi (BL38) and weiyang (BL39) at its outer end, weizhong (BL40) at its exact centre. Below, the calf line steps down through heyang (BL55), 2 cun beneath the crease, chengjin (BL56) in the belly of the calf muscle 5 cun down, and chengshan (BL57), the pointed hollow that appears below the muscle bulge when you rise onto your toes. Feiyang (BL58) and fuyang (BL59) guard the approaches to the ankle; kunlun (BL60) rests in the hollow between the lateral malleolus and the Achilles tendon; shenmai (BL62) sits directly beneath the ankle bone. Across the outer foot pass jinmen (BL63), jinggu (BL64), shugu (BL65), and zutonggu (BL66), until zhiyin (BL67) ends the journey 0.1 cun from the corner of the little toenail. Classical pathway accounts add interior branches — from the crown toward the brain, from the loin toward the kidney and bladder — and describe the current handing over to the kidney channel beneath the little toe. Those depths belong to story; the surface itinerary belongs to anyone willing to trace it.

三Part 3

The Shu Ladder: Organs Queued Beside the Spine

The medial rail earns its fame through cargo. From BL11 to BL28, its 18 stations carry the back-shu titles — classical addresses where each organ’s activity was thought to surface nearest the skin, named accordingly. Feishu (BL13), below the spinous process of the third thoracic vertebra, speaks for the lung; xinshu (BL15) at the fifth thoracic keeps vigil for the heart; geshu (BL17), level with the lower angles of the shoulder blades, stands honoured as the influential point of blood; pishu (BL20) and weishu (BL21) at the eleventh and twelfth thoracic tend the spleen and stomach; shenshu (BL23) waits level with the navel as the kidney’s own window; dachangshu (BL25) aligns with the tops of the hip crests for the large intestine; and pangguangshu (BL28) completes the roster on the sacrum as the bladder’s namesake. Read downward, the ladder files the torso in order — anatomy treated as administration, giving the back a legend all its own.

The counting is friendlier than it sounds. Bend the head forward: the bump that leads at the base of the neck caps the seventh cervical vertebra, and the station just below it is dazhu (BL11). Follow a shoulder blade to its lower angle: that landmark rides level with the seventh thoracic vertebra and geshu (BL17). Rest your hands on your waist with thumbs pointing backwards: the tops of the hip crests mark roughly the fourth lumbar vertebra, where dachangshu (BL25) waits. The navel, opposite the second lumbar vertebra, gives away shenshu (BL23). Distances come from the cun measure, scaled to each person’s own frame rather than to a ruler — the outer rail’s 3 cun is often taught as the width of four fingers held together. Alongside the ladder, the outer rail repeats the theme with 12 stations of its own, from fufen (BL41) down to zhishi (BL52); among them, gaohuang (BL43) grew so storied that its name passed into Chinese idiom for trouble beyond remedy — a proverbial afterlife no other point on this site enjoys.

四Part 4

Five Stations Worth a Closer Look

Jingming (BL1) opens the register as a crossing point — the kind of junction classical maps drew where several channels overlap. Its recorded associations crowd around the eyes: redness and swelling, blurred sight, tired tearing, itching. Odder entries such as acute lumbar sprain sit filed alongside, a reminder that old categories sorted complaints differently from a modern clinic. The dataset also preserves a stern classical warning against applying heat anywhere near this spot, which tells you how much respect the eye’s neighbourhood commanded.

Shenshu (BL23) is the ladder’s best-loved rung. Sitting level with the navel, 1.5 cun beside the midline, it answers for the kidney system in the old scheme, and its recorded portfolio reads accordingly: aching lower backs, weary knees, and the family of deficiency pictures tradition filed under depleted reserves. Weizhong (BL40), dead centre of the knee crease, carries the channel’s he-sea title — the downstream pool where classical imagery gathered the flow — and traditional mnemonics have long sent lower-back complaints to this address first. Both remain, for home purposes, simple places to rest a careful fingertip.

Shenmai (BL62), directly below the lateral malleolus, is one of the eight confluent points, traditionally paired with the yang qiao vessel — the channel said to govern spring in the step and wakefulness in the eyes; its record lists insomnia and the sprained ankle side by side, day shift and night shift of the same neighbourhood. Zhiyin (BL67) closes the register as the channel’s jing-well, a fingertip-sized station 0.1 cun from the toenail corner where the taiyang current was thought to slip over into yin. Its record preserves classical attention to fetal position and difficult labour — territory that belongs exclusively with supervised maternity care today, and which home practice should treat purely as history.

五Part 5

Everyday Complaints the Tradition Brought Here

Modern life keeps rediscovering this channel unaided. Screen-heavy days leave eyes gritty and brows clenched — precisely the geography of cuanzhu (BL2) and jingming (BL1). Desk posture loads the trapezius border where tianzhu (BL10) waits, and the mid-back rails collect the dull fog office workers know well. Cold snaps send attention to the head-line points tradition reached for around congested noses, tongtian (BL7) among them. None of this amounts to treatment; it amounts to a map of where people already instinctively rub — arguably why the channel stayed so popular.

Legs tell the same story. After a long day upright, the calf knots near chengshan (BL57); after heavy lifting, attention drifts to weizhong (BL40) at the knee crease; long walks complain through the heel near pucan (BL61). Traditional practice filed all of these under the channel’s remit. As for what pressing them achieves, honesty remains the house policy. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Enjoy the ritual for the pause it enforces, and let any ease that follows arrive as a bonus rather than a promise.

六Part 6

Exploring the Channel at Home

Begin with tracing, not pressing. Warm your hands, then draw slow arcs over the scalp along the line 1.5 cun beside the midline, follow the trapezius border down the neck, and let the fingers wander as far along the rails as your own arms will honestly reach — most people lose the mid-back partway and should simply concede it, or borrow a partner’s flat palm for that stretch. Everything below the hips is yours alone. An unhurried sweep from gluteal fold to heel, repeated a handful of times, teaches the channel’s shape better than any diagram.

When you want specific destinations, three forgive beginners easily. Chengshan (BL57): rise onto your toes and watch the pointed hollow appear beneath the calf bulge. Kunlun (BL60): find the groove between the tip of the ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. Shenmai (BL62): drop straight down from the outer edge of the ankle bone. In each, settle a fingertip, press only until the sensation feels agreeable, keep it company for several slow breaths, then release by degrees. Swap sides, compare notes with yourself, and stop while it still feels like a pleasure.

Boundaries keep the practice kind. The eyeball itself stays untouched; around jingming (BL1), the most is a warmed palm resting over closed eyes. Skip broken, inflamed, or numbed skin, and give angry joints a wide berth. If you are pregnant, speak with a qualified practitioner before adding pressure anywhere along the lower back, sacrum, outer ankle, or little toe — the dataset carries practitioner-facing cautions in exactly those neighbourhoods, and pregnancy reshapes the rules elsewhere besides. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation ends the session on the spot, wherever it shows up.

七Part 7

Who This Page Helps

It helps the cartographically curious — readers who want the longest river on the chart explained before sampling its tributaries. It helps desk-bound readers who suspect their nape and mid-back have been trying to tell them something. It helps partners and carers assembling gentle, non-clinical rituals for tired backs and restless legs. And it helps students of the tradition who want the shu ladder’s logic clear before opening the individual pages for BL1 through BL67, each of which carries the precise location, recorded actions, and dataset cautions this overview deliberately leaves to them.

Hold expectations in three registers at once. As heritage, the channel is a coherent old system for organising the body’s back country, worth meeting on its own terms. As self-care, tracing and gentle holding are inexpensive rituals of attention some people genuinely enjoy. As medicine, none of it replaces assessment: back pain joined by numbness, weakness, or changes in bladder or bowel control; fever alongside back pain; any new eye pain or visual change; and everything touching pregnancy belong promptly with professional evaluation. Registered acupuncturists and East Asian medicine practitioners can work alongside your regular clinicians, not instead of them. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

限Self-care limits

Educational content only — self-acupressure means fingers and palms, never needles. Pressure stays gentle and comfortable; avoid irritated or broken skin, and leave the eyeball itself untouched. If you are pregnant, ask a qualified practitioner before working on the lower back, sacrum, outer ankle, or little-toe areas. Stop immediately on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. 問答Frequently asked

問Is it safe to press bladder-channel points on my own?
答Along the legs and feet — the calf, the outer ankle, the whole outer foot — gentle fingertip work is a mild practice for most healthy adults. Keep pressure comfortable, avoid broken skin, and leave the eye itself alone. The upper back is awkward to reach and easy to overdo; prefer tracing with a partner, or simply skip it. Stop on any sharp or electric sensation, and check with a qualified practitioner first if you are pregnant or managing a health condition.
問Why does this channel have so many points?
答Sheer mileage. It crosses more territory than any other channel — face, head, the entire back, both segments of the leg, and the whole outer foot — and the doubled rails along the spine give much of the torso two addresses instead of one. Classical enumerators rewarded coverage: the neighbouring lung channel manages eleven points, while this one carries 67. The count measures length, not importance; every channel had its own reasons to matter.
問What are the back-shu points, in plain words?
答Traditional teaching imagined each organ casting a reflection toward the skin along the spine, and named a station for each — lung, heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and onward down the roster from BL13 to BL28. Practitioners read them as useful addresses; historians read them as an elegant filing system. Either way, the landmarks that locate them — the neck bump, the shoulder-blade angles, the hip-crest tops, the navel — belong to everybody.
問Can pressing this channel relieve my back pain?
答No promise fits inside an honest answer. Gentle pressure at the knee crease or along the sacral borders is a comfort ritual some people find settling, and tradition has long directed attention that way; research reviews describe effects as modest and dependent on context at best. Back pain accompanied by leg numbness or weakness, by fever, or by any change in bladder or bowel control calls for medical assessment first, self-care afterwards — if at all.
問What does the name Pang Guang mean?
答The channel’s Chinese name, Páng Guāng (膀胱), simply means bladder — two characters picturing a curved vessel that stores and releases. The water element extends the image: storage, flow, and winter reserves held in common with the paired kidney channel. Five-phase thinking assigned water the cold season and qualities of depth and reserve, and that tint colours how classical texts talk about this entire channel.
問Where exactly does the channel begin and end?
答It begins at jingming (BL1), in the small depression slightly above the eye’s inner corner, and ends at zhiyin (BL67), beside the outer corner of the little toenail, 0.1 cun from the nail. Between those bookends it covers the head, both rails of the spine, the whole back of the leg, and the outer foot, before handing over to the kidney channel beneath the little toe.
