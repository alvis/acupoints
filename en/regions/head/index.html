Head

The head covers the scalp, face, and crown of the skull, where yang meridians converge and classical points for headache, eye strain, and clarity of mind are most concentrated.

The head holds 3 of the points filed on this site: Fengchi (GB20), Baihui (GV20) and Taiyang (EX-HN5).

慎Never press the eyeball, and keep the hollows at the base of the skull feather-light.

慎Before you press

  • Use fingertips and light, comfortable pressure.
  • Keep off broken, inflamed or numb skin.
  • If you are pregnant, skip Hegu (LI4) and Sanyinjiao (SP6).
  • Pain that is new, severe or lasting belongs with a licensed practitioner.

要 · In short

The head — scalp, face, and crown of the skull — is where East Asian tradition pictures every yang channel gathering. This tour walks its classical landscape, introduces fengchi, baihui, and taiyang, the three entries this region’s record flags, and collects gentle, realistic self-touch ideas for headaches, tired eyes, and heavy afternoons.

一Part 1

The Head at a Glance

On this site the body falls into ten working regions, and the head — 頭 in the registry’s Chinese column — covers the scalp, the face, and the crown of the skull. Tradition gives the region an outsized share of attention: classical summaries describe the yang channels, the body’s bright, active, outward-facing lines, as converging here more thickly than anywhere else. Whenever headache, eye strain, or a fogged mind was the complaint, classical practice reached for head points first, which is why the region reads today as one of the most densely discussed landscapes in the old literature.

This page is the regional companion to the individual point pages. It sketches the landscape as classical sources understood it, introduces the three entries this region’s grouping flags — fengchi (GB20), baihui (GV20), and taiyang (EX-HN5) — and gathers self-care ideas that stay strictly within light-touch territory. It is educational material, not medical advice, and it teaches no diagnostic or therapeutic technique. Wording matters here: phrases such as “traditionally used for” report what historical practice reached for, not statements about proven outcomes.

二Part 2

A Landscape of Convergence

Beneath the imagery sits ordinary anatomy worth knowing. The skull is a smooth bony dome wearing a thin scalp, so nearly everything palpable lies close to the surface; the face carries more muscle and vessel than bone, which is why its tissues feel so responsive to the lightest contact. Classical pathway drawings fill this terrain generously. The du channel climbs the spine’s midline, crosses the crown, and continues downward over the forehead. The bladder channel sends two parallel lines from the inner corner of the eyes over the top of the head and away down the back. The gallbladder channel zigzags across temple and side of the head; the stomach channel opens in the centre of the face and sweeps outward across cheek and forehead; the san-jiao channel skirts the ear toward the outer end of the brow.

The convergence is the point, traditionally speaking. Yang names the active, protective, exterior-facing half of the classical physiology, and the head was pictured as its meeting hall — later writers condensed the whole idea into a single saying: the head is where all the yang of the body comes together. The channels named above are exactly the companions this region’s record lists — du, gallbladder, stomach, bladder, and san-jiao — and reading them together turns the head from a scatter of landmarks into one connected map.

三Part 3

Three Flagship Points

Fengchi (GB20), whose name translates as “wind pond”, belongs to the gallbladder channel and sits in the soft hollows either side of the spine’s upper end, where the skull base meets the neck. The name preserves classical pathology: wind, imagined as the restless outside influence that slips in where head meets neck, was said to pool in these depressions. Recorded indications gather around headache, neck tension, eye strain, and the earliest scratchy stage of a cold — the cluster you would expect of a gatekeeper post at the head’s back door.

Baihui (GV20) crowns the whole system. The name renders as “hundred meetings”, and the place matches the poetry: at the very top of the skull, on the midline, right where a line joining the tips of the ears would pass. It belongs to the du channel — the governing vessel — and classical texts treat it as a grand meeting ground to which other pathways pay their respects. Its recorded associations lean inward rather than outward: lifting mood, clearing the mind, and anchoring attention that has scattered.

Taiyang (EX-HN5), the temple point, is the odd one out and the people’s favourite. Its code marks it as an extra point, outside the twelve regular channels altogether, yet few spots are better known in folk practice: the shallow depression a little behind where the outer end of the eyebrow meets the outer corner of the eye. Recorded indications stay close to home — headache, eye strain, and facial tension — which explains why generations of tired readers have found their own way there.

四Part 4

Headaches, Tired Eyes, Heavy Afternoons

The complaints people have always carried to this region are ordinary and recognisable. Headache stands first — the registry’s own summary notes that classical points for headache concentrate here more than anywhere else. Around it gather tired, gritty eyes after long hours of reading or screens; a band of tightness settling across temples and the back of the head after concentrated work; and the dull heaviness of an afternoon when thought itself feels slow.

Classical writing explained such states in its own vocabulary: yang flow failing to rise cleanly to its meeting hall, or external wind settling into the nape and clouding the head. Those pictures belong to a pre-modern physiology and are offered here as cultural context, never as diagnosis. What they preserve is a habit of attention — noticing where the head stores its fatigue — that anyone who works at a desk will recognise at once.

五Part 5

Self-Applied Touch for a Delicate Region

Once a few habits are set, self-applied touch adapts easily to the head’s delicate structures. Use finger pads, never a digging fingertip or a knuckle, and keep pressure at the level of a coin resting on skin. Small slow circles suit the temple depression of taiyang (EX-HN5); a shampoo-style fingertip kneading wakes the whole scalp without hunting for any single point; palms warmed until they glow may rest over closed eyes carrying no weight at all. At the nape hollows of fengchi (GB20), settle two finger pads and hold lightly through several slow breaths, letting the head’s own weight do the sinking. Pace follows the breath, not the clock.

Boundaries matter more here than almost anywhere else on the body. Never press on the eyeball itself — the orbital rim of bone is as close as touch should come. Give the skull-base hollows feather-light contact only; pushing deeper earns nothing. Skip any skin that is broken, inflamed, numb, freshly scarred, or affected by a skin condition, and leave the ear canal and the inside of the nostrils alone. No tools or gadgets belong in this routine. If you are pregnant, manage a health condition, or take medication that affects bleeding, ask a qualified practitioner before starting, and treat sharp, electric, or radiating sensation as an instant signal to stop.

六Part 6

Realistic Expectations

What can head-focused self-touch honestly offer? At minimum, a deliberate pause: closed eyes, slower breathing, hands busy with something kind. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, study quality varies, and results remain unsettled for most uses — so nothing on this page should be read as promising relief.

Keeping the frames apart keeps expectations fair. As heritage, the head is a coherent old map of convergence, worth understanding on its own terms. As self-care, temple circles and scalp kneads are low-cost rituals some people find pleasant and settling. As medicine, they replace nothing: assessment belongs to clinicians, and the head — precisely because a symptom there can occasionally signal something serious — deserves a lower threshold for professional attention than most regions, not a higher one.

七Part 7

When Head Symptoms Need Professional Care

Some head symptoms belong promptly with a doctor, without a detour through self-care. Seek urgent assessment for the sudden, severe, or simply unfamiliar headache unlike any before it; any headache after a blow to the head or a fall; headache accompanied by fever and a stiff neck; new disturbance of vision, speech, or balance; weakness or numbness anywhere in the body; or a familiar headache pattern that abruptly changes character or keeps worsening.

Beyond emergencies stretches the wider country of ongoing complaints — recurring headaches, persistently uncomfortable eyes, jaw and facial tension — where licensed practitioners, including registered acupuncturists and traditional medicine clinicians, can add a channel-informed perspective alongside conventional care. Bring your questions and your medication history, and expect coordination with your regular doctors rather than replacement of them. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

限Self-care limits

Educational content only — self-acupressure here means finger pads and a soft palm, never needles or tools. Never press the eyeball; give the skull-base hollows feather-light contact only; skip broken, inflamed, numb, or freshly scarred skin. Stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問Is it safe to work on head and temple points yourself?
答For most healthy adults, gentle fingertip contact on the scalp, temples, and brow is a mild practice — far lighter than limb work needs to be. Respect the avoid-zones: never the eyeball, feather-light contact only in the skull-base hollows, and nothing on broken or irritated skin. Stop on sharp or radiating sensation. If you are pregnant, manage a medical condition, or take medication that affects bleeding, check with a qualified practitioner first.
問Why does this region highlight only three points?
答Because the regional grouping on this site flags flagship entries rather than listing everything anatomically possible. Many catalogued points along the du, gallbladder, stomach, bladder, and san-jiao channels touch the head, and each lives on its own channel and point pages. Fengchi (GB20), baihui (GV20), and taiyang (EX-HN5) are the entries this region’s record singles out — one for the nape, one for the crown, one for the temples.
問What makes baihui (GV20) so celebrated?
答Location and title reinforce each other. It sits at the very crown of the skull on the du channel, and its name — “hundred meetings” — records the classical picture of countless pathways converging overhead; texts accordingly granted it grand meeting-point standing. At home it asks nothing elaborate: a light fingertip rest at the top of the head, pressure barely there, is the entire practice.
問Where does the idea that all the yang channels meet in the head come from?
答From the classical literature itself, which repeatedly describes the yang channels reaching the face and head and condenses the observation into a single maxim about the head as the confluence of yang. It is a pre-modern organising image, offered as context rather than verified anatomy — though it explains neatly why so much of the point catalogue crowds into this one region.
問Can pressing these points treat my migraines?
答No honest answer fits inside a promise. Self-acupressure is a comfort practice some people find calming during ordinary head tension; it is not a treatment, and this page claims no effect on migraine. Migraine deserves proper medical management, and self-care should never displace a prescribed plan. If gentle temple or nape touch accompanies your usual care pleasantly, enjoy it as a companion, and involve licensed practitioners as complements rather than replacements.
問How firm should pressure on the head be compared with the arms or legs?
答Noticeably softer. Scalp lies directly on bone, the temples overlie sensitive vessels, and the face reacts to far less force than a thigh ever would — so take the lightest touch you would use on a closed eyelid and apply it everywhere else on the head. Brief holds with slow breathing beat long hard sessions, and comfort sets the ceiling, not the target.
