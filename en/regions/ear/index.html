Ear

The ear is a microsystem in its own right, with points used for hearing, balance, and a broad range of auricular-therapy protocols; several regular meridians also skirt its margins.

The ear holds 5 of the points filed on this site: Tinggong (SI19), Tinghui (GB2), Ermen (TH21), Yifeng (TH17) and Jiaosun (TH20).

慎Keep touch feather-light and nothing inside the ear canal; see a doctor for sudden hearing loss or ear pain.

慎Before you press

  • Use fingertips and light, comfortable pressure.
  • Keep off broken, inflamed or numb skin.
  • If you are pregnant, skip Hegu (LI4) and Sanyinjiao (SP6).
  • Pain that is new, severe or lasting belongs with a licensed practitioner.

    要 · In short

    A quiet tour of the smallest region in the atlas: the outer ear, described by classical writers as a gathering place of the channels and later reread as a microsystem of the whole body, with three meridians skirting its margins and self-touch that stays feather-light throughout.

    一Part 1

    The Ear Region at a Glance

    Among the ten regions this site uses to gather acupoints, the ear is by far the smallest, and the only one built mostly of cartilage. Its registry entry is spare almost to bluntness: the single character 耳, pronounced ěr with a falling-rising third tone, and a summary that calls the ear “a microsystem in its own right”, with points tradition associated with hearing and balance while several regular meridians skirt its margins. Every clause of that little description opens a door this page walks through gently.

    One fact deserves stating up front, because honesty reads better than tidiness: none of the catalogue’s classical-meridian points sit on the ear’s own cartilage. The five gathered on this page ring it instead, three in front of the tragus, one behind the lobe and one above the apex, and they belong to the three channels listed beside it in the registry — the San Jiao, gallbladder, and small intestine meridians — whose courses climb past jaw and temple and loop around the ear’s rim in the old pathway drawings. Each keeps its full leaf on its channel page. What remains here is something quieter: the landscape of the ear as tradition saw it, and ways to rest your hands on it safely.

    Delicacy is the operating word throughout. The visible ear is a thin curl of cartilage wrapped in skin, with the opening of the ear canal at its centre and the jaw joint just in front — structures that ask for less force than any muscle pad elsewhere on the body. Everything below keeps to that register: touch measured in grams of intent, attention over manipulation, and clear boundaries drawn before curiosity begins.

    二Part 2

    A Map Drawn in Cartilage

    Classical writers gave the ear a distinguished social position among the channels. Texts describe it generally as a place where the vessels gather — a meeting ground where many pathways of head and body come within reach of one another — and the old pathway diagrams honour the idea, sending yang channels toward and around the ear from several directions at once. Read that way, the ear was less a lone outpost than a busy crossroads, which helps explain why so much classical discussion of hearing lives under neighbouring channels rather than under the ear alone.

    The better-known idea, though, is younger and bolder: the ear as a map of the entire body in miniature. Detailed somatotopic charts trace much of their modern popularity to mid-twentieth-century European practice, building on far older Chinese observations about channels converging near the ear. The resulting field, auricular therapy, developed its own geography, its own point names, and its own clinical settings; it belongs mostly to trained practitioners rather than to home routines. None of that history amounts to a claim of effect, and this page treats the miniature-map idea as cultural inheritance — fascinating to understand, not a licence to diagnose.

    Keeping the two stories separate makes everything clearer. The meridian story says the great channels pass close by, their points sitting along their own lines. The auricular story says the whole body is reflected on the ear itself. This site catalogues the first and only describes the second, and the self-touch suggested below needs neither map to be safe — it asks only for a light hand and ordinary care.

    三Part 3

    Three Channels at the Margin

    First among neighbours comes the hand shaoyang San Jiao channel, whose classical course finishes its head-work by encircling the ear: ascending behind it, entering within it, and emerging in front of it, according to the old pathway accounts, before arriving at the outer end of the eyebrow. The points flanking the ear along this line carry recorded indications clustered around hearing and cheek — details kept deliberately on the channel’s own pages, where each point’s location, actions, and dataset cautions render properly.

    The foot shaoyang gallbladder channel arrives next, zigzagging over the temple and folding back and forth across the ear’s upper margin on its way down the side of the head. Classical commentary enjoyed the symbolism: shaoyang was imagined as a hinge between interior and exterior, and the lateral line expresses that hinge physically. The hand taiyang small intestine channel completes the trio, its course described as passing into the ear itself, sharing the crowded ground around jaw and cheek with the other two.

    For a reader here, the practical meaning is simple. Complaints that seem to sit “in the ear” — fullness, ringing, tension around the jaw — were classically discussed across all three lines at once, because the ear is ringed by them rather than owned by any single one. Following those lines outward is easy: each of the three has a whole-channel companion page on this site, and their individual points carry the precise locations and recorded cautions this regional overview leaves to them.

    四Part 4

    Everyday Complaints Tradition Brought Here

    What did tradition bring to this small territory? Above all, matters of hearing: classical practice reached for the ear-flanking stretches of the three channels whenever deafness, ringing, or abrupt ear trouble appeared in older case lists. Balance came next — dizziness and a swaying world were discussed along the same head-side lines — and then the crowded borderland of jaw, cheek, and temple, where toothache, facial pain, and headache were read as neighbours of ear complaints rather than strangers to them.

    Modern days hand the region a few complaints of its own. Hours of headphones leave ears feeling tired or full; jaw-clenching through concentrated screen work sends tension to the joint sitting directly in front of the ear canal; winter drafts and overnight pillow pressure finish the job. None of this turns the ear into a treatment site, and nothing here claims an effect. It simply marks the ear as everyday territory — a place people notice discomfort — which is precisely why a few habits of gentle handling are worth keeping.

    五Part 5

    Touch That Stays Feather-Light

    The technique that suits this region best is barely a technique at all. Wash your hands, settle somewhere quiet, and let a fingertip rest before it moves: trace the helix — the curled outer rim — from top to lobe and back again; cup the whole ear warmly in a palm for the length of a few slow breaths; cradle the soft lobe between finger and thumb with no more squeeze than a whisper. Warmth and patience do the work. If any touch registers as pressure, halve it.

    Boundaries deserve their own paragraph, because the avoid-zones here are real. Nothing — not a fingertip, not a cotton bud, not anything else — goes into the ear canal; the canal cleans itself, and objects only endanger it. Skip broken, inflamed, flaking, or freshly pierced skin, and give healing piercings a wide berth entirely. Cartilage dislikes firm squeezing far more than muscle does, so no pinching, pinning, or clip-style gadgets. Set hearing aids aside first, treat the jaw joint in front of the canal as look-don’t-dig territory, and stop at once if anything produces pain, dizziness, or a change in ringing.

    Finally, the standing caveats that travel with every page on this site. Self-applied touch is a comfort practice, not therapy; pregnancy, an ongoing health condition, or medication that affects bleeding or skin earns a conversation with a qualified practitioner before any new routine begins. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stopping immediately, wherever it appears. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

    六Part 6

    When Ears Need More Than Comfort

    Some ear stories belong with a doctor promptly, ahead of any self-care and without waiting to see whether time settles them. Sudden hearing loss or a sudden change in hearing; ringing that is new, one-sided, or pulsing; discharge, bleeding, or significant pain from the canal; spinning dizziness arriving without warning; redness or swelling spreading over the outer ear after a knock — each is information a professional should hear soon. Children’s ear complaints move in the same direction: assessed first, comforted second.

    Between emergencies and everyday wear lies the slower country of ongoing matters — recurring fullness, long-standing ringing a doctor has already assessed, stiffness around the jaw — where licensed practitioners of either tradition can offer perspectives alongside regular medical care, coordinating with it rather than replacing it. Bring questions and medication lists to any consultation, expect collaboration, and keep hold of this page’s central proportion: the ear is a small, delicate region, and the care it deserves is sized to match.

    七Part 7

    Realistic Expectations

    What can honest self-touch offer here? A few unhurried minutes of warmth and attention; a reason to pause during loud, bright, screen-lit days; the plain pleasure of a tired ear being handled kindly. Beyond that, restraint in advertising is part of the service. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and auricular techniques in particular are studied mostly as practitioner-delivered care rather than as home habits. No page on this site promises outcomes, and the smallest region deserves the smallest claims.

    Holding frames apart keeps the balance. As heritage, the ear gathers three old stories — a crossroads of channels, a meeting place of vessels, later a miniature map — each coherent in its own century. As self-care, light handling of the outer ear is a safe, low-cost ritual many people enjoy for its own sake. As medicine, ears are organs with specialists of their own, and anything persistent, painful, or frightening goes to them first. When curiosity about a particular point stirs, the San Jiao, gallbladder, and small intestine channel pages make natural next steps, each leading onward to points whose locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions speak for themselves.

    限Self-care limits

    Educational content only. Self-care here means clean fingers on the visible outer ear — never needles, and nothing placed inside the ear canal. Keep every touch lighter than feels necessary, and skip broken, inflamed, or freshly pierced skin. Stop at once on sharp pain, dizziness, or any change in ringing. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

    問答Frequently asked

    問Is it safe to touch my own ears as part of self-care?
    答Outer-ear touch, done lightly with clean hands, is a gentle practice for most healthy adults. Keep to the visible ear: the rim, the bowl, the lobe. Never put anything into the ear canal, skip broken or freshly pierced skin, and stop at once on sharp pain, dizziness, or a change in ringing. If you are pregnant, manage a health condition, or take medication affecting skin or bleeding, ask a qualified practitioner before starting anything new.
    問Why is the ear described as a microsystem?
    答Because an influential school of thought reads the whole body as reflected on the ear in miniature — a map compressed into cartilage. The idea has old roots in observations that many channels converge near the ear, and its detailed modern charts grew largely out of mid-twentieth-century European practice. This page presents it as cultural history: an interesting way of seeing, not a diagnostic tool, and not a claim about what pressing any spot achieves.
    問Does this site catalogue any acupoints on the ear itself?
    答Not on the ear’s own cartilage, and that is deliberate honesty rather than oversight. This page gathers the five channel points that ring the ear instead: Tinggong (SI19), Tinghui (GB2) and Ermen (TH21) in front of it, Yifeng (TH17) behind the lobe and Jiaosun (TH20) above the apex. They belong to the San Jiao, gallbladder, and small intestine channels, and each keeps its own leaf with a precise location and recorded dataset cautions. The famous auricular microsystem points belong to a separate therapeutic system outside this site’s classical catalogue altogether.
    問Can pressing around the ear relieve tinnitus?
    答Nothing here promises that. Ringing deserves assessment before experimentation: new, one-sided, pulsing, or persistent tinnitus calls for a doctor first, because ears can signal things self-care must never manage alone. Once serious causes are excluded, some people simply find a warm, light hold around the outer ear calming during quiet moments — pleasant company for an annoying symptom, framed honestly as comfort rather than remedy.
    問My ears feel tired after long days of headphones — would touching them help?
    答Touching may feel pleasant; whether it does more than feel pleasant is not something this page claims. What reliably serves headphone-tired ears is less heroic: lower volumes, regular silence, breaks that stretch longer than a song. A gentle palm-cup and a slow trace along the rim cost nothing and can round off an evening nicely. Pain, fullness that persists, or any hearing change is a cue to book an appointment rather than press harder.
    問How is this page different from auricular therapy?
    答Auricular therapy is its own clinical system, with detailed ear maps, practitioner-applied methods, and cautions of its own; it usually happens in a clinic, delivered by someone trained in it. This page stays with plain self-touch on the outer ear and the heritage context around the region, teaching no protocols and endorsing none. Curious about the professional field? Raise it with a qualified practitioner — the same person who can tell you whether it suits your situation.
