Leg

The leg spans the hip to the ankle and carries six of the twelve regular meridians, with points along the shin, calf, and thigh used for digestion, back pain, and women’s health.

The leg holds 5 of the points filed on this site: Zusanli (ST36), Sanyinjiao (SP6), Yinlingquan (SP9), Weizhong (BL40) and Chengshan (BL57).

慎Keep off varicose veins, and touch the hollow behind the knee lightly at most. Skip Sanyinjiao (SP6) if you are pregnant.

慎Before you press

  • Use fingertips and light, comfortable pressure.
  • Keep off broken, inflamed or numb skin.
  • If you are pregnant, skip Hegu (LI4) and Sanyinjiao (SP6).
  • Pain that is new, severe or lasting belongs with a licensed practitioner.

要 · In short

The leg carries six of the twelve regular meridians between hip and ankle — stomach, spleen, bladder, kidney, gallbladder, and liver threading thigh, knee, and calf. This page walks the terrain: how tradition mapped the limb, the five registry points marked here, and ways gentle self-touch can suit such muscular ground.

一Part 1

One Limb, Six Channels

The leg is straightforward country for a beginner’s map: one long thigh bone above, two bones running side by side below the knee, and around them the heaviest muscles the body owns. Tradition gave the limb its own character — 腿 — and never treated it as mere scaffolding. The region spans hip to ankle, carries six of the twelve regular meridians, and holds points along the shin, the calf, and the thigh that older practice connected with digestion, back trouble, and women’s health. Those few lines are the whole premise of this page.

Why did so many channels crowd into one limb? Classical teaching drew three foot-yang channels descending from head toward toes and three foot-yin channels climbing the other way, from foot toward chest — which means every one of them crosses this ground. The stomach, spleen, bladder, kidney, gallbladder, and liver channels each trace some part of the thigh, knee, or lower leg before reaching the foot, and each carries the leg’s own weather — fatigue after standing, stiffness in cold months, the ache of a long walk — into the wider conversation of the body. A limb that busy was always going to collect important addresses.

二Part 2

The Six Channels That Travel Here

Seen from outside, three yang channels ride the leg’s public faces. The stomach channel comes down the front of the thigh and continues along the shin, a finger-breadth outside the bone’s sharp front ridge. The gallbladder channel keeps to the outer border, passing the hip and running down the side of the thigh and calf. The bladder channel takes the private rear route, dropping through the hamstrings to the hollow behind the knee and onward into the calf.

The inner face belongs to the three yin channels. Classical drawings place the spleen channel climbing from the ankle along the shinbone’s back edge; the kidney channel rises through the same inner territory a little further back, nearer the Achilles line; and the liver channel travels between the two, up the middle of the inner leg. Near the inner ankle the three run so close together that the old maps gave the junction a name of its own — sanyinjiao, the crossing of the three yin — which tells you how seriously this meeting was taken.

Classical theory liked order, and it paired the six into three couples: stomach with spleen, bladder with kidney, gallbladder with liver. Each couple joins a yang member riding an outer or rear surface to a yin partner on the sheltered inner line, and the pairs were believed to answer for one another — a thought tradition used when a clue on one side pointed practice toward the other. Whatever one makes of the physiology, the pairing is elegant cartography: three thoroughfares down the outside and back, three quiet lanes inside.

三Part 3

Five Points the Registry Marks Here

Five catalogued points carry this region’s flag, and two stand guard just below the knee. Zusanli (ST36) belongs to the stomach channel, settling into the muscle a little out from the shinbone below the knee’s outer side; it ranks among the most honoured addresses in the whole system, at once a he-sea point — in classical imagery the pool where channel flow gathers deep, like a river reaching the sea — and a celebrated command point, invoked for generations around appetite, digestion, and general vitality. Yinlingquan (SP9) answers from the inner leg: a spleen-channel he-sea point tucked into the hollow below the inner knee where the calf muscle meets the shinbone’s back edge, classically sought whenever the body felt heavy or waterlogged.

Lower down the inner leg stands sanyinjiao (SP6), on the spleen channel, about four finger-widths above the tip of the inner ankle bone. Its name means the meeting of three yin, and tradition treats the spot as a junction where the spleen, liver, and kidney channels trade traffic; older texts reach for it constantly in matters of women’s health. Prominence carries a recorded caution: custom asks pregnant readers to leave this point alone, a rule the catalogue states plainly and this page repeats later.

Behind the knee, weizhong (BL40) occupies the exact middle of the crease where thigh folds into calf — a bladder-channel he-sea point and another of tradition’s command points, this one attached in classical teaching to the lower back; when backs complained, old practice famously looked to this crease. Lower still, chengshan (BL57) rests in the hollow beneath the twin bellies of the calf muscle. Its characters picture supporting a mountain: the rounded bellies were likened to a hill’s shoulders and the dip below them to its valley — a name the calf redeems every time it bears the body uphill.

四Part 4

Everyday Complaints Tradition Brought Here

What did older practice bring to these addresses? Digestion leads the list. Both the stomach and spleen channels descend the lower leg, and their points below the knee — zusanli above all — drew centuries of attention wherever appetite, fullness after meals, or flagging energy entered the room. Framed as tradition rather than trial result: these were the addresses classical practice reached for when the belly complained, the way a household knows which cupboard holds the familiar remedies.

Backs and legs form the second cluster. Long days upright sent people looking toward the knee crease, and classical teaching made weizhong the address of choice for the strained lower back. Walkers have always known the knot that settles into the calf near chengshan after hills or stairs, and yinlingquan joined the conversation whenever heaviness or puffiness set the theme — a spring-water name suited to the image of stagnant fluid finally finding a drain. None of this amounts to a modern claim; it is a record of where attention habitually went.

Women’s health forms the third cluster, and here sanyinjiao dominates. Menstrual discomfort, unsettled cycles, fertility worries — older texts return to the three-yin crossing again and again for such topics, usually alongside points elsewhere on the body. Modern readers can take the pattern for what it is: a historical habit of attention, coherent within its own medicine, not a verified protocol. Threaded through all three clusters runs the plainest entry of all — the tired leg itself, which needs no doctrine to justify a little quiet attention.

五Part 5

Fitting Self-Touch to Muscle and Bone

Self-applied touch works best here when it respects the leg’s engineering. The thigh’s deep bulk shrugs off fingertip probing; a flat palm or the heel of the hand resting its own weight reaches what fingers cannot. Along the shin, work the muscle bordering the bone rather than the bone itself — the sharp front crest is all periosteum and protest, and nothing there is worth winning. At the knee, where tendon crowds upon tendon, let the fingertips map lightly instead of pressing firmly.

Two holds suit beginners well. Sit and cross one ankle over the opposite knee so the calf hangs loose: a patient fingertip settled into the hollow beneath chengshan’s muscle bellies, held while the breath stays easy, is one of the gentler pleasures this literature offers. Alternatively, find the soft spot a little below the inner knee where yinlingquan hides against the shinbone’s back edge, and rest a fingertip there with barely any force. The house technique applies everywhere: pressure that remains comfortable, several slow breaths while you wait, a gradual release — short, frequent sessions beat occasional heroic efforts.

Boundaries matter more than techniques. The hollow behind the knee carries vessels and a nerve bundle; touch there, if at all, feather-light and briefly — never press into it. Give varicose veins, broken or inflamed skin, and numb patches a wide berth. A calf that turns red, swollen, warm, or newly painful without a clear cause needs medical assessment before any pressure, however gentle. And pregnancy redraws the map: set sanyinjiao aside until after the birth, and check any routine with a qualified practitioner first.

六Part 6

What Self-Care Here Can Honestly Offer

Strip away the doctrine and what remains is a ritual of attention: sitting down at the day’s end, moving slowly from knee to ankle, noticing where the leg actually lives. The pause has value even before any point is pressed. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and the evidence tied to any single point stays thin — the fair summary is comfort practice, not therapy, and nothing on this page should be read as promising relief.

It helps to keep the frames separate. As heritage, the leg’s six channels and their five flagged points amount to a coherent old way of organising the body, worth understanding on its own terms. As self-care, gentle touch along familiar lines is a low-cost wind-down some people genuinely enjoy. As medicine, none of it replaces assessment — a complaint that persists, worsens, or frightens belongs with a clinician whatever the wellness calendar suggests. Holding all three frames at once remains the most realistic stance.

七Part 7

Red Flags and Sensible Boundaries

Some situations belong promptly with a doctor, and the leg keeps its own list. After a fall or twist, swelling together with an inability to bear weight calls for assessment. A calf that is red, swollen, warm, or painful without explanation — especially on one side only — should be seen quickly, because clots are exactly the worry such signs exist to catch. Spreading numbness or weakness, ulcers that refuse to close, sudden coldness or colour change in a foot, and pain that wakes you at night all deserve professional eyes sooner rather than later.

Between emergencies and everyday wear lies chronic territory — circulation problems, diabetes with numbed feet, recurring cramps, stubborn low-back trouble sending symptoms down the leg — where licensed practitioners, including registered acupuncturists and East Asian medicine clinicians, can add channel-informed perspectives alongside conventional care. Bring your questions and your medication list, and expect coordination rather than replacement. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

限Self-care limits

Educational content only. Self-acupressure uses hands and fingers, never needles. Keep pressure gentle and comfortable. Avoid broken or inflamed skin, varicose veins, and the hollow behind the knee; touch there lightly at most. Stop immediately on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. Set sanyinjiao (SP6) aside during pregnancy unless a qualified practitioner advises otherwise. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

問答Frequently asked

問Is pressing leg points at home safe?
答For most healthy adults, light fingertip and palm work on the thigh, the calf, and around — never inside — the hollow behind the knee is a gentle practice. Keep pressure comfortable, avoid varicose veins and broken skin, and stop on sharp or radiating sensation. Pregnant readers should skip sanyinjiao (SP6) and check routines with a qualified practitioner; anyone on blood-thinning medication or managing a circulation condition should do the same.
問My thighs are solid muscle — how do I reach anything usefully?
答You may be aiming too precisely. Deep muscle mass answers broad contact far better than a probing thumb: rest the heel of your hand or a relaxed flat palm on the thigh and lean in gently, letting weight rather than force do the work. Save fingertip precision for the bony landmarks — the shin’s edges, the crease behind the knee, the hollows of the calf — where structures sit close beneath the surface.
問Why does tradition put digestive points on the leg, so far from the stomach?
答Because the classical system connected the body along channel lines rather than nerve paths. A channel was conceived as one continuous route from surface to organ, so points far downstream were read as addresses with influence upstream; zusanli’s standing as a digestive support grew from exactly that logic. It is a different model of the body from the anatomical one — best understood on its own terms rather than judged by laboratory rules.
問Which leg point should I avoid during pregnancy?
答Sanyinjiao (SP6), the three-yin crossing on the inner leg. The catalogue records it as contraindicated in pregnancy — tradition associates the point with strong downward-moving qualities, historically linked to labour — so custom sets it aside until after the birth. The other points named here, such as zusanli (ST36) and weizhong (BL40), are generally treated as acceptable with light pressure, though confirming any plan with a qualified practitioner is the sensible default throughout pregnancy.
問How often is it sensible to practise these holds?
答Consistency beats intensity. A minute or two of comfortable pressure at a chosen point on most days is a reasonable rhythm for healthy adults; if a spot feels bruised the next day, go lighter or less often. There is no quota to meet, and no evidence favours firmness over patience — and if an evening passes without the ritual, nothing is owed.
問Where exactly are these five points?
答Rough landmarks first: zusanli (ST36) below the outer knee beside the shinbone; yinlingquan (SP9) below the inner knee in a soft hollow; sanyinjiao (SP6) four finger-widths above the inner ankle bone; weizhong (BL40) centred in the crease behind the knee; chengshan (BL57) in the dip beneath the calf’s twin bellies. Exact locations, self-palpation steps, and recorded cautions live on each point’s own catalogue pages, rendered straight from the registry — this overview deliberately leaves measurement to them.
