Shoulder

The shoulder spans the upper trapezius, the joint itself, and the tissue that connects neck to arm; its points are used for shoulder pain, restricted movement, and upper-body tension.

The shoulder holds 4 of the points filed on this site: Jianjing (GB21), Jianyu (LI15), Jianliao (TH14) and Tianzong (SI11).

慎Leave the hollows above the collarbone and the armpit alone. If you are pregnant, skip this region, Jianjing (GB21) above all.

慎Before you press

  • Use fingertips and light, comfortable pressure.
  • Keep off broken, inflamed or numb skin.
  • If you are pregnant, skip Hegu (LI4) and Sanyinjiao (SP6).
  • Pain that is new, severe or lasting belongs with a licensed practitioner.

    要 · In short

    The shoulder is where the neck hands you over to the arm: a cape of muscle, a shelf of bone, and the body’s most mobile joint, crowded with classical points. Four channels crest here, and this page tours the ground they share.

    一Part 1

    A Crossroads Between Neck and Arm

    Every classical map of East Asian medicine needs its junctions, and the shoulder is one of the busiest. The single character that names it in Chinese, jian (肩), joins the glyph for a door leaf to the flesh radical — a hinged panel of muscle hung where neck becomes arm, as the composition itself suggests. Onto this small stage the old geography crowded an unusual amount of traffic. Three channels of the hand ascend the arm and cross the shoulder on their way inward — those tradition groups as the hand yangming large intestine channel, the hand taiyang small intestine channel, and the hand shaoyang san jiao channel — while the foot shaoyang gallbladder channel descends from beside the neck and rides across the top of the shoulder. Where four lines converge, points accumulate; the region holds some of the most frequently discussed addresses in the whole corpus.

    This page is a regional companion to the individual point pages. It walks the landscape in plain anatomy, introduces the stations the classics placed here along with the channels each belongs to, and gathers self-care ideas suited to a part of the body you can reach with your own opposite hand. It is educational material, not medical advice, and it teaches no diagnostic or therapeutic technique. Wording carries weight: “traditionally used for” and “classically associated with” report what historical practice reached for, not findings about outcomes. Codes, names, exact locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions render everywhere from the registry spine; the prose below adds story and context alone.

    二Part 2

    The Lay of the Shoulder Landscape

    Begin with what a fingertip finds. Across the top of the joint runs a small bony shelf — the acromion — continuous toward the front with the collarbone, which slopes down to a soft hollow at the base of the throat-side neck. Behind, a broad triangular plate, the shoulder blade, glides over the ribs as the arm moves; its upper outer corner completes the socket the arm swings in. Draped over all of it lies the trapezius, a cape of muscle rising from the skull’s midline and spreading to the point of the shoulder, and capping the joint itself sits the deltoid, the rounded pad that gives the shoulder its silhouette. Every named address in this region lives on or between those landmarks.

    Why did tradition settle so many points into so small a territory? Partly because the region is a working hinge: the body asks it to combine remarkable freedom of movement with steady carrying load, day after day, and old writers read the consequences in their own vocabulary — cold settling into the tissue as stiffness, strain as stagnation of qi and blood, wear presenting as aching that worsens with lifting. Partly, too, because the converging channels made it a natural meeting ground where lines bound for head, chest, and neck could be addressed from one spot. Classical texts describe the area’s points again and again around pain, restricted raising, and heaviness — themes you will recognise in every portfolio below.

    三Part 3

    Where Four Channels Meet: Points of the Region

    Take the front first. Jianyu (LI15) belongs to the large intestine channel, and classical location places it in the depression below the acromion’s front edge that appears when the arm is raised — a spot so characteristic that locating it doubles as a small test of the joint. Its recorded associations centre on contracture and pain of the shoulder and arm. Immediately behind it, in the next hollow below the shelf, sits jianliao (TH14) of the san jiao channel, whose record speaks of heavy shoulders and arms that will not lift. Slightly inward, jugu (LI16) — large intestine channel again — occupies the depression between the collarbone’s outer end and the ridge of the shoulder blade, with shoulder-raising difficulty filed among its classical uses.

    Around the back, the small intestine channel climbs the rear of the shoulder in steps. Jianzhen (SI9) waits just above the fold at the back of the armpit, one cun up when the arm hangs relaxed; naoshu (SI10) nestles beneath the lower border of the shoulder-blade ridge; and tianzong (SI11), perhaps the best-loved of the three, rests at the centre of the blade’s flat surface, classically reached for around scapular and shoulder-back pain. From above, the gallbladder channel sends down jianjing (GB21), placed halfway along the line from the prominent bone at the neck’s base to the tip of the shoulder. Its record runs from stiff painful neck through painful obstruction of the shoulder and back — and reaches, notably, into the events of childbirth, a fact that shapes one firm rule later on this page.

    A shared title binds several of these addresses together: jianyu, jugu, naoshu, and jianjing all carry the crossing-point label in the dataset — junctions where the maps drew more than one channel overlapping. That is as neat a picture of the region as any: not a row of unrelated dots but a knot of intersecting routes, each point page holding the precise location and recorded cautions this tour deliberately leaves to them.

    四Part 4

    Everyday Complaints the Tradition Brought Here

    Modern life keeps re-filing its grievances under this same geography. Hours at a laptop gather tension across the trapezius until the neck feels bolted to the shoulders; a heavy bag rides on exactly the strap-line where jianjing (GB21) sits; a draught from a window earns blame for a morning of stiffness; sleeping on one side leaves the underneath shoulder complaining into the afternoon. Classical practice met such troubles with the addresses above — reaching for them around painful obstruction, stiffness after exposure to cold and wind, and arms slow to raise. Presented as history, that pattern explains why these remain among the most familiar names in everyday wellness writing about the upper body.

    What can pressing them honestly offer? Candour comes first. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence tied specifically to this neighbourhood is no thicker than anywhere else. What remains is quieter but real: a few unhurried minutes of warm contact, slower breathing, and attention paid to a part of the body that carries quietly all day. This page makes no efficacy claims, and nothing here should postpone assessment for a complaint that deserves one.

    五Part 5

    Self-Applied Touch for a Two-Handed Region

    The shoulder has one great advantage for home practice: your opposite hand already knows how to hold it. Warm the palm first, then lay it across the far trapezius and draw a few slow arcs from the neck’s side out to the point of the shoulder — the sweep people make instinctively when a day has been long. Follow with small circles over the fleshy cape, letting pressure stay light enough that the muscle never braces against you. Move with the exhale rather than the clock, and give both sides their turn even when only one complains.

    When you want particular destinations, three forgive beginners easily. Tianzong (SI11) is awkward to reach alone and effortless with a partner’s thumb resting flat at the centre of the shoulder blade. Jianyu (LI15) and jianliao (TH14) sit close together on the joint’s rounded cap; settle a fingertip in either hollow and let the arm hang loose while you breathe. Jianzhen (SI9) hides behind, just above the rear armpit fold, easiest to find with the arm relaxed at your side. In every case the style is patient: contact first, only comfortable pressure, several slow breaths of company, release by degrees.

    Boundaries keep the practice kind. The soft hollows immediately above the collarbone, where the neck roots into the chest, take warmth only — a resting palm at most, no probing fingers — and the armpit hollow stays untouched altogether. Skip broken or irritated skin, sharp bony edges, and any joint that is hot, swollen, or freshly injured. If you are pregnant, leave jianjing (GB21) entirely alone unless a qualified practitioner directs otherwise; the tradition’s own links between that point and childbirth are reason enough. Anyone pregnant, managing a health condition, or taking medication that affects bleeding should ask a qualified practitioner before adding new routines. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation ends the session on the spot.

    六Part 6

    Red Flags, and What Self-Care Cannot Do

    Some situations belong promptly with professional assessment, before any self-care is tried. A shoulder injured in a fall or accident; an arm that suddenly will not lift; numbness, tingling, or weakness running down toward the hand; pain accompanied by fever; a joint that looks deformed or feels hot and swollen; shoulder discomfort arriving together with breathlessness or chest symptoms — each of these calls for medical evaluation straight away. So does anything that simply frightens you. Urgency is information, and it deserves a professional audience rather than a massage routine.

    Between emergencies and ordinary tiredness lies the wide middle country of persistent trouble: the shoulder that has stiffened over months, the ache that outlasts every rest, the rotation that no longer reaches. These belong with licensed practitioners — doctors, physiotherapists, registered acupuncturists, and East Asian medicine clinicians can each offer channel-informed perspectives alongside conventional care, working with your regular clinicians rather than instead of them. And if you want the fine print behind this tour, the individual pages carry exact locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

    限Self-care limits

    Educational content only. Self-acupressure means fingertips or a soft palm — never needles or tools. Keep pressure gentle and stay on muscle rather than bony edges. Leave the hollows above the collarbone and the armpit untouched. During pregnancy, skip everything here unless a qualified practitioner directs otherwise — jianjing (GB21) especially. Stop immediately on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. Self-acupressure is educational self-care, not medical care. Stop if a sensation becomes sharp, and seek professional help for persistent symptoms.

    問答Frequently asked

    問Is it safe to work on my own shoulder at home?
    答For most healthy adults, gentle fingertip or flat-palm contact over the muscle is a mild practice. Stay off the hollows above the collarbone and out of the armpit, avoid hot or swollen joints and broken skin, and stop at once on sharp or electric sensation. Check with a qualified practitioner first if you are pregnant, manage a health condition, or take medication that affects bleeding.
    問Which point in this region suits a beginner best?
    答Jianyu (LI15) is the friendliest start: lift the arm, watch the front hollow below the acromion appear, let the arm drop, and rest a fingertip there with light pressure. Tianzong (SI11) rewards partners — flat thumb contact at the centre of the shoulder blade. In both spots the rule is comfort first, patience second, force never.
    問Why does jianjing (GB21) need special care?
    答Two reasons live on its own record. First, the classical portfolio ties the point to difficult labour and retention of the placenta, which is why anyone pregnant skips it entirely unless a practitioner directs otherwise. Second, it sits on soft ground between the neck’s base and the shoulder tip — terrain treated with respect in the dataset cautions — so home touch there stays feather-light at most.
    問My shoulder clicks and aches after long desk days — will pressing points fix it?
    答No honest answer fits inside a promise. Gentle pressure over the trapezius is a comfort ritual some people find genuinely settling, and regular breaks to stand, roll the shoulders, and move likely matter as much as any single point. Persistent or worsening trouble belongs with proper assessment; self-care accompanies good care, never substitutes for it.
    問How many points actually sit in this small region?
    答This page names seven: jianyu (LI15) and jugu (LI16) of the large intestine channel, jianliao (TH14) of the san jiao channel, jianzhen (SI9), naoshu (SI10), and tianzong (SI11) of the small intestine channel, and jianjing (GB21) of the gallbladder channel. Draw the boundary differently and the count shifts — neighbouring channels keep further addresses nearby — which is precisely why each point page exists.
