Taichong, the Grand Surge, rests on the top of the foot in the hollow where the two long bones behind the first and second toes meet. Tradition treats it as the pool where the liver channel’s vitality gathers, and classical practice reached for it amid headaches, dizziness, and a short temper. This guide covers finding it, reading its name, and pressing it kindly.

## Where Taichong Sits on the Foot {#where-it-sits}

The dataset describes Taichong plainly: on the dorsum of the foot, in the depression distal to the junction of the first and second metatarsal bones. Unpacked, that reads friendlier than it sounds. The dorsum is simply the top of the foot — the surface you see glancing down while standing. Behind each toe runs a long slender bone, a metatarsal; the first backs the big toe and the second sits beside it. Farther up the foot those two shafts angle toward one another and join at a firm bony junction, and distal means on the toe side of it. Taichong occupies the shallow valley between the two shafts, right where the valley deepens into a hollow below the junction. Unlike many channel points recorded in proportional body-measures, this one asks no arithmetic at all: it is located entirely by feel.

What waits under the fingertips is modest terrain, and that is part of the point’s charm. The valley is floored with soft tissue; slender cords, the tendons that lift the toes, fan outward toward the web. Press gently and the two bone walls answer on either side of the finger, firm and unmistakable. One honest observation belongs here: an artery crosses the upper part of this corridor, and some people notice a soft beat under a resting fingertip. The beat is a landmark, not a target — the traditional location sits in the hollow, and if a pulse thuds strongly under pressure, the kindest response is to ease off a little and let the fingers drift toward the junction until the beat quiets.

Two neighbours help fix the position. Nearer the toes, just behind the web margin between the first and second toes, lies Xingjian (LV2); Taichong continues the same valley one stretch farther back, at the place the shafts merge. On the outer side of the big toe’s tip, beside the corner of its toenail, sits Dadun (LV1), where the whole liver channel takes its start. Read together, the sequence tells a small story: the channel emerges at the toe tip, slips between the toes, and gathers strength in the Taichong hollow before climbing the leg — fourteen named points in all carry it from the foot to the ribs.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit somewhere comfortable with the foot free: resting flat on the floor, propped on a low stool, or crossed over the opposite knee, whichever lets the top of the foot relax. Lay the index finger of the opposite hand in the web between the big toe and the second toe. Now slide slowly backward, toward the ankle, keeping the fingertip in the valley between the two bone shafts. The finger rides a gentle uphill slope; after a stretch the walls widen, rise, and fuse into a solid bony ridge. Settle into the soft hollow immediately on the toe side of that ridge — that hollow, between the shafts, is Taichong. Repeat the search on the other foot so both sides receive equal attention.

A few quiet confirmations earn trust in the placement. Rest the fingertip feather-light first and compare: many people notice a faint dull awareness at the true hollow and little at all a finger-width away. Wiggle the toes slowly and feel the slight shift of tissue under the pad. Explore the walls on either side — firm bone should greet the fingertip left and right, confirming the valley rather than the soft middle of the instep. And check the neighbourhood: the hollow sits clearly above the web margin yet well before the crease at the front of the ankle, midway along the valley’s course. Even when the placement feels certain, give the second foot its own patient search rather than assuming symmetry.

Three missteps account for most misses. Stopping early leaves the finger near the web, on ground closer to Xingjian than to Taichong; the true hollow lies noticeably farther back, at the bony junction. Drifting sideways lands on the springy middle of the instep, where no bone walls bracket the finger; returning to the web and re-entering the valley corrects it. Overshooting runs past the junction toward the ankle, where the valley narrows and the landmarks thin out. None of these matter much — the search costs nothing and repeats happily. Find the web, walk the valley, stop where the bones meet: the whole instruction fits in one breath.

## Reading the Name: Grand Surge {#name-and-meaning}

The name arrives loaded. Tai 太 is the superlative: grand, supreme, the greatest degree of a thing, as in the great sun or the great mountain. Chong 衝 pictures a surge — water rushing forward with force — and by extension a main road, a thoroughfare traffic pours along. Joined, Taichong reads as the grand surge, or the supreme thoroughfare. Classical commentators took the name at face value: the liver channel’s arrival here was understood to be plentiful, a gathering swell pooling in this hollow before the climb up the leg. A point named for abundance invites a reader to picture exactly that — not a trickle passing through, but a full current collecting.

The tradition also files Taichong under two formal titles, and both repay a look. It is the shu-stream point of the channel — in the classical series of five transport points, the stage where qi, having bubbled up small at the toes, broadens and flows steadily like a stream. And it is the yuan-source point, the one station on each of the twelve regular channels where the organ’s own vitality was thought to gather closest to the surface; every channel keeps exactly one, and the liver’s is here. In the five-phase sorting the tradition applies to the points, the stream position of a wood-element channel counts as wood itself, so the liver meets its own nature at this spot. Titles aside, the practical upshot in classical eyes was simple: among fourteen stations, this is where the organ shows itself most openly.

Pinyin writes the name Tàichōng: tai falls away on a fourth tone, chong rides a level first tone. The hanzi pair 太衝 is spare enough to hold in mind at a glance. Classical teaching also kept company for this point: it was regularly discussed alongside Hegu (LI4) on the back of the hand, and the pair together carried the nickname the Four Gates — doors through which qi and blood were encouraged to keep moving. The imagery suits a source point on the body’s busiest little highway. Names in this literature are rarely decoration; they compress the teaching into two syllables and hand it forward.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The licensed record attaches a long portfolio to this point: headache, dizziness, and red painful eyes; facial paralysis, stroke, epilepsy, and infantile convulsions; jaundice, hypochondriac pain, vomiting, and abdominal distension; irregular menstruation, painful periods, and absent periods; bedwetting; difficulty walking; swelling and pain of the foot. Seventeen entries read like a crowd, but the traditional reasoning sorts them neatly. Start at the top of the body: the liver channel sets out from the big toe and climbs the inner leg to the ribside, and classical teaching extended its reach onward to the head and the eyes. Classical physiology held that when the liver’s activity rose upward too eagerly, the head paid — throbbing headache, swimming dizziness, eyes that burned red. Practitioners of the tradition therefore consulted points along that ascending route, and as the channel’s source point, Taichong stood near the head of such discussions.

The middle cluster — facial paralysis, stroke, epilepsy, and infantile convulsions — shares an older logic. Conditions that arrive suddenly, shake, stiffen, or paralyse were gathered under the imagery of internal wind, and the liver was wind’s traditional address in the body; settling that wind belonged to this channel’s art, with its source point among the first considered. The digestive set follows a different thread. The channel’s course passes the ribside, and tradition assigned the liver a governing hand over the free movement of qi; when that movement snagged, the flank ached, the stomach rebelled, the abdomen swelled, and bile — the gallbladder being the liver’s traditional partner — lost its easy flow, which the old books read into jaundice. Each link is doctrine, not experiment; the coherence is what kept the point central inside the system.

The remaining entries gather lower and later in life. Tradition made the liver the storehouse of blood and the steward of its smooth flow, so women’s cycles fell largely within its keeping: irregular menstruation, painful periods, and periods that failed to arrive were all brought to liver-channel points, and the source point carried particular weight. Bedwetting connected through the channel’s passage across the genital region, and difficulty walking and a swollen, painful foot were simply local matters, treated where they lived. It bears saying plainly: these are recorded traditional uses, preserved for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point relieves illness. Modern research on self-applied pressure is still young; reviews to date suggest effects, where seen, are modest and shaped by context, and evidence for any single point remains sparse. Hold the tradition warmly, and hold the boundaries firmly.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the hollow confirmed, settle in: seated, foot supported, shoulders easy. Rest the pad of a thumb or index finger in the hollow — pads, never nails, knuckles, or tools. Begin with contact alone and a few slow breaths, letting the foot grow accustomed to being noticed. Then add small, unhurried circles, allowing the skin to glide over the tissue beneath instead of dragging across it. Aim for a pleasant dullness, a mild spreading warmth, or a faint achiness that loosens rather than bites; sharpness is the signal to lighten or stop. A workable home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice a day, swapping hands or sides so neither foot becomes the neglected one.

Circumstance shapes the session more than technique does. Warm hands on a warm foot teach the tissue to soften; cold fingers make everything guard. A quiet room beats a glowing screen, because the practice doubles as a pause: exhale slowly into each circle, and let attention settle from the head down to the feet — a descent the tradition would recognise, since easing an overeager upward surge was always this point’s poetic job. People who sustain the habit usually attach it to an existing moment: after washing the feet at night, during the evening wind-down, beside a cup of tea taken without a phone. Regularity, not intensity, is the entire secret, and five unhurried evenings outweigh one forceful campaign.

Keep expectations sized to the act. Across a few weeks you may notice the hollow feeling less tender, the evenings ending a shade calmer, or nothing at all except ninety seconds of attention spent on your own body — and all of those count as honest outcomes. What does not fit is escalation: pressing harder, lingering longer, or hunting for stronger sensations in order to force a result. Self-acupressure is companionship for the body, not leverage against it. When the urge to push arrives, treat it as information, choose gentleness, or simply close the session for the day and let the practice resume tomorrow.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

The licensed dataset records no special cautions for Taichong — and the absence deserves a careful read. It reflects forgiving geography: the top of the foot is soft country, far from lungs and internal organs, where gentle surface pressure has little to disturb. Absence of warnings is not, however, an invitation to force. Everything kind about this location depends on staying superficial; the practice lives in skin and the cushion beneath it, and the moment pressure aims downward through the hollow toward the bones, it stops being the practice this page describes. Light is not the timid version of the technique. Light is the technique.

The working rules fit on one hand, as it were. Use finger pads only — no nails, knuckles, elbows, or gadgets. Never press through pain, and never press while bracing or holding the breath. Skip the point over cuts, blisters, insect bites, fungal irritation, or any broken skin of the foot. Set the whole practice aside during a feverish illness or an acute flare, and go gently, if at all, after a foot or ankle injury until ordinary walking feels normal again. If a strong pulse thuds under the fingers, ease toward the junction and lighten. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation ends the session for the day. Pregnant readers should check with a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice, and anyone with reduced sensation in the feet, poor circulation, or prescribed blood-thinning medication does well to ask a clinician first.

Some symptoms outrank any self-care routine outright. A sudden headache unlike any before, headache with fever and a stiff neck, dizziness with fainting, slurred speech, or weakness on one side, sudden disturbance of vision, or any symptom after a blow to the head call for urgent medical care — not a pressure session. Persistent or worsening headaches, recurring dizziness, unusual pelvic pain, bleeding that is unusually heavy or prolonged, or a missed period with any chance of pregnancy belong promptly with a clinician as well. This page is educational only; acupressure is self-care, never diagnosis or therapy. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint. The surge imagery is stirring, but the surest gift Taichong offers most households is a calmer evening and a better acquaintance with one’s own two feet, met kindly and pressed lightly.
