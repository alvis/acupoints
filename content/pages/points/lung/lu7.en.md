LU7 — Lieque, seventh point of the Lung channel — sits in a shallow cleft on the thumb-side edge of the forearm, just above the wrist crease. Classical practice reached for it when coughs, sore swollen throats, headaches, or stiff necks flared. This guide walks through finding the point, reading its name, understanding its traditional roles, and pressing it safely at home.

## Where Lieque Lives on the Forearm {#where-lieque-lives}

Turn your palm toward you and look at the thumb side of your wrist. Just above the crease where the hand meets the forearm rises a rounded bony bump — the styloid process of the radius. Lieque sits on the outer, radial border of the forearm immediately above this landmark, roughly a thumb-width and a half up from the crease. Textbooks place it in the groove between two cord-like structures: the fleshy brachioradialis muscle toward the outside and the slender tendon of abductor pollicis longus closer to the palm.

The groove is easiest to feel when you spread your thumb away from the palm; the abductor tendon stands out like a bowstring, and the hollow beside it deepens. Slide your fingertip up the bone edge until you find a natural dip just above the bump — most people notice a distinctly tender spot there when pressed. That combination of cleft plus mild ache is the practical signature practitioners look for before settling on this point rather than its neighbours.

## The Crossed-Hands Method {#crossed-hands-method}

Classical texts preserved a locating gesture so characteristic that commentators treat it as part of the point’s identity. Rest the hands loosely together with palms facing, and hook the webs between thumbs and index fingers into each other, one hand crossing over the back of the opposite wrist. The index finger of the upper hand now stretches naturally along the forearm, and its tip lands right beside the bony bump above the crease. Beneath that fingertip lies the small cleft of Lieque.

You can cross-check with a simple measure. In Chinese medicine the cun is a proportional unit taken from your own body, and the breadth of the index and middle fingers held side by side is traditionally taken as one and a half cun. Lay those two fingers directly above the wrist crease on the thumb side; the far edge of the middle finger marks the level where Lieque waits. When both methods agree — crossed-hands fingertip and two-finger measure landing on the same tender dip — you can be confident you have the right spot, and remember there is a mirror-image point on each wrist.

## A Name Built From Lightning and a Gap {#name-meaning}

The written name pairs 列 (liè), to split, divide, or set in sequence, with 缺 (quē), a gap, break, or fault. Read literally, Lieque suggests a broken sequence or a splitting gap — an image commentators first ground in anatomy: the point occupies a visible fissure in the tissue above the radial styloid, a place where the orderly line of the forearm seems to crack open. The crossed-hands gesture dramatises the same idea, because the gap only announces itself when the hands cross and the index finger points into it.

There is a second, older reading. Lieque appears in early Chinese literature as a poetic compound for lightning — the flash that tears open a bank of storm clouds. Classical commentators carried that image into the point: lightning arrives suddenly and splits the sky, and the name hints at swift movement along the channel rather than promising any particular outcome. A third thread ties the name to function: as the Lung channel’s connecting point, Lieque is exactly where a branch vessel splits off the main ordered flow toward the paired Large Intestine channel — the sequence, quite literally, breaking here.

## Two Special Roles: Connecting Point and Eight Confluent Point {#two-special-roles}

Every channel carries one luo-connecting point, the place where a network vessel departs to link the channel with its interior-exterior partner. For the Lung channel that departure happens at Lieque, joining it to the Large Intestine channel, whose classical pathway climbs the arm and traces the lower teeth, cheek, nose, and shoulder region. Traditional reasoning therefore treats Lieque as a bridge: a single wrist point through which practice can reach both respiratory complaints on the lung side and head-and-face complaints on the yangming side.

Lieque also belongs to the eight confluent points, a select group classically matched to the extraordinary vessels. Its assignment is the ren vessel — the conception vessel that runs up the body’s front midline through chest and throat. In formula tradition Lieque is paired with Zhaohai, a point below the inner ankle that opens the yin heel vessel, the two together addressing throat and chest complaints. Between the connecting link and the confluent pairing, one modest wrist point acquired an outsized role in classical prescriptions — which is why commentaries return to it so often despite its unassuming location.

## What Classical Practice Reached For {#traditional-indications}

The recorded actions cluster into three circles. The innermost is respiratory: cough and asthma, the bread-and-butter territory of the Lung channel itself. Within the traditional framework, Lieque is described as releasing the exterior and diffusing lung qi, and classical practice reaches for it early in wind-type patterns — the scratchy throat, chilliness, and neck tightness that accompany an ordinary cold. Framed plainly, these are traditional explanations drawn from the channel system, not claims about biological mechanism.

The second circle is head, face, and throat: sore and swollen throat, headache, toothache, stiff neck, and facial paralysis. The connecting link helps explain why a wrist point would address toothache or facial paralysis — those territories belong to the paired Large Intestine channel — while the ren-vessel pairing reinforces the throat focus. The third circle is simply local: the point sits over the radial wrist, and classical lists record it for wrist pain, the way many points are used for discomfort where they lie.

How much weight does modern evidence carry here? Reviews of acupressure research suggest possible short-term comfort benefits in some contexts, but findings remain mixed, and direct research at this specific point is limited. It is fairest to hold the traditional list as a map of what practitioners historically selected the point for, while treating home acupressure as low-risk comfort care rather than treatment — and persistent symptoms as a signal to see a licensed practitioner.

## Self-Care Acupressure: A Quiet Ritual at the Wrist {#self-care-acupressure}

Settle somewhere warm and upright. Locate the point with whichever method suits you — crossed hands, or two fingers above the crease — then rest the pad of the opposite thumb in the groove. Angle gently toward the cleft beside the bone rather than digging straight down; the tissues here are thin, and the nerves and vessels of the radial wrist run close beneath the skin. Aim for pressure you would rate around three or four out of ten: clearly present, comfortably achy, never sharp.

Give the pressure a rhythm instead of clamping down. Sink in as you exhale, hold for the length of a slow breath, ease off, then repeat — roughly ten breath-cycles to a round. Some people prefer small slow circles over steady pressure; both are fine, so choose the one that lets your shoulders stay relaxed. After three to five rounds on one wrist, switch sides and repeat. Once or twice a day is plenty for self-care; useful moments include the first tickle of a sore throat, the build-up of a tension headache, or stiffness after a long stretch at a desk.

Warm hands make gentler instruments: rub your palms together first if they are cold, and keep the supporting wrist loose so the pressure stays responsive rather than rigid. Expect sensations to travel — a spreading warmth down the thumb side, or a softening around the throat area, are commonly reported feelings. None of these are required for the practice to be worthwhile; quiet consistency matters more than dramatic sensation.

## Cautions and Sensible Limits {#cautions-and-limits}

The catalogue entry records no specific cautions for Lieque, and none are needed to justify ordinary care. Apply the universal rules regardless: stop immediately on any sharp, shooting, or electric sensation; do not press over broken skin, rashes, sunburn, or healing scars; and if you bruise easily or take blood-thinning medication, keep the touch featherlight or skip it altogether. Because the superficial branch of the radial nerve and nearby vessels sit close to the surface here, deeper pressing gains nothing and invites an unpleasant zing — comfort-level pressure is the whole game.

Some readers should check with a professional first. If you are pregnant, the dataset records no pregnancy-specific contraindication for this point, yet many licensed practitioners still prefer gentler protocols during pregnancy, so ask before making it a habit. Children respond well to lighter, shorter contact. Recent wrist fractures, sprains, or inflammatory flare-ups in the area deserve time to heal before any pressing. And red flags always outrank self-care: high fever, severe breathlessness, sudden one-sided facial weakness, or a severe new headache belong with urgent medical care, not with acupressure.

Keep expectations proportionate. Acupressure at Lieque is best understood as a small, low-risk ritual of attention — a way to pause, breathe, and care for an irritated throat or tired neck while the underlying process runs its course or while professional care takes over. Persistent or worsening symptoms call for a licensed practitioner; nothing on this page replaces diagnosis or individualised advice.
