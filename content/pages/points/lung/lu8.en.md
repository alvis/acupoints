Along the thumb side of the forearm, just above the wrist crease, LU8 rests in a slim groove beside the pulse — the lung channel’s jing-river point, traditionally reached for cough, chest discomfort, and sore throats, and easy to explore at home with a gentle fingertip.

## Finding LU8 on your own wrist {#where-the-point-lives}

The catalogue places LU8 on the radial aspect of the palmar surface of the forearm — the thumb side you see when the palm faces up — in the depression between the styloid process of the radius and the radial artery, one cun above the wrist crease. Turned into everyday landmarks: flip your palm upward, find the crease where hand meets forearm, then travel one thumb-width up the arm along the thumb-side edge, toward the elbow.

Under the fingertip you will meet a small bony prominence: the styloid process of the radius, the rounded flare at the wrist end of the forearm’s larger bone. Tucked between that prominence and the cord of the radial artery — the vessel where most people can feel their pulse — lies a soft groove. That hollow, hugging the pulse line, is the neighbourhood of Jingqu. Anatomy varies from person to person, so expect the exact hollow to sit a few millimetres this way or that; the cun is a proportional measure based on your own thumb, not a fixed ruler.

Two textures confirm the address: rhythmic pulsing felt beside or beneath the fingertip, and the firm resistance of bone on the groove’s other side. Pulse plus bone together mean you have arrived; if you feel neither, drift back toward the thumb side of the wrist and search again.

## Why “channel ditch”: reading the name Jingqu {#channel-ditch-name}

The pinyin name Jīngqú joins two characters: 經 (jīng), the very character that names the body’s channels, and 渠 (qú), a ditch or canal — a waterway cut to guide flow where it is wanted. Read together, the classical commentators picture a narrow channel carved through firm ground: a small, clearly bounded watercourse that carries whatever travels along it with unusual directness.

The image suits the point’s traditional job. Early texts describe the transport points of each limb channel as stages of moving water — a well bubbling from depth, a spring seeping thin, a stream pooling, a river running swift, and at last a sea where flow gathers deepest. Jingqu holds the lung channel’s river stage. Where a river runs between banks its current quickens and its course narrows, and the old teaching reads the ground beneath the radial styloid the same way: qi passing through a tighter passage, close to the surface, reachable with the lightest touch.

The classical scheme also gives each river-stage point an elemental colour, and Jingqu is traditionally matched with the metal phase — the same phase as the lung channel itself, which is why older manuals call it the point where the channel meets its own nature. These are teaching images rather than laboratory findings; they shaped how practitioners reasoned about point selection, and they explain why so small a patch of forearm gathered so much commentary.

## What the tradition reaches for here {#traditional-indications}

The recorded indications cluster around the chest and throat: cough, asthma, chest pain, sore and swollen throat, joined by wrist pain. Classical practice tends to reach for river-stage points when a complaint seems to lodge along the channel’s course rather than at its far end, and the lung channel descends the inner arm before connecting upward to the lungs and throat — so a point near the wrist becomes a natural address for trouble felt higher up.

Say plainly what that list is and is not. It records traditional use: the situations in which generations of practitioners chose this point, handed down through manuals and classroom lineages. It is not a claim that pressing the point resolves asthma or any illness. Modern reviews of acupoint research suggest possible benefit in some contexts while remaining far from settled conclusions, and honest writing keeps the two registers apart.

How does a wrist point come to carry throat and chest indications at all? The channel map supplies the traditional logic. The lung pathway surfaces at the chest, runs down the inner arm, crosses the wrist, and ends at the thumb, so a wrist-level point stands on the same road the discomfort travels. Practitioners liken choosing a point along that road to servicing a route midway rather than only at its terminus — an image drawn, once again, from the tradition’s own habits of thought.

## Jingqu between its neighbours {#along-the-lung-channel}

On the lung channel’s eleven-point run from shoulder to thumbnail, Jingqu sits second from the wrist end: Lieque (LU7) lies just above it and Taiyuan (LU9) occupies the crease itself. The neighbours share turf — all three live on the thumb side of the forearm and all appear in classical lists for cough and throat complaints — yet their characters differ. Taiyuan, planted squarely on the crease and the pulse, carries the channel’s gathering duties; Lieque, higher and freer, is famed for its crossing toward the head and neck.

For the home explorer the practical lesson is simply that this stretch of forearm is crowded with named points separated by small distances. If your searching finger lands slightly above or below the hollow you first found, you remain in classical company. What marks out Jingqu is its address inside the groove beside the styloid process — nearer the bony prominence than crease-hugging Taiyuan — together with its river-stage role in the old scheme.

## Gentle self-acupressure, step by step {#self-care-acupressure}

Acupressure at Jingqu is a light-touch practice, and the local anatomy insists on it: the radial artery runs alongside. Sit comfortably, palm up, shoulders easy. Rest the fingertips of the opposite hand on the thumb side just above the wrist crease, find the bony prominence, then slide into the soft hollow between prominence and pulse. Feeling the heart’s rhythm beside your fingertip confirms the landmark.

Once located, soften everything. Traditional technique favours slow, small circles or a steady gentle hold rather than hard digging. Picture the weight of a coin resting on skin, increasing only to comfortable contact, never to pain. Many people pair the hold with slow breathing — four or five unhurried breaths, release, rest, then repeat once or twice if it feels good. Short sessions scattered through the day suit this point better than one long, forceful bout.

Because the point is small, precision matters more than strength. Should the fingertip wander onto the artery and the pulsing turn unpleasant, shift a few millimetres toward the bony side of the groove and begin again. Pressing here should never bruise; any tingling that shoots into the hand is a signal to lighten or stop, not to persist.

## Cautions: respecting the artery {#cautions-and-safety}

The dataset records a single caution for this entry, and it deserves emphasis even for readers who will never meet an acupuncture needle: LU8 lies immediately beside the radial artery, so any treatment aimed there must steer clear of the vessel — territory reserved for trained practitioners. For self-care, the same geography translates into gentler house rules: stay off the pulse line itself, favour the bony flank of the groove, and keep sessions brief.

Stop at once if pressure brings sharp pain, numbness, pins and needles, or light-headedness, and never massage broken skin, rashes, swelling, or a recent injury. Set the practice aside during a fever, and if you are pregnant, managing a circulatory condition, taking blood-thinning medication, or living with any diagnosed illness, ask a qualified practitioner before adding new pressure work. A cough that lingers, breathlessness, chest pain, or a sore throat that will not settle are all reasons to see a clinician — acupressure accompanies care; it never replaces assessment.

Above all, hold expectations lightly. This page exists to explain a tradition faithfully, not to promise outcomes. The fairest summary: Jingqu is a precisely located, historically rich point that people have pressed for centuries as one strand of a wider discipline — quietly interesting, low-risk when handled gently, and best received as a companion to everyday healthcare rather than a stand-in for it.
