Xiabai — the fourth point of the lung channel — rests high on the soft inner side of the upper arm. Classical practice reaches for it when cough, labored breathing, queasiness, or a worn-out arm calls for a calm touch. Learn to find it, read its name, and press it gently at home.

## First, the map: where LU4 sits along the lung channel {#channel-context}

The lung channel begins high on the chest and flows outward and downward along the front of the shoulder, then down the soft inner face of the upper arm, across the elbow crease, through the forearm, and out to the thumb. Its eleven classical points are numbered in the order the channel travels, so the low numbers live near the shoulder and armpit while the high numbers arrive at the wrist and hand. LU4 belongs to that quiet stretch of inner upper arm — above the busy elbow region marked by Chize (LU5) and below Tianfu (LU3), which nestles close to the armpit.

That placement matters for how the point is understood. Points on the upper reaches of a limb channel were traditionally read as speaking to the deeper trunk — the chest, the lungs, the diaphragm region — while still remaining reachable for purely local complaints such as a sore upper arm. Xiabai therefore wears two hats at once in classical listings: a distant helper for chest and breathing concerns, and a nearby one for the very muscles it rests against. Holding both readings makes the rest of this guide easier to follow.

## Finding Xiabai by touch, step by step {#finding-xiabai}

Sit comfortably and let your left arm hang loose or rest across your lap, palm turned forward. Look at the inner surface of the upper arm and squeeze gently: the rounded belly of the biceps rises under your fingers, carrying a firm ridge along its outer-facing edge on the thumb side. The catalogue locates Xiabai on the medial aspect of the arm, on the radial border of biceps brachii — in everyday terms, on the inner arm, right along that muscular edge.

To pin down the height, use the elbow crease as your anchor. Traditional measurement divides the inner arm from cubital crease to the front of the armpit into nine equal parts called cun — proportional units taken from your own body rather than from a ruler. Xiabai sits five of those parts above the crease, a little past halfway up toward the armpit; measured from above, the same spot lies four parts below the front fold of the armpit. Both descriptions land in one place. Press around that neighborhood until you find a tender, slightly dense spot in the groove hugging the biceps edge — that confirmable soreness is the usual signature of the point.

## Reading the name: xia, bai, and flanking white {#name-meaning}

Chinese point names compress imagery into two or three characters, and Xiabai rewards a slow read. Bai, white, is the colour the five-phase tradition assigns to the lungs and to metal: autumn light, clarity, crisp dry air — and the pale complexion classical writers watched for in lung disorders. Naming a lung-channel point white ties it visibly, from the first character onward, to the organ it serves.

Xia is the livelier character. Commentaries gathered in A Manual of Acupuncture read it as escort, guardian, or flanking companion: the point stands guard beside the whiteness of the lung qi, like an aide walking alongside a pale banner. Other glosses link xia phonetically to jia, to flank, which suits the anatomy neatly — Xiabai truly stands beside the whitish sheen of the biceps and its tendon on the inner arm. Whichever layer you favour, the name keeps pointing at one picture: a modest sentinel posted next to something luminous, high on the inner arm where the lung channel gathers itself for the journey down.

## What tradition reaches for at Xiabai {#traditional-indications}

The compiled catalogue lists five classical actions for Xiabai: cough, asthma, dry retching, upper arm pain, and rapid heartbeat. Read with traditional-use framing rather than as modern claims, these are the situations in which classical practice historically reached for this point — a record of where earlier practitioners found it useful, passed down through the literature rather than tested in contemporary trials.

The chest-related entries fit the point’s channel position. Because the lung channel descends from the chest along exactly this corridor of the inner arm, a point here was treated as a downstream doorway toward the lung itself: cough and wheezing breath, the most repeated lung-channel entries, sit in Xiabai’s listing alongside those of its neighbors. Dry retching reads differently — it belongs to the stomach rebelling upward — and classical listings often pair lung-channel points with such counterflow, reflecting the traditional view that the lung’s descending quality helps settle what the stomach sends upward. Rapid heartbeat extends the same settling logic toward the chest as a whole. Upper arm pain is finally the plainly local entry: a point lying on the radial border of the biceps was reached for in classical practice for aching in precisely that region. None of this amounts to a modern evidence statement; it is the traditional map, faithfully reported, and the shared safety note on every page of this site applies in full.

## Gentle self-acupressure at home {#self-care-acupressure}

Self-acupressure at Xiabai asks for less force than you probably expect. Sit upright in a chair, shoulders easy, and support the working arm across a cushion or your opposite forearm so the inner arm faces up. Locate the point with a fingertip first, then settle your thumb pad onto it. Begin with light contact and let the pressure grow over a few slow breaths until you reach a firm-but-comfortable depth — enough to feel a spreading ache, never enough to make yourself flinch.

From there, work in small, unhurried circles, keeping the pressure even, and let your breathing stay slower than conversation. One to two minutes per side is plenty for a first session; many people simply repeat it morning and evening on days they feel they want it. If one side feels markedly more tender than the other, give the tender side the gentler treatment rather than the harder one. Between sessions, loose fisted tapping along the inner arm, armpit toward elbow, offers a lighter alternative some people enjoy. Stop at once if anything sharpens into true pain, and skip the work entirely over broken, inflamed, or freshly bruised skin. Acupressure here is self-care, not treatment: it complements, and never replaces, advice from a qualified clinician.

## Cautions, limits, and honest boundaries {#cautions-safety}

The catalogue entry for Xiabai records no point-specific cautions, and this page echoes that emptiness deliberately rather than inventing warnings the dataset does not carry. The absence is not a blank cheque: it means no classical restriction was transmitted for this point, while the universal rules of safe self-care still govern everything above. Pressure stays moderate, sessions stay short, and sharp sensation ends the session immediately.

Some situations belong with professionals, full stop. Persistent or worsening cough, breathlessness that arrives uninvited, chest pain, palpitations that feel new or frightening, vomiting that will not settle — these deserve assessment by a physician or licensed acupuncture practitioner, not a longer session with your thumb. If you are pregnant, managing a chronic condition, or taking blood-thinning medication, ask a qualified practitioner before adding any new point work. Deeper professional techniques at this point — including needling, which requires licensed training and is never part of self-care — fall entirely outside what this page teaches. What remains is simple: a quiet point on the inner arm, a few minutes of patient pressure, and the good sense to hand over whatever self-care cannot address.
