Chize sits in the soft hollow at the outer end of the elbow crease, just beside the cord of the biceps tendon. Classical practice reaches for this he-sea point when a cough runs hot, the throat feels swollen, or the forearm carries the tension of a long day.

## Finding the Hollow: Locating Chize by Touch {#finding-the-hollow}

The catalogue describes the site plainly: at the cubital crease, in the depression on the radial side of the tendon of biceps brachii. Rendered into ordinary language, that means the soft dip at the front of the elbow, on the thumb side of the strong cord-like tendon that stands up when the arm bends. Everything about locating this point follows from three landmarks: the crease, the tendon, and the hollow just beyond them.

To find it on yourself, sit comfortably and let the arm go loose, palm turned upward. Bend the elbow slightly, perhaps thirty degrees, so the crease softens and the tendon relaxes enough to move under your fingers. Walk your fingertips across the front of the elbow until you meet the taut cord of the biceps tendon; it rolls noticeably when you make a fist against light resistance. Now slide your fingertip to the thumb side of that cord, right onto the crease line, and let it settle into the depression there. Most people find a small, tender pocket that yields under the fingerpad while the tendon beside it stays firm.

Two habits make the search easier. First, compare elbows: many people find the landmark clearer on one side, and the tenderness often differs between arms. Second, resist hunting with a stiff, pressing thumb; a relaxed fingerpad reads the terrain far better than force does. When the fingertip rests in genuine softness bounded by tendon on one side and forearm muscle on the other, you have arrived. That contrast — firm, hollow, firm — is the tactile signature practitioners describe.

## Cubit Marsh: Reading the Name 尺澤 {#name-meaning}

The Chinese name rewards a slow reading. Chi , the first character, is the cubit: the old body-based measure spanning roughly elbow to wrist, the distance a hand travels along the forearm. In classical point names, chi frequently anchors a point to the elbow end of that measure. Ze means marsh or pool — low ground where water gathers, spreads, and grows quiet. Together the two characters sketch a small landscape: a marsh at the cubit, pooled at the elbow.

That image is not merely decorative; it encodes how classical texts describe this family of points. Chize belongs to the he-sea class, and the classics picture each channel as a river that begins as a trickle near the fingertips and widens as it travels toward the trunk. At the he-sea points, the teaching runs, the water finally pools — wide, deep, and slow. A marsh is exactly where a broad, unhurried current would settle. Setting that imagery at the elbow matches lived anatomy too: this is where the channel’s thread of sensation seems to deepen into something fuller than it feels down at the wrist.

Five-phase teaching goes one step further and pairs each he-sea point with an element; for the Lung channel the elbow point is traditionally read as water. Such labels served as a shared vocabulary for qualities — cooling, descending, settling — rather than as measurements. Read this way, the name does double duty: geography and temperament compressed into two characters.

## Traditional Uses Recorded for This Point {#traditional-uses}

The dataset behind this page records a wide traditional portfolio for Chize: cough, asthma, coughing blood, sore and swollen throat, spasmodic pain of the elbow and arm, acute vomiting and diarrhoea, heatstroke, and infantile convulsions. Read as history rather than prescription, the list sketches the moments when classical practice reached for the elbow: hot, irritated conditions of the chest and throat, sudden digestive upset, and states of overheating or alarm. Traditionally used for — that is the honest frame for every item here.

An internal logic runs through the list. Because Chize marks the deepest pooling place of the Lung channel, classical texts describe he-sea points as natural addresses for rebellious, upward-rushing movement in the upper body: the cough that refuses to settle, breath that climbs shorter and shorter, a stomach that empties upward without warning. Calming the marsh, in that way of thinking, lowers the flood. Whatever one makes of the metaphor, it explains the shape of the indication list remarkably well.

Modern evidence deserves its own sober paragraph. Reviews of acupressure and related touch-based practices suggest possible comfort benefits in some contexts, but findings remain mixed, study designs vary widely, and nothing here should be mistaken for settled medicine. What can be said simply is this: gentle pressure at the elbow hollow is a low-effort, low-risk habit drawn from a long tradition, and many people keep it up as much for the ritual of attention as for anything else.

## A Gentle Self-Acupressure Sequence {#self-care-practice}

Settle in before you press. Sit where the whole forearm can rest supported, palm facing up, shoulder dropped away from the ear, and shake the hand out loosely once or twice; a tense arm hides its own landmarks. Locate the hollow using the palpation steps above, then rest the pad of your opposite thumb there with no pressure at all for a few slow breaths.

Then begin. Sink the thumb pad into the hollow gradually, angled slightly toward the tendon but never digging into it, until you meet the first clear layer of resistance — enough to feel distinctly, well short of discomfort. Hold that depth and let small circles travel from the wrist, or simply keep steady contact while the breath lengthens. Somewhere between half a minute and two minutes per side suits most people; the aim is unhurried contact, not endurance. Ease off in the reverse of how you pressed in, then repeat on the other arm.

Quality of touch matters more than quantity of force. Well-judged pressure here feels like a dull, spreading warmth or mild ache that loosens as you stay with it. Sharp, electric, or suddenly stabbing sensations — especially anything that pulses — mean the thumb has found tendon or, more often, a superficial vessel; glide a few millimeters toward the middle of the hollow and begin again more softly. Anyone who bruises easily should favour the lightest end of the range.

## Where It Fits an Ordinary Week {#daily-contexts}

Desk-bound stretches are the natural candidates. Hours of typing hold the elbows bent and the forearms loaded across exactly the region this point occupies, and by late afternoon the crease area often feels dense and reluctant. A brief pause to locate the hollow, breathe, and apply easy circular pressure doubles as a micro-break for the whole forearm — the precise technique matters less than interrupting the posture that created the stiffness.

Seasonal transitions send their own invitations. Weeks of dry, scratchy throats and the first coughs of cold season fall squarely in the territory classical practice assigned to the Lung channel, and although pressing an elbow hollow neither prevents nor treats illness, many people find that one deliberate minute of self-care on a scratchy evening is steadying in its own right. Hot commutes and crowded trains earn the same reasoning: tradition filed heatstroke-type misery under this address, and a cool-room pause with quiet pressure honours that spirit even now.

Treat the practice as punctuation rather than therapy. Its realistic gifts are a moment of downshift, sharper awareness of where the arm stores tension, and a small repeatable act of looking after oneself. Held that way, expectations stay proportionate — and the habit sustains itself, which is honestly most of what any self-care ritual can promise.

## Cautions and Safety: Respecting the Vessels {#cautions-safety}

This point carries the one caution the catalogue records for it, and it deserves real weight: the dataset warns about the cephalic vein, noting that piercing a vessel may cause bruising and swelling. The warning is written for professional contexts in which the skin is pierced, yet its anatomical lesson transfers directly to self-care. The hollow being pressed overlies superficial veins; the very depression that makes the point easy to find is also where vessels lie close to the surface. Pressing into the centre of the hollow, beside rather than on top of any visible or pulsing cord, honours both the tradition and the plumbing.

A handful of simple rules covers nearly everything. Skip the session entirely if the crease skin is broken, sunburned, rashy, or infected. Choose clearly lighter pressure if you bruise easily or take blood-thinning medication, and consider consulting a licensed practitioner before making elbow pressure a standing habit. During pregnancy, keep self-acupressure feather-light and mention any new routine to a midwife or doctor as ordinary prudence. Children may receive the lightest, briefest touch, and only when they welcome it.

Escalate rather than persist. Symptoms that keep returning, worsen, or arrive with fever belong with a qualified healthcare professional, not with longer sessions at the elbow. Coughing blood in particular warrants prompt medical assessment, whatever tradition records about the point linked to it. And the universal rule holds here as everywhere: sharp pain means stop, reassess, and either soften the approach or leave the point alone until another day.

## Chize Among Its Neighbors on the Channel {#among-neighbors}

Context sharpens the portrait. The Lung channel runs through eleven named stations from the shoulder region down the arm to the nail side of the thumb, and Chize stands fifth — the last stop before the channel leaves the elbow for the long descent along the forearm. Directly below come Kongzui (LU6), the xi-cleft point classically reached for abrupt, acute episodes, and Lieque (LU7), a frequent companion for throat and neck complaints. Each neighbor has its specialty; the elbow point remains the deep-water anchor of the group.

Classical teaching assigns the channel’s he-sea role to this station, which is why commentators reach for river imagery again and again: the trickle that starts near the collarbone at Zhongfu becomes, by the elbow, a body of water with weight and direction. For a home practitioner the practical translation is modest — the elbow hollow tends to feel denser, deeper, and slower to yield than the delicate points near the wrist — and the technique adjusts accordingly: more patience, less probing, longer exhales.
