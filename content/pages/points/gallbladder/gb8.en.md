Shuaigu, Leading Valley, sits on the side of the head, a thumb-width and a half above the tip of the ear, tucked inside the hairline. Classical practice reached for it when pain gathered over one temple, when dizziness made the room sway, or when the ears rang. This guide walks through locating it by touch, reading its name, and pressing it gently and safely at home.

## Where Shuaigu Sits on the Side of the Head {#where-it-sits}

The dataset places Shuaigu on the head, one and a half cun directly above the tip of the ear, within the hairline, and directly above Jiaosun (TH20). Three anchors hide in that sentence. First, the ear tip: fold the ear gently forward and the uppermost point of the rim — the peak of the visible ear — becomes the starting mark. Second, the vertical line: everything happens straight above that tip, not angled toward the face or toward the back of the skull. Third, the distance: a cun is the traditional proportional unit taken from the person being examined, and on the head it is usually taken as roughly the width of the person’s thumb — so one and a half cun is a thumb-width and a half, measured slowly upward through the hair.

The territory under the fingertip is the temple region proper — the flat, slightly curved stretch where the side of the head meets the hair. Press lightly and the scalp glides thinly over bone; clench and release the jaw and a broad fan of muscle above and slightly behind the ear firms against the fingers, because the chewing muscle of the temple spans this whole neighbourhood. Nearer the front edge of the ear, a vessel’s beat can sometimes be felt under the skin. None of this asks for force. Scalp and bone carry sensation close to the surface, and the traditions of self-care at the head have always favoured patient, feather-light contact over pressure.

Neighbours help confirm the placement. Below, at the hairline directly above the ear tip, sits Jiaosun (TH20) of the san jiao channel — the very point from which the dataset measures Shuaigu upward. Behind, the catalogue places Tianchong (GB9) about half a thumb-width away along the channel’s backward path. Read together, the three points sketch a short itinerary up the side of the head: ear tip, hairline, then a climb of a thumb-width and a half into the hair. If a fingertip lands somewhere that feels wrong, the remedy is always the same — return to the ear tip and measure again.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit upright in a comfortable chair and let the shoulders drop, because a tensed neck makes every spot on the head feel tender. With the opposite hand, fold the ear gently forward and find the topmost point of the rim; that peak is the anchor. Rest the pad of the index or middle finger just above it, then walk straight upward through the hair for about a thumb-width and a half, slowly enough to feel the scalp shift over bone. Most people arrive at a shallow, quietly sensitive hollow or flattening somewhere inside the hairline — the neighbourhood the dataset describes.

Two confirmations turn a guess into a placement. First, clench the back teeth gently and release: the broad temple muscle should tighten and soften under the fingertip, confirming that it rests over muscle and bone rather than in loose hair alone. Second, compare sides with a feather-light touch — many people notice a faint, dull awareness at the true point and little sensation a finger-width away, and the two sides rarely feel identical. Small asymmetries are ordinary; what matters is that each side is found by its own landmarks, not by copying the other side’s measurement blindly.

The common slips are predictable. Drifting forward carries the finger toward the softer hollow nearer the ear’s front edge, where a pulse sometimes announces itself — a sign to slide gently back above the ear tip. Trusting the hairline instead of the ear misleads, because hairlines sit at very different heights on different heads; the ear tip, not the border of the hair, is the fixed landmark the record names. And hunting for sensation with force defeats itself: heavy pressure makes the whole region report the same dull ache, erasing the contrast that identifies the point. Lighten the touch, slow the search, and let the spot declare itself.

## Reading the Name: Leading Valley {#name-and-meaning}

Shuaigu joins two characters with workmanlike elegance. Shuai 率 carries the old senses of to lead and to guide — the same character serves in words for leading a column of people or setting a pace — and by extension, to travel along a course. Gu 谷 is a valley: the low corridor between heights. Set together on the side of the head, Leading Valley pictures a guiding groove — a corridor along which movement travels the temple road, the image classical writers enjoyed for a point standing midway on the channel’s winding route over the ear.

The catalogue records a second credential: Shuaigu is listed among the crossing-points, the stations classical theory marks where two pathways share ground. Commentary handed down with the point describes the gallbladder channel meeting the bladder channel here — fitting, since the bladder channel’s crown territory spreads across the top of the head just above. Whatever weight one gives channel metaphysics, the crossing label captures something real about the geography: this stretch of temple is a crossroads where the side of the head meets the crown, and the tradition named the junction.

Pinyin renders the name Shuàigǔ: shuai carries a falling fourth tone, clipped and decisive, while gu dips through the third tone before settling. The hanzi pair 率谷 is compact — a handful of strokes apiece — and joins the large family of head points named for landscape: valleys and ravines scattered along the hair. Like the gates and courtyards elsewhere on the maps, the metaphor instructs: valleys collect what drains from the heights, and a guiding valley implies a path worth following. Learn the name and half the location lesson is already learned.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

Six entries make up the recorded portfolio: one-sided headache, dizziness, tinnitus, deafness, infantile convulsions, and vomiting after drinking. The first shapes the rest of the list. The gallbladder channel zigzags down the side of the head, so discomfort that settles over one temple — the classic one-sided headache of classical description — was naturally approached at points along that same route, and Shuaigu stands squarely on the road. Framed as tradition: classical practice traditionally used this point when pain gathered over one side of the head, understood in that framework as the channel’s own territory complaining.

Dizziness, tinnitus, and deafness belong to the ear-side geography and to older physiology. The point stands close above the ear, and classical teaching drew the gallbladder channel past the ear’s neighbourhood on its way over the temple; spinning sensations and sounds inside the ears were therefore read as disturbances of that shared district, and the point was reached for accordingly. In classical imagery, ascending wind and rising yang were pictured disturbing the clear openings of the head — vivid language for what people describe as vertigo or ringing. These are traditional ways of mapping complaint to location, documented for understanding rather than promised as outcomes.

The last two entries belong to their era and deserve honest handling. Infantile convulsions appear throughout old pediatric records; whatever the classical response was, a convulsion in a child today is an emergency belonging to immediate medical care, and nothing here suggests otherwise. Vomiting after drinking preserves an old observation that excess drink unsettled the stomach and the shaoyang systems together, and the point entered those discussions; it is kept here as cultural history, neither endorsement nor advice. As for modern evidence, reviews suggest effects of acupressure and related practices are modest and depend heavily on context, and research pinned to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition lightly; hold the safety rules firmly.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

Settle in first: seated, spine easy, shoulders heavy, a few slow breaths marking the transition. Locate the point by the walkthrough above, then rest the pads of the index and middle fingers — never the nails — on the spot. Begin with contact alone, letting the fingers simply listen for several breaths. Then draw small, slow circles, letting the scalp glide over the bone beneath. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or spreading warmth, never sharpness. A comfortable home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, each circle riding a slow exhale. Finish by swapping hands and giving the other side the same unhurried attention.

Small courtesies make the practice kinder. Warm cold hands before touching the head, because chilled fingertips feel harsher than they intend. Gather long hair loosely first so fingers do not tug strands mid-circle, and keep nails trimmed short. Choose a quiet corner rather than a screen: the pause is half the benefit, and pairing the pressure with slow nasal breathing turns a brief daily round into a genuine rest for a busy head.

Treat the exercise as attendance, not treatment. Across weeks, notice what changes: perhaps the temples hold less guard, perhaps the pause itself becomes the treasured part, perhaps nothing shifts beyond the quiet pleasure of having attended to yourself. All of that fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a particular result. When curiosity pulls that way, return to lightness or set the practice down for the day. Gentleness is not the timid version of the technique; it is the technique.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

The licensed dataset records no cautions for this point — a calm bill of health worth reading precisely. It does not mean the head invites force; it means the compiled records flag no special danger of the kind noted for certain chest and shoulder points, where deep structures lie close beneath the skin. The terrain here remains delicate in its own way: thin scalp over curved bone, a busy muscle, vessels near the ear’s front edge. Professional acupuncture certainly reaches this site, guided by training home practice does not replicate; household self-care keeps a different and simpler covenant — stay on the surface.

Practical rules follow. Use finger pads only, and keep pressure light enough to stay pleasant — dull warmth, never sharpness. Skip the point over broken, irritated, or freshly scraped skin, and pause the practice during any acute illness until energy returns. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once, for that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with neurological, circulatory, or chronic headache conditions, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice — not because this point is flagged, but because new routines deserve a professional glance.

And the wider rules stand over everything. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for a sudden, severe headache unlike any before; headache after a blow to the head; headache with fever and a stiff neck; or headache accompanied by vision changes, weakness, numbness, confusion, or repeated vomiting. Persistent or steadily worsening symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner, medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. This page is educational only, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. The safest gift Shuaigu offers most people is a calmer acquaintance with their own head, treated kindly.
