Chengling, whose name reads as Receiving the Spirit, stands near the crown of the frontal scalp — the highest of four stations climbing in a straight line above the pupil. Classical practice reached for it when head and eye pain, dizziness, or congested sinuses weighed on a person. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it gently, safety rules first.

## Where Chengling Sits Above the Forehead {#where-it-sits}

The dataset places Chengling on the head, four cun above the anterior hairline, 2.25 cun lateral to the midline of the head. Two coordinates fix the spot, and both deserve unpacking. A cun is the traditional proportional measure of Chinese point-location, taken from the body being examined rather than from any ruler: about one thumb-breadth at the hand of most adults. Four cun therefore means roughly four thumb-breadths measured straight up the scalp from the front hairline. The lateral figure sets the point off-centre: well clear of the central seam of the skull, yet nowhere near so far sideways as the temple. Height and offset together put Chengling high on the forehead’s own upward continuation, where the brow-line points give way to the crown.

The neighbourhood is a vertical family, and the records describe them as one row. Toulinqi (GB15), at the row’s foot, sits directly above the pupil, half a cun inside the anterior hairline. Muchuang (GB16) follows one-and-a-half cun above the hairline; Zhengying (GB17) stands two-and-a-half cun up, recorded as one cun behind Muchuang; and Chengling completes the row at four cun. Every one of them carries the same lateral offset — 2.25 cun from the midline — so the row climbs straight along the line the pupil suggests, with the gaps widening as it goes: a single cun between the lower stations, one-and-a-half cun between the upper pair. Knowing the row’s shape turns a hunt into a walk: whichever station your fingertips find first, the others stand evenly spaced above or below on the same invisible plumb line.

Beneath the fingertip, the geography is forgiving. This is scalp riding on unbroken bone — skin, a thin layer of fascia, then skull — without the hollows or great vessels that make some regions of the body demand extra respect. Most people notice that the true point feels faintly different from the scalp around it: a little denser, sometimes quietly tender, especially after long hours of concentrated work or a restless night. Hair covers the area for nearly everyone, so expect to part strands and search with the pads of the fingers rather than the eyes. Nothing about locating Chengling needs force; patient, light contact lets the landmarks announce themselves, and the search should feel closer to listening than to pressing.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit facing a mirror with shoulders released, and look straight ahead: the line rising from the centre of either pupil, up over the forehead and into the hair, is the track you want. Follow it to the front hairline — trace upward from the brow until the skin of the forehead gives way to hair. From that crossing, measure straight up the scalp about four thumb-breadths, walking the thumb in careful steps and honouring the curve of the head as you go. The patch of scalp you arrive at — still on the forward slope, short of the true crown — is where the dataset locates Chengling. Repeat the whole measurement on the other side, so both halves of the row receive equal attention.

A shorter route confirms the placement: climb the row instead of measuring from scratch. From the same hairline crossing, one-and-a-half thumb-breadths up finds Muchuang (GB16); one more thumb-breadth higher sits Zhengying (GB17); and one-and-a-half thumb-breadths beyond Zhengying lands on Chengling. When the two routes agree, confidence is earned. Before any pressing begins, touch feather-light and compare sides: most people feel a faint, dull awareness at the true point and little sensation a thumb-width away. Nudge the scalp in tiny circles and notice how it glides over bone — that mobility confirms tissue-covered skull rather than tendon. The mirror helps police the line: the point should hang directly above the pupil, not drifted inward toward the top of the head’s centre.

Two slips account for most misses. The first is wandering inward toward the midline, where the governing vessel runs its own quiet row; Chengling keeps faithfully to the pupil’s line, out at 2.25 cun. The second is stopping early — landing on Zhengying, one-and-a-half thumb-breadths below, which shares so many qualities that only the count separates the two. Tenderness misleads here as everywhere on the scalp, because tension makes many spots grumble after a long day. Counting prevents both errors: four thumb-breadths from the hairline, or three stations up the row. When in doubt, drop the hands, shake them out, and begin again from the hairline slowly. The search should take a minute, not a session.

## Reading the Name: Receiving the Spirit {#name-and-meaning}

The characters carry the meaning plainly. Cheng 承 means to receive, to hold up, to bear — the open hand beneath something offered, the shoulder set under a load. Ling 靈 means spirit, the numinous, the quick intelligence of things; it survives in everyday words for cleverness and for whatever answers when called upon. Joined, Chengling reads as receiving the spirit or upholding the numinous — an image of the upper head as the place where something bright settles and is held. Classical teaching favoured exactly this picture: it describes the head as the high ground where clarity lodges, and a point standing high upon that ground was naturally imagined as the shelf on which the treasure rests.

The dataset tags Chengling among the crossing-points, and the company is striking: every record in the vertical row — Toulinqi (GB15), Muchuang (GB16), Zhengying (GB17), Chengling (GB18) — carries the same tag. Crossing-points, in classical theory, are stations where the main channel shares ground with other pathways of the traditional map; the label marks busy junctions rather than quiet way-stations. This module stops short of reciting which routes the old books tied together at each junction, because the traditions themselves differ on such details. Even so, the clustering of the tag along one straight stretch says enough: the whole line above the pupil was treated as shared, well-travelled territory, a corridor where more than one road ran.

Pinyin renders the name Chénglíng, and both syllables carry the second tone — Mandarin’s rising tone, written with an acute accent: chéng, líng. Two identical rises in succession give the name an uplifting, almost questioning lilt, easy to remember once heard. The hanzi pair 承靈 is short enough to take in at a glance, and like many points named for reception and light — courtyards, windows, halls — the metaphor does real work. It says the tradition regarded this spot as a place where something arrives and is received, not a bare milestone on the channel’s climb. The name is itself a small set of instructions.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: head and eye pain, dizziness, sinusitis, nosebleed, nasal congestion, and tinnitus. Take the first pair together. The gallbladder channel owns the side of the head — the site’s own summary describes it zigzagging down the side of the head and body to end at the fourth toe, threading forty-four stations from beside the outer corner of the eye — so headache in all its shadings gathered along the route. The eye belongs to the same story: the row above the pupil was drawn in the image of sight itself, and the neighbour one station below is even named Muchuang, the Eye Window. Head-and-eye pain travelling together was, in the traditional frame, precisely what a point on this line existed for.

Dizziness threads through the whole row’s records — each of the four stations lists it — and the classical explanation follows the wind. In the traditional frame, stirrings of inner wind rise upward, and the head is the high place such movement reaches first; a point near the summit stood in the path, and was reached for when the world seemed to tilt. It must be said plainly: these are historical frameworks for understanding the body, recorded for cultural context. Dizziness that is sudden, severe, or accompanied by speech difficulty, weakness, or vision change belongs to urgent medical care today, and nothing on this page suggests otherwise.

The remaining indications follow the map toward the senses. Sinusitis, nosebleed, and nasal congestion stand together in the record because classical practice grouped disorders of the nose with wind troubling the face, and reached for yang-channel points high on the head to clear the passages — the reasoning being that the orifices open most freely when the pathways above them move. Tinnitus rides the channel’s broader territory: the gallbladder pathway wraps the ear, and its neighbouring records brim with complaints of ringing and muffled hearing. All of this is traditional reasoning, offered for understanding. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence for any single point remains thin.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle in: seated, spine tall but unforced, jaw loose, eyes closed or softly focused. Curl the index and middle fingers together and rest the pads — never the nails — on the spot. Begin with contact alone for a few slow breaths, then add small, unhurried circles, letting the scalp glide over the skull beneath rather than dragging the skin. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or a spreading mild warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, each circle matched where possible to a long exhale. Swap hands afterwards and repeat on the other side, keeping the two sessions symmetrical.

Quality outranks duration here as everywhere else on the body. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; jabbing at a tender scalp teaches the body nothing and sours the habit. Warm cold hands before starting, because chilled muscle guards. Choose a quiet corner over a screen and let the minutes double as a breathing pause: inhale through the nose, feel the scalp rise faintly under still fingers, lean the pressure in on the exhale and release it on the next breath. People who keep a routine often anchor it to an existing habit — morning washing, an afternoon stretch break — because consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Honest expectations keep the practice kind. Across weeks you may notice the area feels less guarded, or discover that the real value is the pause itself — ninety seconds of attending to your own head in a loud day. Perhaps nothing shifts measurably; that, too, sits within the honest scope of self-acupressure, which is a comfort practice, not a treatment session. What falls outside the scope is escalation: pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a result. When curiosity pulls that way, return to gentleness, or finish for the day and begin again tomorrow with lighter hands.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

The licensed record attaches no point-specific caution to Chengling; unlike entries for some points near the eyes or the great vessels, this one carries no warning addressed to practitioners. An empty caution column is not permission to press harder — it hands responsibility to the ordinary rules, which must then be kept thoroughly. The terrain helps: scalp stretched over unbroken bone tolerates light surface contact well, and the point sits far from the eyes, the ears, and the major conduits of the neck. Even so, scalps differ and health differs, and the discipline stays the same as everywhere on the body — gentle, superficial, attentive, and always ready to stop.

The working rules are few and firm. Fingertip pads only — nails, knuckles, elbows, and gadgets all stay outside the practice — and the pressure lives in skin and scalp layers, never driven against the bone beneath. Leave the point alone over broken or irritated skin, during a feverish illness, and for some days after any knock to the head, returning only when everything feels ordinary again. Sharpness, electricity, or a sensation that spreads oddly means stop immediately, and the day’s practice ends there. Readers who are pregnant, or who manage chronic conditions such as a seizure disorder, do best to check with a qualified practitioner before adding any new point to their routine, however gentle the technique.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for the worst headache of your life, headache with fever or stiff neck, sudden dizziness, vision change, weakness, vomiting, any complaint following a head injury, or any symptom that frightens you. Persistent headaches, ongoing sinus trouble, or new ringing in the ears deserve proper assessment in their own right, whatever comfort the practice adds alongside. The image of a crown receiving light is lovely, but the safest gift Chengling offers most people is a quieter, kinder acquaintance with their own head, touched lightly and put away on time.
