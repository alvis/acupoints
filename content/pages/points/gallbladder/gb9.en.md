Tianchong, the Heavenly Rush, hides in the hair above and behind the ear, high on the side of the head. Classical practice set it among the points reached for when headache, startle, or ringing ears unsettled a person. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it gently.

## Where Tianchong Sits on the Side of the Head {#where-it-sits}

The dataset places Tianchong on the head, two cun directly above the posterior border of the root of the ear, within the hairline, and half a cun behind Shuaigu (GB8). Every part of that sentence rewards unpacking. A cun is the traditional proportional measure of Chinese point-location, taken from the body being examined rather than from any ruler: about one thumb-breadth at the hand of most adults, so two cun means roughly two thumb-breadths measured straight upward. The posterior border of the root of the ear is simply the back edge of the ear where it rises from the scalp. And within the hairline tells you the point hides under hair for many people — this is a landmark you find with fingertips, not eyes.

The neighbourhood clarifies further. Shuaigu (GB8), the stepping-stone neighbour named in the location itself, sits one-and-a-half cun directly above the tip of the ear according to its own dataset record; from there, half a cun toward the back of the head brings you to Tianchong. Behind Tianchong the channel keeps travelling: the records describe an arcuate line running from Tianchong down to Wangu (GB12) behind the ear, with Fubai (GB10) and Touqiaoyin (GB11) marking its thirds along the way. So Tianchong is not an isolated dot but the upper anchor of a curved chain of points tracing the skull behind the ear — knowing that shape helps you feel whether your fingertip has landed sensibly within it.

Beneath the fingertip, the geography is forgiving. This is scalp over solid skull — tissue layers riding on unbroken bone, with none of the hollow concerns that make chest-wall points demand respect. Most people notice that the true point feels faintly different from the scalp around it: slightly denser, sometimes faintly tender, especially after long screen hours or a poor night. Hair whorls differently over everyone, so expect to part strands and search by touch. Nothing about the search needs force; the landmarks announce themselves to patient, light contact, exactly as they do everywhere else on the body.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit upright in front of good light with shoulders released so the neck softens. Start from the ear tip, the highest point of the ear rim. Measure straight up from it into the hair about one-and-a-half thumb-breadths and press gently: a spot of mild tenderness there is Shuaigu (GB8). Now slide half a thumb-breadth straight back, toward the back of the head, keeping the same height. The patch of scalp you settle on — faintly tender, sitting inside the hair — is Tianchong. Cross-check with the other landmark: walk two thumb-breadths straight up the back edge of the ear root and you should arrive at the same place. Repeat the search on the other side so both receive equal attention.

A few confirmations help before any pressing begins. Touch feather-light and compare sides; most people feel a faint, dull awareness at the true point and little sensation a thumb-width away. Nudge the scalp gently in tiny circles and notice how it glides over bone — that mobility confirms you are on tissue-covered skull rather than on the firmer grip of tendon near the temple. A mirror helps you judge the position relative to the ear: the point should sit above the back half of the ear, not above the ear canal and not up near the crown. If what you feel is hard bone with no give at all, ease off and re-anchor from the ear tip.

Two classic mistakes account for most misses. The first is drifting forward into the temple hollow, where a different family of points lives; Tianchong stays behind the plane of the ear tip. The second is climbing too high toward the crown, chasing tenderness upward — tenderness alone misleads, because scalp tension makes many spots grumble. Counting prevents both errors: one-and-a-half thumb-breadths above the ear tip, then half a thumb-breadth back. When in doubt, drop the hands, shake them out, and begin again from the ear tip slowly. The search should take a minute, not a session.

## Reading the Name: Heavenly Rush {#name-and-meaning}

Tian 天 means heaven or sky — the highest reaches of things — and in point names it almost always plants a flag on the head, the body’s own high country. Chong 衝 carries the sense of a surge or rush, and by extension a thoroughfare: everyday language still uses the character in compounds for a strategic hub where roads converge. Joined, Tianchong reads as the heavenly rush or the celestial thoroughfare — an image of movement pouring into the head’s high ground, and of a junction standing in its path. Classical teaching loved exactly this double image: a place where something rises, and a place where pathways cross.

The dataset tags Tianchong among the crossing-points, and the company it keeps is telling. Along the curved chain behind the ear, Fubai (GB10), Touqiaoyin (GB11), and Wangu (GB12) carry the same tag in the same records. Crossing-points, in classical theory, are stations where the main channel shares ground with other pathways of the traditional map; the label marks busy junctions rather than quiet way-stations. This module stops short of reciting which routes the old books tied together at each junction — the traditions themselves differ on such details — but the clustering of the tag along this one stretch tells you the whole sweep behind the ear was treated as shared, well-travelled territory.

Pinyin renders the name Tiānchōng, and both syllables carry the first tone — Mandarin’s flat, level, high-register tone, written as a macron over the vowel: tian, chong. Two level tones in a row give the name a steady, chanting quality, easy to remember. The hanzi pair 天衝 is short enough to take in at a glance, and like many points named for landscape and movement — gates, valleys, rushes, pools — the metaphor does real work. It says the tradition treated this spot as a place of ascent and exchange high on the head, not a quiet cul-de-sac. The name is itself a small set of instructions.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: headache, epilepsy, wind convulsions, fright palpitations, tinnitus, deafness, goitre, and toothache. Take the plainest first. The gallbladder channel owns the side of the head — the site’s own summary describes it zigzagging down the side of the head and body to end at the fourth toe, threading forty-four stations from beside the outer corner of the eye to that toe. A channel so devoted to the head’s lateral country naturally collected its aches: side-of-head headache was, and in traditional practice remains, this channel’s signature complaint, and Tianchong stands high in that territory.

The middle of the list — epilepsy, wind convulsions, fright palpitations — belongs together in the classical frame. Wind and fright were traditional ways of describing sudden upheavals: convulsive movement, racing panic, a spirit knocked loose from its calm. Because the head was imagined as the high place where such winds gathered and surged — the very picture the point’s name paints — head points were drawn into classical discussions of these conditions. It must be said plainly: these are historical frameworks for understanding the body, recorded for cultural context. Convulsions and suspected seizures today belong to emergency and specialist medical care, and nothing on this page suggests otherwise.

The remaining indications follow the map. Tinnitus and deafness sit naturally beside a point hovering just above the ear, on a channel stretch whose neighbouring records brim with ear complaints. Goitre — the old records grouped certain neck swellings under this word — reflects the classical habit of reaching for points on a channel that descends from behind the ear past the side of the neck toward the shoulder; the neighbouring entries carry scrofula and goitre records in the same spirit. Toothache rides the same logic one joint along: temple and cheek-jaw neighbours record it too, and facial pain belonged to this channel’s rounds. All of it is traditional reasoning, offered for understanding — where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence for any single point remains thin.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle in: seated, spine tall but unforced, jaw loose. Curl the index and middle fingers together and rest the pads — never the nails — on the spot. Begin with contact alone for a few breaths, then add small, slow circles, letting the scalp glide over the skull beneath rather than dragging the skin. Pressure should read as pleasant dullness or mild warmth spreading under the fingers, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, each circle matched where possible to a slow exhale. Swap hands and repeat on the other side, keeping the sessions symmetrical.

Quality outranks duration here as everywhere. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; jabbing at a tender scalp teaches the body nothing and sours the habit. Warm cold hands first, because chilled muscle guards. Choose a quiet corner over a screen and treat the minutes as a breathing pause: inhale through the nose, feel the scalp rise faintly under still fingers, press gently on the long exhale. People who keep a routine often anchor it to an existing one — morning washing, an afternoon stretch break — because consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Honest expectations keep the practice kind. Across weeks you may notice the area feels less guarded, or that the pause itself becomes the value — ninety seconds of attending to your own head in a loud day. Perhaps nothing shifts measurably; that, too, is within the scope of self-acupressure, which is a comfort practice, not a treatment session. What falls outside the scope is escalation: pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a result. When curiosity pulls that way, return to gentleness, or simply finish for the day and try again tomorrow with lighter hands.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

The licensed record attaches no point-specific caution to Tianchong — unlike some chest and shoulder neighbours whose entries carry stern warnings for practitioners, this entry stands empty of them. That emptiness is not licence to lean harder; it simply means the ordinary rules carry the whole load. The geography helps: scalp over solid skull forgives gentle surface work in a way deep hollows never do. But scalps differ, heads differ, and health differs, so the universal discipline of self-acupressure applies undiminished — light, superficial, responsive, and always willing to stop.

Practical rules follow. Use finger pads only — no nails, elbows, tools, or devices — and keep the intention in skin and scalp layers, never grinding against bone. Skip the point over broken, irritated, or recently injured skin, and pause the practice during feverish illness or in the days after any blow to the head, until ordinary activity feels normal again. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once and do not retry that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with chronic conditions — seizure disorders especially — should speak with a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice, however gentle.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for the worst headache of your life, headache with fever, stiff neck, vision change, weakness, vomiting, any headache after a head injury, or any symptom that frightens you. Persistent or recurring complaints belong with a licensed practitioner, whether medical or East Asian medical suits the complaint. The image of a heavenly thoroughfare is lovely, but the safest gift Tianchong offers most people is a quieter, kinder acquaintance with their own scalp, pressed lightly and put away on time.
