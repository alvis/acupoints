Chengqi, the Tear Container, sits directly below the pupil, in the soft interval between the eye and the lower rim of its bony socket — the opening point of the stomach channel. Classical practice reached for it when eyes watered in wind or vision grew dim. This guide covers finding it by touch and pressing with feather-light care.

## Where Chengqi Sits: Below the Pupil, Above the Orbital Rim {#where-it-sits}

On the face, the dataset places Chengqi directly below the pupil, in the narrow space between the eyeball and the infraorbital border — the bony lower rim of the eye socket. Two features of that wording deserve notice. First, the anchor is the pupil itself, which means the reference exists fully only while the eyes look straight ahead; every step of locating flows from a level, forward gaze rather than from a knob you can pinch or a groove you can count. Second, the recorded position names a gap rather than a prominence: not a bump on the skull but the soft interval where the globe meets the shelf of bone beneath it. Many channel points sit on muscle bellies or tendon corridors where fingertips have something substantial to report. Here the finding is an absence — a yielding softness just above the orbital rim — and learning to feel for softness instead of structure is most of the skill.

What lies beneath the skin explains why tradition handles this address so respectfully. The lower eyelid carries some of the thinnest skin the body keeps; under it there is no robust muscle layer to buffer force, only delicate connective tissue, fine vessels, and then the eye itself resting above the rim. Periocular points appear throughout the classical literature precisely because this region is both interesting and fragile, and the old descriptions consistently pair such points with unusual handling cautions — a pattern this point’s own record follows faithfully, as the safety section below sets out. For self-palpation the consequence is simple: everything about touching here should feel lighter than touching anywhere else on the face. If a fingertip press here feels like a press anywhere else, it is already too much.

The catalogue numbers Chengqi as the first of forty-five points on the stomach meridian, and the recorded channel picture makes sense of that seemingly odd starting place. Our meridian reference draws the stomach channel beginning on the face, at this point beneath the eye, and traveling down the front of the body all the way to the second toe. An earth-element channel named for digestion thus opens at the eyes: in the traditional frame the stomach channel supplies the face abundantly, and its course ties eyes, cheeks, jaw, chest, and digestive core into one continuous descent. The catalogue also tags the point as a crossing-point — classical channel descriptions treat it as a meeting place where more than one pathway converges — and crossroads carry weight in traditional reasoning, because whatever gathers at such a spot was thought to arrive from several directions at once.

## A Feather-Light Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Begin with clean hands and a plain mirror at eye height, contact lenses removed so the eyes rest naturally. Sit or stand comfortably and let your gaze settle straight ahead at your reflection — the recorded location is defined by the forward-facing pupil, so the searching posture and the practising posture are one and the same. Find the vertical line running through the centre of one pupil, from brow to lip. Now bring the pad of your ring finger — the lightest-pressing finger most hands own — up along that line toward the eye, and let it rest on the skin just beneath the lower lashes, eyes gently closed. Under the pad you should feel the soft curve of the lower lid, and a few millimeters farther down, the firm arc of the orbital rim.

The point occupies the shallow, yielding interval between those two landmarks — above the bony rim, beside the globe — which is precisely the relationship the dataset records: between the eyeball and the infraorbital border. Confirm with comparison rather than force. Touch feather-light and compare sides; many people notice a faint, dull awareness or mild tenderness at the true spot and almost nothing a few millimeters away. Keep the fingertip perfectly still and let the eye rest behind the closed lid; there is no reason to slide the fingertip inward, upward, or around once contact is made. The whole search should take about half a minute. If anything about the contact feels uncertain, simply lift the finger away and pause before deciding whether to continue at all — near the eyes, ending a session early is always a respectable outcome.

The mistakes here are mostly errors of excess rather than of aim. The gravest is letting pressure drift onto the eyeball itself: the dataset’s caution names avoiding the eyeball explicitly, and home practice honours that absolutely — the fingertip rests on the soft tissues at the socket’s edge, never on the globe. A common slip is hunting too far down the cheek; once the pad lands on the firm flat expanse of the cheekbone it has left the recorded location entirely, because the true site hugs the upper margin of the socket. Another is pressing through moving tissue — rubbing while the eye darts about — which turns gentle contact into friction. Stillness, height, lightness: hold those three words together and the walkthrough nearly performs itself.

## Reading the Name: The Container That Receives Tears {#name-and-meaning}

The two characters sketch the point in miniature. Cheng 承 — pronounced chéng, climbing on the second tone — carries the sense of receiving, holding up, or bearing what is handed to you. Qi 泣 — pronounced qì, falling firmly on the fourth tone — means tears, and by extension quiet weeping. Side by side, Chengqi reads as the place where tears are received: the container that holds them for a moment before they fall. Anyone who has watched eyes brim knows the geography — tears gather in the tiny pocket along the lower lid, welling exactly where the point sits. The name is not decoration; it is a location description written as poetry, in the manner classical authors favoured.

Names like this show how the tradition wrote with the body rather than over it. Points on the face tend to borrow their names from visible behaviour — what a place does when feeling moves through it. A gate beside the nose, a courtyard at the cheekbone, a container under the eye where tears pool: each name doubles as a miniature anatomy lesson and a memory aid, encoding the location in an image no student forgets. Chengqi carries older alternative designations in the classical literature, as many storied points do, but the tear-container image is the one that endured, and it remains the quickest way for a newcomer to remember both where the point lies and what moods pass across it.

Pinyin renders the name Chéngqì: the first syllable rises through the second tone, the second falls away on the fourth, and that falling contour suits the meaning — tears letting go. The hanzi pair 承泣 is compact enough to memorise at a glance. Like many channel points named for humble domestic objects — wells, valleys, basins, gates, containers — the metaphor does real work. It says the tradition regarded this as a place where something collects, pauses, and is held, not merely a waypoint things rush past. In a naming system otherwise full of movement language, a still vessel set beneath the eye stands out quietly — and it asks to be approached the way it is imagined: slowly, softly, with attention.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: twitching of the eyelids, tearing in wind, blurred vision, deviation of the mouth and eye, facial spasm, and infraorbital neuralgia. Read in their own era’s vocabulary these form coherent clusters. Tearing in wind describes eyes that water uncontrollably out of doors — a complaint common enough to earn its own tidy phrase. Twitching eyelids and facial spasm sit together in the traditional understanding of wind stirring in the channels: involuntary movement read as agitation traveling along the face. Blurred vision joined the same portfolio because the channel beginning here was thought to nourish the eyes; a point at the doorway of sight naturally appears in classical discussions of sight growing dim.

The remaining indications follow the map rather than the metaphor. Deviation of the mouth and eye — the classical phrase for facial droop patterns — and infraorbital neuralgia, pain along the tender corridor beneath the eye, are both disorders of the very territory the point occupies. Traditional practice reasoned locally as well as along channels: a point placed amid facial structures belonged in discussions of facial complaints, and its recorded status as a crossing-point gave it standing in those conversations beyond its modest footprint on the skin. None of this requires accepting the old physiology to appreciate its internal consistency — indications, location, and channel story all point the same direction.

It bears repeating plainly: these are traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and described in classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats disease — and eye complaints especially deserve modern assessment, because vision is precious and its warning signs are not do-it-yourself material. Where modern research touches acupressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and heavily context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition lightly, and hold the safety rules firmly.

## Self-Care Acupressure Around the Eyes {#self-care-practice}

If you choose to practice here, let gentleness be the entire method. Wash your hands and remove contact lenses first. Rest the pad of one ring finger on the located point through the gently closed lid — contact, not compression; think of the weight of a resting hand, not of pressing. Some practitioners favour pure stillness: fingertip down, a handful of slow breaths, lift away. Others add the smallest circles, barely moving the skin. Half a minute to a minute per side is plenty for a home practice; near the eyes, restraint is the technique. Breathe slowly throughout, and let the session end while it still feels like very little — that is what appropriately light contact feels like.

Quality matters more than duration, and rhythm beats intensity. A short session daily, or a few times each week, fits the wellness register this page practises; hard jabbing near the eyes causes harm and teaches nothing. Skip the practice entirely on tired, gritty, or irritated eyes — the work assumes comfortable, healthy tissue. Pair it, if you like, with habits eyes appreciate anyway: stepping back from screens, blinking deliberately, letting the gaze rest on something far away. Many people anchor the routine to washing the face in the evening, when lenses are already out and hands already clean. Consistency, not vigor, is what self-care traditions actually ask for — nowhere more than here.

Treat the exercise as a minute of attention rather than a treatment session. Across weeks you may notice the area feeling less tense, the pause itself restful, or nothing shifting except that you spent a quiet minute caring for your own face. Every one of those outcomes fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — firmer pressure, longer sessions, drifting nearer the globe to chase a stronger sensation. Around the eyes the usual advice inverts: the moment a practice starts feeling like more, it has become less than safe. Return to lightness, or close the day’s practice altogether.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

This point carries heavier recorded cautions than nearly all of its neighbors, and the licensed record states them plainly: do not apply moxibustion here, and approach professional needle work only with great care — steering clear of the eyeball and refraining from vigorous lifting, thrusting, or rotating manipulation. Those warnings belong chiefly to professional technique, but they encode a truth every reader should absorb: between the skin and the eye itself there is almost nothing, and this is ground where force reaches vulnerable structures quickly. The moxa prohibition is equally part of the point’s permanent record — heat applications were ruled out at this address — a reminder that some locations simply decline certain therapies outright. Home practice honours the spirit of all of it by staying superficial, staying light, and staying brief.

For fingertip self-care the rules condense to a short list. Contact stays feather-light and strictly superficial, through the closed eyelid, on the soft tissues at the socket’s edge — never on the eyeball itself, and fingernails stay out of the arrangement entirely. Skip the point during any active eye condition: infection, inflammation, styes, injury, or the recovery period after any eye surgery or procedure, until ordinary activity is cleared by the treating clinician. Stop at once on any sharp, stinging, or worsening sensation, and do not retry that day. Anyone living with glaucoma, a retinal condition, or another diagnosed eye disease should ask their eye-care professional before adding any pressure practice near the eyes at all — for some conditions even gentle pressure around the globe is unwise.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt professional care — not a pressure routine — for sudden changes in vision, eye pain, persistent redness or discharge, double vision, flashes, or any symptom that frightens you. Persistent watering, drooping, or other lasting facial symptoms belong with a qualified clinician promptly, since several causes need timely assessment that no self-care practice can supply. The container imagery is lovely, but the safest gift Chengqi offers most people is a slower, kinder acquaintance with their own face — and a clear sense of when to place care in trained hands.
