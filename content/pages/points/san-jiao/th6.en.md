Zhigou, the Branch Ditch, is the sixth station of the San Jiao channel, resting in the long groove between the two forearm bones on the back of the forearm, three cun above the wrist crease. Classical practice reached for it when voice, ears, flanks, or bowels felt stuck. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it gently.

## Where Zhigou Sits on the Forearm {#where-it-sits}

Turn the hand so the back of the forearm faces up, and the neighborhood comes into view. The dataset places Zhigou on the dorsum of the forearm, on the straight line connecting Yangchi (TH4) at the wrist with the tip of the elbow, three cun above the dorsal transverse wrist crease, between the radius and the ulna. A cun is the traditional proportional unit of Chinese point-location, taken from the body being measured rather than from any ruler: the width of the person’s own thumb counts as roughly one cun, so measurements scale naturally from child to adult. Three cun above the wrist crease therefore means three thumb-widths of your own hand climbed up that line — still comfortably in the lower stretch of the forearm, well before its middle.

The geography explains the name before any commentary does. Two bones share the forearm: the radius on the thumb side, the ulna on the little-finger side, and between them runs a long, shallow lane of soft tissue that the fingertips can trace like a path between fences. Zhigou rests inside that lane, partway up. Under the skin here lie layers of extensor muscle doing their ordinary work, and beneath them the firm bridging tissues that bind the two bones together. Nothing delicate lies close to the surface on this stretch — no hollow organs, no glands, nothing that earns a stern written warning in the licensed record — which makes the dorsal forearm among the more forgiving ground on the body for gentle, patient touch.

Neighboring points make a handy cross-check. Waiguan (TH5) occupies the same bone-to-bone corridor two cun below Zhigou’s level, closer to the wrist; Huizong (TH7) shares Zhigou’s own three-cun height but sits about one finger-breadth away toward the little-finger side, hugging the edge of the ulna. So the corridor itself carries the channel upward step by step, while the ulnar margin belongs to a different address. If a search wanders too far toward the little finger and meets bone, it has overshot into Huizong’s neighborhood; if it slides onto the hard rise of the thumb-side bone, it has drifted off the line entirely. Between those two errors lies the shallow groove the dataset describes.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Set yourself up before you search. Sit at a table and rest the left forearm on the surface with the back of the hand facing the ceiling, palm down, elbow softly bent, shoulders easy — the whole arm heavy and unguarded. Find Yangchi first: at the back of the wrist, right at the crease, most people feel a broad hollow where the tissues dip over the joint; that hollow is your starting marker. From there, sight an imaginary straight line toward the pointed bony tip you can feel at the back of the bent elbow. That line is the road; everything happens along it.

Measure with your own hand. Lay the four fingers of the right hand together, edges touching, and place their combined width along the top of the forearm just above the dorsal wrist crease, keeping the fingers aligned with the wrist-to-elbow line. Traditional measuring counts this four-finger span as three cun, which lands almost exactly at the recorded position. Press lightly as you arrive: many people notice a faint, dull awareness at the true spot and little a finger-width away, and comparing the two forearms side by side sharpens the picture further. The confirming texture is the lane itself — the fingertip should settle into soft ground with the firm rise of a bone visible on either horizon, leaning toward neither.

Two slips account for most misses. The first is measuring from the wrong crease: the front of the wrist wears its own creases, and starting from the palm side shifts every measurement inward, clean off the back-of-the-arm line. The second is sideways drift — out over the ulnar margin, where the neighbor point waits one finger-breadth away, or up onto the ridge of the thumb-side bone. When in doubt, reset rather than improvise: find the dorsal crease, find the wrist hollow, lay four fingers up the line once, slowly. Precision matters less than patience; the search itself teaches you the lay of your own forearm.

## Reading the Name: Branch Ditch {#name-and-meaning}

The name reads plainly once the ground is known. Zhi 支 carries the sense of a branch — an offshoot dividing from a main stem, and by extension the limbs themselves — and classical commentators have heard in it both the branching of the channels and the plain fact that the point lives on a limb. Gou 溝 is a ditch or groove, the carved channel that lets water move. Rendered freely, Zhigou is the Branch Ditch. The fit with the geography needs no stretch: between the two forearm bones runs exactly such a long shallow groove, and the point rests inside it, partway up. Names of this kind double as maps; whoever coined it had fingers that already knew the terrain.

Pinyin renders the name Zhīgōu, and both syllables ride the same melody: a flat, high first tone on zhi and the same flat, high first tone on gou — the macrons in the recorded spelling mark both vowels as level and sustained. The hanzi pair 支溝 is compact and visually distinct, easy to memorise at a glance. Like so many points named for landscape and architecture — gates, pools, valleys, courtyards, ditches — the metaphor does genuine work rather than decorating. A ditch implies direction: water entering at one end and leaving at the other, movement with a job to do.

The water imagery suits the point’s traditional office. The channel it serves is named for the san jiao — the triple burner, the classical division of the trunk into upper, middle, and lower regions through which qi and fluids were taught to circulate — and a ditch-name on that channel of all channels reads less like decoration than like doctrine: a place associated with helping what has stalled begin to move again. Whether or not the original namer reasoned so neatly, generations of practitioners met the name and the indications together, and the pairing shaped how the point was remembered and passed on.

## A River Station on a Fire Channel {#jing-river-station}

Classical teaching sorts the points nearest the hands and feet into a celebrated ladder of five transporting stations, imaged as a river running from its source to the sea: a well at the fingertip where the flow barely trickles, then a spring, a stream, a river, and finally a sea near the elbow, where the flow gathers deep and wide. Zhigou holds the river rung of the San Jiao channel — the stage at which, in the traditional picture, the qi of the channel moves in steady volume along a defined bed.

A second layer of classical bookkeeping assigns each transporting station a phase of the five elements, and on the yang channels — the outward-facing lines of the arms and legs — the river stations belong to fire. The San Jiao channel itself is filed among the fire channels in the traditional scheme, so its river point carries, in the old reckoning, fire upon fire. None of this describes laboratory physiology; it is the tradition’s own filing system, and filing systems mattered because they told generations of practitioners what a point was for before any list of indications was opened.

Taken together, the images sketch why classical practice reached for this spot when movement had failed: a river station on a fire channel, lying in a groove named for flowing water. Sudden loss of voice, bowels gone quiet, an oppressive weight across the chest or ribs — the traditional reading treats each as a kind of stopping, and reaches for stations associated with abundant onward flow. This is the framework explaining itself, offered here as cultural understanding rather than as a claim about how bodies behave under modern examination.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The licensed record lists seven indications for Zhigou: sudden loss of voice, deafness, tinnitus, hypochondriac pain, constipation, cervical scrofula, and shoulder and arm pain. Read as tradition rather than promise, the list clusters naturally. One cluster gathers the ears and the voice; another pairs the flank with the bowels; a final pair covers the arm the point lives on and one old category of neck complaint. Each cluster makes sense against the channel map and the triple-burner teaching, taken in turn.

The ears come first and most obviously. Classical texts trace the San Jiao channel from the ring finger up the back of the hand and arm, over the elbow, across the shoulder, and up the side of the neck, curling past the ear before ending near the temple — the route the registry summarises as running from the ring finger up the outer arm to the temple. Deafness and tinnitus stand squarely along that passage, and the voice joins them: the same classical course brushes the cheek and jaw region, and commentators linked sudden loss of voice to the channel’s headward journey. Modern readers should hold one distinction firmly: sudden hearing change or voice loss today is a reason to see a clinician promptly, whatever the old records noted.

The flank-and-bowel pair belongs to the triple burner itself. In the traditional frame the san jiao supervises the movement of qi and fluids through the trunk’s three regions — chest, midriff, and lower abdomen — and discomfort at the hypochondrium, the soft flesh below the ribs at the side, was read as that movement binding up; constipation likewise, the lower region’s traffic stalling. A river-named station on the supervising channel became a classical reach for both complaints, the name and the use reinforcing one another. These are the tradition’s internal mechanics, documented for understanding — not assertions about digestion or anatomy as modern medicine maps them.

The last pair spans old and new concerns. Shoulder and arm pain needs little explanation: the channel runs the length of the limb, and local ache was always fair game for local points. Cervical scrofula is the gravest item — an old umbrella term for nodular swellings of the neck, historically approached through points whose courses pass that region. Here conservatism matters most: a new or persistent neck lump belongs with a doctor promptly, because several distinct causes can look alike and must be told apart. And the standing honesty clause applies throughout: these indications are transcribed from a licensed dataset and classical literature for cultural and historical understanding, not as claims that pressing the point treats disease. Where research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and heavily context-dependent, and evidence tied to any single point remains thin.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle in: seated, left forearm resting back-of-hand-up on a cushion or the table’s edge, shoulders released. Bring the pads of two or three fingers — never the tips, never a knuckle — onto the groove and begin with simple contact, letting the hand learn the texture beneath. After a few slow breaths add small circles, letting the skin glide over the tissue rather than dragging it. Aim for a sensation of pleasant dullness or mild spreading warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, matched loosely to the exhale so each circle rides the breath out; then swap arms and repeat, keeping the two sessions symmetrical.

Quality outranks quantity, as throughout the wellness register this page keeps. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact is the entire art; harder pressing adds risk and teaches the tissue nothing new. Warm the hands first if they run cold, since chilled muscle tightens under touch. Choose a quiet corner over a screen, and let the session double as a pause: inhale through the nose, notice the forearm beneath still fingers, lean the smallest degree of extra weight into the long exhale. People who keep such routines usually anchor them to existing habits — the morning kettle, an afternoon stretch — because regularity, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Keep expectations sized to the practice. Self-acupressure is a ritual of attention with a modest, honest scope: perhaps the forearm feels looser afterward, perhaps the pause itself is the benefit, perhaps nothing measurable happens at all — every one of those outcomes is legitimate. What falls outside the scope is escalation, pressing harder or longer to force a result, and any hope of the point standing in for care from a qualified practitioner. When curiosity pulls toward escalation, return to gentleness, or end the day’s session early.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

Begin with what the record says — and with what it does not. The licensed dataset records no specific cautions for Zhigou, and the point’s geography supports that calm: the dorsal forearm offers the fingertip muscle and a well-padded bone-channel, with no vulnerable structure waiting close beneath the skin. An empty caution field, though, describes the dataset’s silence rather than an exemption from care; the universal rules governing every point on this site apply here in full, and the guidance below leans deliberately conservative.

Practical rules, then. Keep contact superficial: finger pads, light pressure, no tools or devices, no pressing through pain — the lane between the two forearm bones is inhabited territory, and it asks for gentleness rather than determination. Skip the point wherever the skin is broken, rashy, bruised, or recently injured; during feverish or acute illness; and whenever pressing produces anything sharper than comfortable dullness. Anyone pregnant, or managing a condition or taking a medicine that affects clotting, skin fragility, or sensation, should ask a qualified practitioner before adding new point work. Sharp, electric, or radiating feeling under the fingers means stopping immediately and leaving the point alone for the rest of the day.

Finally, the referral lines, drawn plainly. New hearing loss, one-sided or sudden ringing in the ears, or a voice that fails without an obvious passing cold deserves prompt professional attention; so do constipation that persists or changes character, any new lump in the neck, and flank pain that lingers or worsens. Acupressure can sit alongside such care as a comfort practice; it cannot precede or replace it. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner, and this page is educational only — the Branch Ditch offers most people a better acquaintance with their own forearm, treated kindly, and that alone is worth the visit.
