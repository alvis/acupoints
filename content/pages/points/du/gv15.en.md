Yamen, the Gate of Muteness, stands at the top of the nape, on the body’s midline a half-thumb-width above the rear hairline. Classical practice reached for it when voice and speech faltered. Because vital structures lie close beneath, every technique in this guide stays deliberately at the surface.

## Where Yamen Sits at the Top of the Nape {#where-it-sits}

The dataset places Yamen on the nape — the back of the neck where it meets the skull — standing on the posterior midline, half a cun directly above the midpoint of the rear hairline, and lying below the first cervical vertebra. A cun, as ever in this system, is a proportional measure borrowed from the body being examined rather than a fixed length: a full cun is conventionally taken as the width of a thumb at its knuckle, so half a cun is roughly half a thumb-width. The rear hairline midpoint means the centre of the hairline at the back of the head, where hair gives way to neck skin; because hairlines sit higher or lower from person to person, the landmark starts the search, and fingertip confirmation finishes it.

Two features set this location apart from most on the channel. First, it is a single point: the midline carries no left-right pair, so there is one Yamen, centred, shared equally by both halves of the neck. Second, the terrain is unusually delicate. Unlike the mid-back, where bony ridges offer themselves to be counted, the upper neck hides its vertebrae deep beneath layered muscle, and the space under the skin here guards the passage between trunk and skull. Nothing about that geography forbids a light fingertip visit — but it argues, already at the locating stage, for the lightest possible touch and zero appetite for firm probing.

The neighbourhood confirms the address. One full cun directly above the same hairline midpoint, the catalogue stations Fengfu (GV16), the du channel’s better-known nape point, in the hollow beneath the skull’s rear prominence; Yamen therefore sits midway along the short vertical step between the hairline and Fengfu. Measured from the other direction, the next midline station below waits much farther down at the base of the neck. Practically, the search window is generous: even an imprecise half-thumb lands within a finger-width of true, and the twin reference points above and below tidy up the remainder.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit upright on a firm chair, feet flat, shoulders heavy, and let the chin dip slightly toward the chest — a small bow lifts the rear hairline into easy reach. Trace the centre of the hairline at the back of your head with a fingertip until you are confident of its midpoint. Rest the pad of your index or middle finger there, then glide straight upward along the central seam of the neck about half a thumb-width, and settle wherever the pad comes to rest — usually a soft, slightly sheltered flat or a shallow hollow. That spot, on the midline, is the location the record describes.

Before any real attention is paid, run the quiet confirmations. Check the midline: your finger should sit over the spine’s central groove, not on the taut cords of muscle that flank it. Compare sensation with a point a finger-width off-centre — many people register a faint, dull awareness at the true spot and almost nothing beside it. Then cross-check from above: slide up a full thumb-width from the hairline to find Fengfu, recognisable as the tender hollow beneath the skull’s rear bump, and come down half that distance again. When both routes agree, you have it; when they disagree, repeat each slowly rather than splitting the difference by feel alone.

The classic mistakes are predictable. Hair thickness fools the eye, and many people start from an imaginary hairline an inch too high; if the search feels wrong, borrow a mirror or a helper to see where the hair actually ends at the centre-back. Head tilt changes everything: the measured gap shifts as the head nods, so choose one comfortable position and stay in it. Fingers also drift off-centre unnoticed — return to the central seam whenever in doubt. Above all, conduct the whole hunt at feather weight: nothing in this neighbourhood rewards pressure, and a search that leaves the neck sore has already pressed too hard. Two patient passes beat ten firm ones.

## Reading the Name: Gate of Muteness {#name-and-meaning}

The characters carry the story plainly. Ya 啞 — drawn with the mouth radical beside 亞 — means mute, unable to speak; simplified texts write it 哑. Men 門, the gate, means what English expects: gate, doorway, threshold. Read together, Yamen is the gate of muteness, and the reason the name fits is visible in the record itself, whose leading entries concern a tongue too stiff to speak and a voice that vanishes suddenly. Classical medicine named points the way towns name gates: after the traffic they handle. Here, the traffic was speech.

The dataset adds a second credential: the category crossing-point. Classical teaching describes certain stations on the vessels as crossings — places where channels meet, mingle, and exchange rather than merely pass. Which lines exactly converge at this spot, the older literature discusses in its own idiom; the licensed record here simply logs the class, and this page follows the record rather than reconstructing the debate. What can be said safely concerns the setting: the du vessel, the governing line that climbs the body’s midline and gathers the yang channels upon it, runs through this nape corridor on its way into the head — the seat classical thinking assigned to mind and the speech it governs. A gate on that road earns its name.

Pinyin writes the name Yǎmén, and the tones deserve a moment. Ya is carried by a third tone — the falling-then-rising dip, like a slow nod — while men rides a rising second tone that lifts at the end, the tone of asking. These are dictionary-checked against the catalogue’s own spelling and worth trusting over the ear: learners routinely flatten both. On paper, the traditional characters 啞門 appear in simplified texts as 哑门, with the same sounds and senses. As with the many gates of the channel maps — wind gates, life gates, spirit gates — the metaphor works for its living: a gate implies both a lock and a latch, a place where passage can fail and be attended.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The record lists six: stiffness of the tongue with inability to speak, sudden loss of voice, headache, stiff neck, epilepsy, and hysteria. Take the first pair in the classical idiom. Tongue stiffness describes speech blocked at its root — words present in the mind, jammed at the doorway. Sudden loss of voice covers a voice that departs abruptly rather than fraying slowly. Headache and stiff neck need little translation, and their presence follows the geography: the point stands where neck becomes skull, on the governing vessel whose course runs up the spine’s midline into the head. Discomfort along that corridor was read as the channel’s own territory, and its stations were the natural addresses.

The last two names belong to an older vocabulary and demand careful handling. Epilepsy in the classical records gathered convulsions, seizure-like fits, and the sudden collapses described across the old literature; hysteria — a term modern medicine has retired — collected dramatic disturbances of emotion, behaviour, and speech that swept over a person. These are pre-modern sorting boxes, not diagnoses, and this page presents them as history, never as guidance. Their modern descendants — a first seizure, abrupt loss of speech or voice, sudden confusion — are precisely the situations emergency medicine exists for, and no self-applied pressure belongs in their company. Anyone living with a seizure condition follows their physician’s plan; a point page adds nothing to it.

So the honest summary repeats itself: these six entries are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and discussed in classical literature, offered for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats anything, and where modern research grazes the topic at all, reviews suggest effects are modest, context-dependent, and poorly mapped to any single point. Hold the tradition lightly and the safety rules tightly — at no point more literally than at this one.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

Given the address, home practice here is deliberately modest — closer to attentive contact than to massage. Sit with the head comfortably level and rest one fingertip pad, not a tip or a knuckle, on the point. Begin with contact alone through several slow breaths; if you continue at all, add circles smaller and lighter than seem necessary, letting the intention stay in the skin and the thinnest surface layer. Think in tens of seconds rather than minutes — ten to twenty unhurried breaths make a complete session — and once a day is plenty. The sensation to expect, if any arrives, is a faint warmth or a dull settling; anything sharper ends the session on the spot.

Many readers will prefer the honest alternatives, and this page recommends them freely. The thick muscular slopes flanking the midline — the trapezius territory running from the tops of the shoulders toward the skull — tolerate pressure happily and carry none of this point’s caution load; a few minutes of unhurried kneading there serves a tired neck far better than any amount of midline attention. Or skip technique altogether: a warmed palm rested over the whole nape for a dozen slow breaths is a complete practice, and arguably the wisest one available at this address. Whichever route you take, anchor it to something habitual — the evening wind-down, the end of the commute — because regularity is what self-care traditions actually reward.

Whatever form the session takes, treat it as a pause of attention rather than an intervention. Notice the neck unclenching as the day’s screen posture releases; notice breathing deepen as the shoulders drop; notice, perhaps, only that ninety seconds passed in which someone attended to their own body kindly. All of that sits squarely inside what self-acupressure honestly offers. Outside it lies escalation: pressing harder to force a result, stretching sessions to justify the effort, or reaching for tools to amplify what fingers deliver. At this particular gate, the discipline of stopping early is not timidity — it is the entire skill.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

This point carries one of the sternest caution records on the channel, and its substance deserves restating plainly: the licensed dataset warns practitioners not to needle deeply here, because the medulla oblongata — the lowermost portion of the brainstem, carrying the traffic between brain and body — lies beneath this small patch of nape, and going deep risks injuring vital centres. The same record rules out moxibustion at this point, noting that classical literature associated the practice with hearing damage. Whatever one makes of the ancient association, the practical meaning survives intact: this is ground where depth of any kind belongs to trained hands in clinical settings only.

Translated into household rules, the caution reads: fingertip pads only; pressure light enough not to disturb a sleeping cat; no elbows, knuckles, tools, massage guns, or devices of any kind; and no heat remedies aimed at the point, from hot-water bottles to heat pads — the same conservatism the record applies to moxibustion. Step away entirely after any neck injury, whiplash, or neck surgery until a clinician clears ordinary activity; with diagnosed cervical spine conditions, dizziness or balance trouble, or numbness and tingling in the arms, leave this point to professionals outright. Pregnant readers should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation at any moment means stop at once, and the day’s practice is over.

Finally, the red flags — and note how they rhyme with the point’s own historic portfolio. Sudden inability to speak, a voice that vanishes without warning, drooping on one side of the face, weakness or numbness down one side, a seizure, the worst headache of one’s life: each calls for emergency services immediately, not for a pressure routine. These were the very complaints classical practice once brought to this gate, and they are exactly the ones modern medicine must judge swiftly. For everything less urgent that persists — a stubborn stiff neck, hoarseness outstaying its welcome, headaches gathering week upon week — the destination is a licensed practitioner. This page is educational only, and self-care is a companion to medicine, never a substitute.
