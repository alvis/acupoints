Naohu, the Door of the Brain, sits at the very top of the nape, in the hollow just above the bony bump at the back of the skull where the Du vessel passes on its long climb toward the crown. Classical practice reached for it when headache, stiff neck, or dizziness weighed on a person. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it gently.

## Where Naohu Sits on the Back of the Head {#where-it-sits}

Naohu occupies one of the most sheltered addresses on the whole channel map: the dead centre of the back of the head, where skull gives way to neck. The dataset places it on the head, 2.5 cun directly above the midpoint of the posterior hairline, 1.5 cun above Fengfu (GV16), in the depression on the upper border of the external occipital protuberance. A cun is the traditional proportional measure of Chinese point-location, taken from the body being examined rather than from a ruler — the width of the thumb joint being the classic unit — so the same written distance lands slightly differently on different heads. On the midline there is no left-right ambiguity at all: whatever your proportions, the point sits exactly halfway between the two sides, straight above the highest hollow of the nape.

The external occipital protuberance deserves a proper introduction, because it is the landmark everything here hangs from. Run your hand over the back of your skull and, near the bottom, you will meet a definite ridge of bone — sometimes a pronounced hook, sometimes a broad gentle swell — where the occipital bone thickens and the strong muscles of the neck take hold. It is a feature nearly everyone can find, which is precisely why classical location texts anchored this point to it. The depression the record describes lies on the upper border of that ridge: not on the face of the bone itself, and not yet down among the muscles of the nape, but in the shallow shelf where the back of the skull rounds away toward the crown.

Neighbors help confirm the address. Below Naohu, deeper toward the hairline, lies Fengfu (GV16) — the dataset measures the gap at one and a half cun — and above it the midline continues its climb toward the crown, carrying the remaining points of the Du vessel upward. Reading the three levels together gives a vertical ladder on a single invisible line: the hollows near the hairline, then the ridge-top point, then the road onward to the vertex. Because every rung of that ladder sits on the midline, losing your place almost always means drifting sideways rather than up or down, and centring yourself again takes only a moment.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit upright on a firm chair, shoulders released, and let the head hang level or bow forward a little so the muscles at the back of the neck soften. Reach your right hand over the top of your head and rest the finger pads — index and middle together — at the back of the skull. Walk straight down the midline until you feel the ridge of bone described above: the external occipital protuberance, unmistakable once noticed. Now creep the fingertips upward onto the top edge of that ridge and feel for the shallow depression that opens there. That hollow, centred on the midline just above the bony prominence, is the place the dataset describes as Naohu.

A few quiet confirmations earn their keep before any pressure begins. Check the midline twice: divide your attention between the fingertips until you are satisfied the contact point sits equidistant from both ears. Compare sensation with feather-light touches slightly above, below, and beside the spot — many people notice a faint dull awareness right at the hollow and little elsewhere. Let the head nod slowly while the pads stay put, feeling the tissue glide over the ridge beneath them. If what lies under the fingers is smooth bare bone with no hollow at all, you are still on the face of the protuberance; slide upward until the bone falls away into the shelf above.

Two mistakes account for most mis-finds. The first is stopping too low: the softer depressions farther down, toward the hairline, mark the direction of Fengfu (GV16) and the upper nape, not Naohu. The second is wandering off-centre, where the ridge simply continues to the left and right; the hollow exists only at the middle. Neither error matters during a search — only before pressure — and both resolve themselves by returning to the start: find the bump, trace the midline, settle into the hollow above it. Take as long as the search needs; locating a point slowly is part of the practice, not a delay before it.

## Reading the Name: Door of the Brain {#name-and-meaning}

The two characters draw a vivid little picture. Nao 腦 means brain — the character sets a component long associated with the mind’s organ beside the flesh radical. Hu 戶 means door, and specifically the older kind: a single-leaved door for a room or household, as distinct from the grand double gates of a palace. Together, Naohu reads as the door of the brain, or the brain’s doorway. The image installs this small hollow on the back of the head as an entrance hall to the body’s most protected chamber — a threshold rather than a destination, a place where something passes through.

The name suits the geography the tradition drew. Classical texts describe the Du vessel climbing the spine and the midline of the head, and old commentary pictures it drawing near to the brain along this stretch; the catalogue reinforces the picture by recording Naohu as a crossing-point, a place where more than one pathway was mapped onto the same ground. A door, after all, implies traffic: comings and goings between the channel world of the body surface and the interior. Whether one reads such maps literally or as metaphor, they explain why a point named for the brain stood high in classical discussions of the head.

Pinyin renders the name Nǎohù. The first syllable carries the dipping third tone — nao begins mid-low, dips, and rises again; the second carries the falling fourth tone, hu spoken sharply downward from a high start. The hanzi pair 腦戶 is compact enough to memorise at a glance. Like so many points named for architecture — gates, courtyards, storehouses, doors — the metaphor does real work: it says the tradition treated this spot as a passage with an inside and an outside, not merely a dot on a map. The name is a small set of directions for the imagination.

## The Du Vessel and the Midline of Yang {#the-du-vessel}

Naohu belongs to the Du vessel — the Governing Vessel — the pathway that runs up the midline of the back and head and unites all the yang meridians. In the traditional picture it is the channel charged with overseeing yang qi: the bright, active, outward-facing half of the qi-and-blood economy. Its route could hardly be more exposed — straight up the spine, over the nape, across the back of the skull — and its points consequently read like milestones along the body’s central ridge, each guarding a pass on the way.

The vessel’s climb gives Naohu its context. Below it, the Du vessel threads the nape, where classical texts placed some of its most consequential stations; above it, the line continues over the crown, completing the circuit of the head. Naohu sits where that ascent crosses from the world of the neck into the world of the skull — the last shelf of the nape, the threshold of the vault. Classical descriptions add that the vessel links with the brain in this region, which is exactly the company the point-name keeps. Geography, name, and category all tell one story from three angles.

Traditional reasoning followed routes: where a channel travels, points along it were reached for complaints of that travel. A vessel running the entire midline of the back and head therefore made its head-points natural mentions when the head itself was troubled — stiffness rising from the neck, aches settled at the back of the skull, the swimming sensation of dizziness. None of this amounts to mechanism as modern medicine understands it; it is cartography-based inference inside a coherent old system. Appreciating the system’s internal logic matters more than adjudicating it, and the next section reports simply what the record lists.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: headache, stiff neck, dizziness, epilepsy, hypertension, and optic neuritis. Framed as tradition rather than promise, the first three sketch the point’s local portfolio. Headache gathering at the back of the head, stiffness where neck meets skull, the whirling unsteadiness classical texts gathered under dizziness — all belong to the territory Naohu overlooks, and all sat comfortably within midline-of-yang reasoning. Classical practice traditionally reached for such a point when discomfort pooled at the junction of head and neck, much as one might knock at the nearest door rather than cross the whole house.

The last three names sound strikingly modern, because they are: epilepsy, hypertension, and optic neuritis are current clinical labels carried in the dataset’s action list. Old records approached such territory with a different vocabulary — convulsions and seizures, sensations of pressure and surging in the head, dimmed or disturbed sight — and Naohu appears within those historic discussions. What matters for a reader today is the boundary, stated plainly: these are serious medical conditions belonging under the ongoing care of licensed clinicians. Their presence in a traditional record documents how the tradition catalogued the body; it offers nobody a reason to manage seizures, blood pressure, or eye disease anywhere but a clinic.

It bears repeating plainly: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats disease, and nothing in this guide replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Where modern research has touched self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and heavily context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition lightly, and hold the safety rules firmly.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the hollow found by the walkthrough above, settle in: seated, spine easy, head bowed a fraction so the nape softens. Because Naohu sits on the midline there is one point to serve, not a symmetrical pair — most people find one hand reaches it comfortably from above. Rest the pads of the index and middle fingers in the depression and begin with contact alone for a few slow breaths. Then add small, unhurried circles, letting the skin travel gently over the firm ground of the skull beneath. Aim for a pleasant spreading dullness or a faint warmth; anything sharp means lighten at once. A common home rhythm is one to two minutes at a stretch, once or twice daily, alternating hands if the reaching arm tires.

Quality outranks quantity here even more than usual, because the ground is bone. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; grinding pressure against the skull teaches the body nothing and risks days of soreness. Warm your hands first if they run cold. Choose a quiet corner rather than a screen, and let the practice double as a pause for breath: inhale through the nose, feel the head resting heavy in the supporting hand, ease the pressure on the long exhales. People who keep such routines usually anchor them to an existing habit — after washing the hair, while the morning drink cools — because regularity, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Treat the minute or two as a small ceremony of attention to a part of the body almost nobody ever touches kindly. Across weeks you might notice the nape feels less guarded, or that pausing there unties a little of the day’s accumulated clench — or nothing at all beyond the noticing itself, which also sits within the honest scope of self-acupressure. What lies outside that scope is escalation: pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a result. When curiosity pulls in that direction, return to gentleness, or simply close the practice for the day.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

This point carries a heavier safety annotation than most, and the licensed record states it without decoration. The dataset cautions: classically recorded as not to be needled or warmed by moxibustion; the literature warns moxibustion here causes deafness. Whatever one makes of an old warning in modern terms, the editorial duty is clear — the prohibition is preserved intact, given pride of place, and never argued away. Professional practitioners weigh such records within full training; nobody practising at home needs to weigh them at all, because home acupressure uses neither needles nor burning herbs. The record still earns its prominence here as a reminder that the back of the head has always been treated by the tradition as sensitive ground.

For fingertip self-care the working rules are correspondingly strict — stricter than at most points. Contact stays light and purely superficial: skin and the immediate tissue over solid bone, nothing driving inward, and no tools, devices, knuckles, or elbows. Leave the point alone entirely during any acute illness with headache or fever, during any episode of active dizziness, after any recent blow to the head or neck injury until a clinician clears ordinary activity, and over broken or irritated skin. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once and do not try again that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with seizures, blood-pressure conditions, or disorders of the eyes, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice — and should treat this point as one to approach last and lightest.

Certain symptoms never belong to a self-care routine at all; they belong to urgent medical assessment, promptly. Seek emergency care for the sudden worst headache of one’s life, headache with fever and a stiffening neck, any seizure, fainting or near-fainting alongside dizziness, slurred speech or weakness on one side, or a sudden change in vision. These overlap deliberately with the serious conditions in the traditional record — the same gravity that placed them there keeps them in clinical hands now. For less dramatic but persistent complaints — recurring headaches, ongoing unsteadiness, lingering neck stiffness — make a licensed practitioner the next step rather than a longer pressing session.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. The universal disclaimer that accompanies every page here applies with full force. If the doorway imagery lingers, let it do useful work: the door of the brain is best honoured from the outside, touched lightly, and opened — when opening is called for at all — by trained hands. The safest gift Naohu offers most people is a better acquaintance with the back of their own skull, and a firmer instinct for when a symptom leaves the province of self-care entirely.
