Fengfu, the Wind Mansion, stands at the top of the midline at the back of the neck, in the hollow beneath the bony prominence of the skull. Classical practice reached for it in headache, stiff neck, and dizziness, and it carries one of the sternest cautions in the collection. This guide covers finding it, reading its name, and approaching it with restraint.

## Where Fengfu Sits on the Nape {#where-it-sits}

Place a fingertip at the centre of the back of your neck and slide upward. You are traveling a shallow groove flanked by thick columns of muscle, and at its top the groove ends against something unmistakable: a rounded bony prominence at the back of the skull, easy to feel on nearly everyone. The dataset places Fengfu on the nape, one cun directly above the midpoint of the posterior hairline, below the external occipital protuberance — that rounded prominence — in the depression between the two trapezius muscles. A cun is the traditional proportional measure of point location, taken from the person rather than from a ruler; one cun approximates the width of your own thumb. Because hairlines sit at different heights on different heads, the prominence above and the groove below are the steadier guides.

What rests beneath this particular patch of skin earns the point its reputation. Below the surface lie ligament and the uppermost fibers of neck muscle; beyond them the spine finishes its upward journey, and the dataset’s caution names what follows: the medulla oblongata lies beneath — the lowermost portion of the brainstem, tissue through which the body’s automatic business flows. Nothing about that geography invites vigor. Of all the points in this collection, few announce so plainly that force has no place here, and this page returns to that fact more than once.

Two neighbors share this crowded stretch of midline and help orient the search. Directly below sits Yamen (GV15), recorded half a cun above the midpoint of the posterior hairline; above rises Naohu (GV17), recorded one and a half cun above Fengfu, close against the upper border of the same bony prominence. All three live within a hand-span of one another, which is why classical location leans on measured relationships along a single vertical line: hairline midpoint, then Fengfu, then the prominence with Naohu beyond it.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit tall on a firm chair with the head balanced easily over the shoulders, neither tipped down toward a screen nor thrown back. Rest the pads of the index and middle fingers at the centre of the back of the neck and walk them slowly upward along the midline groove, letting each small stretch register. The fingers climb between two soft muscular ridges — the inner edges of the trapezius muscles — until they meet the hard rounded prominence at the skull’s lower back edge. From there, glide a fraction downward off the bone into the hollow that opens just beneath it, between the muscle ridges: that depression, below the prominence and centred on the midline, is the location the dataset describes.

A second route confirms the first. Find where hair meets skin at the centre of the back of the head, measure one thumb-width straight up, and see whether the two answers agree; on most heads they land within a finger-width of one another. Then compare left and right: sensation at a true midline point usually feels centred rather than lopsided, because the fingertip rests between the muscle columns instead of atop either. Many people notice a faint dull ache or spreading warmth at the spot and little feeling a thumb-width away. Everything here happens with the weight of a coin resting on skin, never the push of a thumb.

Three slips account for most misses. Landing on bare bone means settling onto the prominence itself rather than the hollow below it — retreat half a knuckle down. Drifting sideways places the finger on trapezius muscle, which feels meaty and one-sided instead of central. And trusting a low or uneven hairline can pull the search too far down; hairlines vary, so let the bony prominence arbitrate whenever the routes disagree. When the search stalls, the answer is never more pressure. It is stopping, resting the hands, and beginning again another day — a rule repeated here because the terrain asks for it.

## Reading the Name: Wind Mansion {#name-and-meaning}

Two characters build the name. Feng 風 is wind — the dictionary’s first sense, air in motion; pinyin renders it feng on the first tone, held high and level like a note sustained on a flute. Fu 府 is a mansion or official residence — the seat where a dignitary keeps house, by extension any repository where something important is stored and managed; it sounds on the third tone, a dipping fall-and-rise. Together, Fengfu reads as the Mansion of Wind: the place where wind arrives and is housed. The hanzi 風府 are simple enough to memorise at a glance.

Why hang wind at the top of the neck? Classical teaching treated wind as the most restless of the outside influences it watched for: ailments imagined under its banner were those that arrive suddenly, move from place to place, and shift their shape — headaches, dizziness, wandering stiffness among them. The same texts describe the nape as ground where such influences were thought to enter, and they picture the governing vessel climbing the spine along the midline, carrying yang up past this station into the head. The dataset records Fengfu among the crossing-points — stations where pathways are traditionally described as converging on the governing line. A mansion stood at the door; the name encodes the theory.

Said aloud, the name teaches its own pronunciation: Fēngfǔ — the first tone riding high and steady across feng, the third tone dipping and recovering on fu. Point names built from weather and architecture recur across the channels: gates, wells, valleys, pools, mansions. They are not decoration. Each compresses an argument about what the tradition believed happened at that coordinate — traffic concentrates at gates, water gathers in valleys, and wind, said the old physicians, gathers at the top of the neck. Learning to read the names is learning to read the system’s poetry of place.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: headache, stiff neck, dizziness, nosebleed, sore swollen throat, loss of voice, stroke, mania. The first three need least introduction, and they obey the oldest principle of point selection — a point tends its own neighborhood. A mansion set at the junction of head and neck stood naturally closest to the complaints of that junction: pain wrapping the head, a neck gone rigid, the reeling unsteadiness the old records grouped under dizziness. Classical practice reached for the governing line’s upper stations when such complaints presented, and Fengfu headed those discussions.

The remaining entries ask for historical imagination. Nosebleed, sore swollen throat, and loss of voice belonged, in the traditional frame, to influences moving upward through the body’s upper territory — channel logic placed the governing vessel’s course over crown and face, so upper-body manifestations entered its portfolio. Stroke and mania sound startling to modern ears; in the old records those words named frightening acute changes in movement, speech, consciousness, and mood that physicians of any era would recognise as emergencies. Tradition assigned them discussion at points along the governing line because the line ran nearest the brain; the assignments describe a worldview, not a promise, and nothing here modernizes them.

It bears repeating plainly: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered for cultural and historical understanding — not claims that pressing this point helps any condition. Where modern research has examined pressure-based practices broadly, reviews suggest modest, context-dependent effects, and evidence tied to any single point remains thin. The safety translation matters more than usual here. Sudden severe headache, weakness or numbness on one side, facial droop, slurred speech, confusion, or fainting call for emergency care immediately, not a pressure routine; hoarseness lasting beyond a few weeks, recurrent nosebleeds, or dizziness that persists deserve a clinician’s assessment promptly.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

Everything standard about home acupressure applies here in a gentler key. Sit comfortably with the head neutral and the shoulders released. Choose a single fingertip pad — never a knuckle, elbow, tool, or device — and rest it in the hollow found by the walkthrough above with no more force than a coin resting on skin. After a few slow breaths of simple contact, add the smallest circles you can make, letting the skin travel over the tissue beneath. Pleasant warmth or faint tingling is the ceiling of sensation; anything sharper means stopping at once. A minute or two suffices — ten to thirty unhurried breaths is plenty — and once daily is often enough.

Many readers prefer an even softer option, and it suits this point particularly well: cup one palm warmly at the back of the neck and simply hold it there, letting stillness and warmth do all the work while slow exhales lengthen. Pair either version with an easy posture reset — chin gently level, crown lifting, jaw softening — because this neighborhood responds kindly to release and badly to strain. Anchor the minute to an existing habit: after washing hands, before an afternoon stand-and-stretch. Consistency, not intensity, is the entire craft, and nowhere is that truer than at a point whose own record counsels restraint.

Keep the honest scope in view. Across weeks of practice you may notice the pauses themselves becoming restful, the shoulders lowering on cue, the breath dropping while your hand rests at the neck — all squarely within what self-acupressure offers. What stays outside the boundary: pressing harder to chase a stronger feeling, extending sessions to force results, or practising while any headache, dizziness, or neck complaint is actively flaring — those moments belong to observation and, where symptoms persist, to professional care. When curiosity pulls toward force at this address above all others, let the anatomy win the argument.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

This point carries one of the gravest caution records in the collection, and it deserves a slow reading. The dataset warns against deep professional technique at this site because the medulla oblongata lies beneath — the lowermost part of the brainstem — and it further records that moxibustion, the traditional warming of points with burning mugwort, is classically avoided here, with old literature associating it with loss of voice. Whatever one makes of the old warning, the portrait is consistent: every generation of the tradition treated this coordinate as ground for maximum conservatism. Translated into home practice, the lesson is starkly simple. Self-acupressure neither pierces nor burns anything — but at this site even pressure wears a shorter leash than anywhere else: light only, brief only, surface only.

Practical boundaries follow. Skip the point entirely during feverish illness, over broken or irritated skin, and for weeks after any neck injury, whiplash, or surgery until a clinician clears ordinary activity. Readers living with diagnosed conditions of the cervical spine, or with vascular or neurological diagnoses, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice, as should pregnant readers, following the general prudence that governs all point work. During practice itself, sharp, electric, or radiating sensation — and equally any sudden dizziness, nausea, or visual oddity — means stopping immediately and not resuming that day. Older readers and anyone frail may prefer the palm-resting option exclusively; there is no requirement to press this point at all.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, acupressure is self-care, and neither diagnoses nor treats anything. Certain signs end all home practice categorically and point to emergency care: the sudden worst headache of one’s life, weakness or numbness down one side, drooping face, garbled speech, new confusion, fainting, or a stiff neck arriving with fever. Persistent headache, ongoing dizziness, unexplained voice change, or neck pain that will not settle belong with a licensed practitioner promptly, medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. The safest gift Fengfu offers most visitors is a finer acquaintance with the top of their own neck, met with respect rather than force.
