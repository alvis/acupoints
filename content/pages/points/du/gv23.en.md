Shangxing — the Upper Star — stands on the exact centreline of the forehead, one cun above the middle of the front hairline, among the highest named points on the face. Classical practice reached here when headache, dizziness, or congested sinuses dimmed a person’s days. This guide walks through finding it by touch, reading its name, and pressing it gently and safely at home.

## Where Shangxing Sits on the Forehead {#where-it-sits}

The dataset locates Shangxing on the head, one cun directly above the midpoint of the anterior hairline — and almost every useful word in that sentence deserves unpacking. Directly above means straight up the body’s central seam, not angled toward either temple. The midpoint of the anterior hairline is the centre of the forehead where hair gives way to skin: trace upward from the bridge of the nose, through the slight plateau between the brows, and continue until the hairline crosses your path; the halfway mark of that crossing, measured left to right, is the anchor. A cun is the traditional proportional unit of Chinese point-location, taken from the person rather than a ruler — the width of the middle joint of the thumb being the handiest everyday version. One thumb-width straight up from the hairline’s centre: that is the whole address.

The neighbourhood confirms the address. According to the catalogue, Shenting (GV24) sits half a cun above the hairline — half a thumb-width nearer the brows, a step downhill from Shangxing — while Xinhui (GV22) lies at two cun, one full thumb-width beyond Shangxing toward the crown. The catalogue further records Xinhui as three cun in front of Baihui (GV20), which places Baihui five cun above the hairline and leaves Shangxing four cun short of the crown’s most famous point. All of these neighbours string along the du vessel — the Governing Vessel, which the site’s meridian notes describe running up the midline of the back toward the head, uniting all yang meridians. Standing anywhere on this seam, you are touching the vessel’s main street through the head.

What rests beneath the fingertips here is reassuringly solid. Skin and a thin layer of fascia lie over the frontalis muscle, whose fibres blend upward into the broad flat tendon of the scalp; beneath all of it spreads the frontal bone, one of the thickest, most even plates of the skull. Unlike points guarding delicate corridors elsewhere on the body, a midline forehead point offers the searching finger a firm, predictable floor. Nothing about this geography invites force — it simply means that gentle, patient contact meets with kind terrain. The point does not hide, does not shift with breathing, and does not ask to be hunted for; it waits on the seam, exactly where the old measurements promise.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit facing a mirror in good light and begin by finding the hairline’s centre. Rest the tip of one index finger on the bridge of your nose and draw it slowly upward, through the shallow plateau between the eyebrows, until the hairline crosses under the pad; adjust sideways until the finger sits evenly between the two temples — that centred spot is the midpoint of the anterior hairline. Now measure: place the pad of the other thumb just above the anchoring finger, joint-width by joint-width, climbing straight up the seam. One thumb-width above the hairline’s centre, settle your fingertip. You should be unmistakably on the midline, in territory where the forehead rounds gently back toward the crown.

A few quiet confirmations help before any pressing starts. Check the mirror again: the finger should look dead-centre, equidistant from both temples, because even a small sideways drift leaves the point entirely. Press feather-lightly and compare — most people notice a faint, dull awareness at the true spot and something vaguer half a thumb-width away in any direction. Move the face through a small expression, a raised brow or a frown: the skin slides a little over the bone, but the point itself does not travel. Cross-check with the neighbours whenever doubt creeps in — half a thumb-width downhill should bring Shenting within reach, and one more uphill toward Xinhui — and if the arithmetic feels off, return to the hairline and measure once, slowly.

The classic mistakes are few and worth naming. Drifting off the midline is the most common; the fix is the mirror and the habit of returning to the nose-bridge line. Confusing neighbours is the second: Shenting hugs the hairline and feels almost part of the fringe itself, while Xinhui sits noticeably deeper into the scalp — if your spot feels too close to the hair, or too buried in it, recount from the centre. Finally, hairlines differ: a strongly receding hairline blurs the landmark the measurement depends on. In that case search gently for the centred, faintly responsive place the sequence describes, hold the intention lightly, and let a qualified practitioner confirm the placement at your next visit — certainty is worth one professional minute.

## Reading the Name: Upper Star {#name-and-meaning}

Two characters carry the whole image. Shang 上 means above or upper, and the character itself performs the idea: a short marker set upon a baseline, pointing skyward. Xing 星 means star, and builds the word visibly — the sun radical shining above sheng, a character of arising and growth — a small picture of light kindled in the dark. Joined, Shangxing reads naturally as the Upper Star or the Star Above: the one bright point standing high on the face’s centreline. Point names in this tradition are rarely decoration; they compress a teaching about position, character, or office into a couple of brushstrokes, and this one announces rank and altitude at once.

Commentators through the centuries have warmed to the astronomy. Classical teaching likens the head to heaven and the face below to the human and earthly realms, so a solitary point shining above the brow invites the evening-star reading: the first light to appear at dusk, stationed higher than everything around it. Others have connected the star’s brightness with the eyes and the clear nose occupying the territory just beneath — the very features the point’s traditional portfolio gathers around. However one renders the poetry, the practical moral is stable: this is a senior station on the vessel’s route through the head, set deliberately high, and the tradition treated high stations with correspondingly measured respect.

Pinyin renders the name Shàngxīng, and the tones carry their own instruction — checked against the dictionary rather than recalled from memory: shàng falls squarely on the fourth tone, a decisive descending step, while xīng rides the first tone, held high and perfectly level, like the star it names. The hanzi pair is compact enough to take in at a glance, and it joins a family of head-point names drawn from courts, courtyards, and heavens. The naming logic rewards attention: where a limb point might borrow a valley or a stream, the points of the face’s centreline borrow rank — and Upper Star wears its elevation openly.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: headache, dizziness, sinusitis, nasal congestion and nosebleed, red swollen eyes, neurasthenia, malaria, and febrile disease. Framed as tradition rather than promise, the first pair forms the natural heart of the list. Classical texts grouped head complaints by where they showed themselves, and a point planted on the forehead’s centreline stood squarely in discussions of ache gathering at the front of the head and of the swaying, unsteady sensations the records call dizziness. Classical teaching regards the head as the meeting place of the yang, the high ground toward which qi and blood ascend — a summit post, naturally consulted for weather at the top. To say classical practice traditionally reached for Shangxing in such moments describes the consultation habits the literature preserves, not a promise that pressing the point settles any particular headache tonight.

The next cluster concerns the nose and eyes: sinusitis, nasal congestion and nosebleed, and red swollen eyes. Geography does the explaining — Shangxing overlooks the bridge of the nose, and traditional reasoning placed complaints of the upper face within reach of points along the face’s centreline, the way a magistrate tends the district outside his window. Congested noses, bleeding noses, and reddened, swollen eyes recur together across the records of the head’s midline points, and the starlight imagery of the name even chimes with the bright-eye theme. All of it is offered in the same key: traditional frameworks for reading the body, documented for cultural understanding rather than asserted as outcomes.

The final entries carry their era on their sleeves. Neurasthenia is a historical label — still met in parts of East Asian diagnostic vocabulary — for a wide band of nervous exhaustion: weariness, unrefreshing sleep, frayed concentration. Malaria and febrile disease record the classical consultation around intermittent and heat-driven illnesses; in the modern world, malaria and significant fever are infections that belong promptly with medical care, and the record preserves history, not a home protocol. It bears repeating plainly: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats disease. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located, settle in: seated, spine tall but unforced, elbows resting somewhere supportive, hands warmed if the room is cool. Shangxing is a single midline point, so the working hand differs slightly from paired limb points — one fingertip pad centred on the spot, or the index and middle pads set side by side over it. Begin with contact alone for a few slow breaths, then add small, unhurried circles, letting the skin glide over the bone beneath rather than grinding against it. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or mild warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes at a stretch, once or twice daily, each circle riding a slow exhale out.

Quality matters more than duration. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; hard jabbing teaches the body nothing and serves a tired forehead worse. Choose a quiet corner away from screens — the practice doubles as a pause for a brow worn tight by a day of looking down at work — and let the expectation rest on comfort and attention themselves, not on fixing anything. People who keep a routine often anchor it to an existing habit: after morning washing, before the afternoon slump, beside the evening wind-down. Consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually request, and a single remembered minute each day outranks a heroic session forgotten by Thursday.

Treat those minutes as a small ceremony of attention rather than a treatment session. Notice what changes across weeks, honestly: perhaps the brow feels less guarded, perhaps the pause itself becomes the point, perhaps nothing shifts except that you spend ninety seconds a day attending to your own face. Every one of those outcomes sits inside the honest scope of self-acupressure. What does not sit inside it is escalation — pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a result the practice never promised. When curiosity pulls that way, return to gentleness, or simply stop for the day and let the star stay where it is.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

An honest safety section starts with the record: the licensed dataset lists no specific caution for this entry, and the echoed field above preserves that emptiness deliberately. Absence of a recorded warning is not an invitation to force — it is the dataset speaking about professional technique, while this page speaks about home self-care, where common sense and the anatomy anyone can feel set the rules. Here the terrain helps: beneath the fingertip lies the thick, even plate of the frontal bone, and gentle surface contact has nothing fragile to wander into. The rules below exist not because the spot is treacherous but because good habits travel well from point to point.

For fingertip acupressure the practical rules are short. Keep contact light and strictly superficial — pads of the fingers, never nails, knuckles, elbows, or tools. Skip the point over broken, irritated, freshly waxed, or recently shaved skin; set the practice aside after any knock to the head, following facial procedures, and during any illness with fever, until ordinary activity is clearly comfortable again. Stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation, and do not retry that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone managing a chronic condition, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice — a minute of advice that always costs less than a guess.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, acupressure is self-care, and neither diagnoses nor treats. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for a sudden severe headache or the worst headache of your life; headache with stiff neck, fever, confusion, or disturbed vision; repeated fainting or dizziness that will not settle; a nosebleed that will not stop; a red, painful eye with any change of sight; or high or lingering fever. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner, medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. The star imagery is lovely, but the safest gift Shangxing offers most people is a steadier acquaintance with their own forehead, touched kindly.
