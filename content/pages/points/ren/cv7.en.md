Yinjiao, the Yin Intersection, rests on the front midline of the lower belly, one cun below the navel. Classical practice reached for it when the belly ached, the bowels ran loose, or water gathered where it should have flowed freely. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it gently, with the careful limits this deeply placed point deserves.

## Where Yinjiao Sits on the Lower Belly {#where-it-sits}

The dataset fixes the position in one measured line: on the lower abdomen, on the anterior midline, 1 cun below the centre of the umbilicus. Each term translates cleanly. The lower abdomen is the soft stretch of belly beneath the navel. The anterior midline is the vertical seam running down the exact centre of the front of the torso, equidistant from left and right at every height. The umbilicus is the navel, and its centre means the middle of that hollow rather than its rim. One cun below sets the height: starting at the very middle of the navel and travelling straight down toward the pubic bone, you arrive after about one thumb-width of distance. A cun is the proportional measure of Chinese point-location, taken from the person being examined rather than from a ruler, and for proportional purposes one cun is commonly taken as the width of the recipient’s own thumb.

What lies beneath deserves gentleness. Skin and a soft layer of fat sit over the linea alba, the pale fibrous seam where the two columns of the abdominal wall knit together down the body’s centre; beyond it begins the abdominal cavity, where loops of the small intestine ordinarily rest close beneath the wall in this part of the belly. Nothing bony shields the area — unlike the chest, no ribs stand overhead here — so the wall is supple and the space behind it soft and living. This is the plain anatomical reason every instruction on this page keeps contact superficial. Self-palpation needs only the lightest fingertip awareness; the landmarks of this region announce themselves through patience, not pressure.

The neighbourhood is crowded, because this stretch of midline carries a point at nearly every measured step. Directly above Yinjiao, at the centre of the navel itself, sits Shenque (CV8). Half a thumb-width further down lies Qihai (CV6), recorded one and a half cun below the navel; one more thumb-width brings you to Shimen (CV5) at two cun. Across the navel, mirrored on its upper side, Shuifen (CV9) — the Water Divide — waits one cun above. Reading the sequence downward from the navel gives a simple ladder: one cun Yinjiao, one-and-a-half Qihai, two Shimen. Keeping those steps distinct matters when you palpate, because a wandering thumb can slide from one point onto its neighbour without ever leaving the midline.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Lie back on a bed or a firm mat and bend the knees so the abdominal wall goes slack; a tensed belly hides its own landmarks. Find the centre of the navel with a fingertip, then rest the pads of your index and middle fingers immediately below it, on the vertical seam of the midline. Walk straight down the seam about one thumb-width — the width of the recipient’s own thumb, nail edge to nail edge — and let the pads settle where the seam still feels softly corded under the skin. That spot, plainly below the navel hollow and squarely on centre, is where the dataset places Yinjiao. Swap hands or use a mirror if reaching downward feels awkward.

Two quick confirmations help before any real pressing starts. First, check the vertical relationship: Qihai should lie about half a thumb-width below wherever your fingers rest, so if that deeper, softer zone seems to sit above your fingers instead, you have drifted low — slide back up toward the navel. Second, compare sides: touch feather-light a finger’s width to the left of the seam, then to the right, then on it. Most people notice a faint, dull awareness on the midline itself and noticeably less beside it. Let one slow breath move under your fingertips as you check; the belly rises and falls, and a truly centred placement stays centred through the whole cycle.

Common slips are few but worth naming. The first is sliding off the seam without noticing: the abdominal wall curves gently toward the flanks, and a searching thumb drifts sideways with it, so re-find the centre line whenever doubt arises. The second is locating while standing and braced, which tightens the wall and blurs everything; lying down is the honest posture for this point. The third is confusing Yinjiao with Qihai, its slightly lower neighbour, because the two share the same line and feel alike; the answer is method rather than force — return to the navel, measure down one slow thumb-width a single time, and trust the count.

## Reading the Name: Where Yin Meets {#name-and-meaning}

The opening character, yin 陰, is among the most load-bearing words in Chinese thought. Its older form pictures the shaded side of a hill — the northern slope the sun does not warm — or, by the same logic, the southern bank of a river. From that image grows a whole family of meanings: the cool rather than the hot, the receptive rather than the asserting, the hidden and nourishing rather than the visible and expanding. Classical body-mapping borrowed the term wholesale: the back was assigned to yang, sunlit and exposed, while the chest and belly belonged to yin territory — sheltered, soft, inward-facing. Yinjiao therefore stands, fittingly, on the yin face of the trunk.

The second character, jiao 交, means to cross, to meet, to join — the stroke of two lines intersecting, the exchange of things passing hand to hand. Joined, the name reads the Yin Intersection, the place where yin meets. The dataset tags the point as a crossing point, and classical texts describe such places as junctions where more than one pathway converges on the same ground. They describe the ren vessel itself as climbing the front midline of the body, gathering and uniting the yin channels as it rises. Whatever the finer classical discussions of exactly which pathways touch here, name and classification agree in spirit: this is ground where roads cross.

The pinyin spelling Yīnjiāo places a level first tone on both syllables — the macron over each vowel is the dictionary mark of that high, flat, sustained pitch — so the name is spoken evenly, almost ceremonially, without dips. The two characters 陰交 are easy to hold in the eye, and they belong to a naming style the tradition favours: gates, valleys, seas, courtyards, meetings. Like Zhongfu’s treasury or Qihai’s sea, the metaphor quietly instructs. It says the ancients experienced this reach of the lower belly as a place of convergence rather than mere transit — and in this literature, names are compressed observations.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications open with the gut cluster: abdominal pain and diarrhoea. In the traditional frame these belong together. Pain circling or settling below the navel, and bowels that lose their orderly rhythm, were understood as qi failing to move smoothly through the middle and lower reaches of the trunk. Practice therefore reached for points on the midline of the lower abdomen, where the ren vessel passes closest to the organs of digestion, and Yinjiao stood among the customary addresses. The dataset also carries the modern label enteritis among its action entries; that is the compilers’ present-day vocabulary laid over the older abdominal indications, restated here descriptively rather than as any claim of treatment.

A water cluster follows: edema — swelling understood as fluid gathering where it should move — and difficult urination. The grouping is no accident. Just above the navel the midline carries a point named the Water Divide, and the old books read this whole stretch as territory concerned with the body’s handling of fluids: perspiration, swelling, and the waters that should pass downward and away. Hernia completes this part of the list. In classical usage the term covered dragging, bulging, or colicky complaints of the lower belly and groin, understood as contents or qi descending when they should be held. These are traditional categories of experience recorded by the dataset, not diagnoses made by this page; the modern label prostatitis appears among the entries for the same descriptive reason as above.

The remaining cluster concerns women’s cycles: irregular menstruation, uterine bleeding, and vaginal discharge. Midline points of the lower abdomen were classically grouped as close neighbours of the womb’s territory, and the older literature returns to them again and again in discussions of menstrual rhythm and discharge. It is precisely this closeness that makes the safety note bound to this point so emphatic — the caution about pregnancy and the uterus stands in the same record as these indications, because the point lies over the very structures it was traditionally used to address. The record’s uses and the record’s warnings are not separate facts; they are the same geography stated twice.

It bears repeating plainly how to hold all of this. These are the traditional uses entered in a licensed dataset and described across classical literature, offered for cultural and historical understanding. They are not assertions that pressing this point treats disease, and nothing here replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Where modern research has touched self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and dependent on context, and evidence tied to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition as tradition — a map of how people once organised care and meaning around the body — and hold the safety rules as rules.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located by the walkthrough above, arrange yourself as you searched: lying back, knees bent, shoulders released. Join the pads of index and middle fingers and rest them on the spot — pads, never tips, and never a knuckle or elbow. Begin with contact alone for a few slow breaths, letting the belly rise and fall beneath still fingers. Then add small, unhurried circles, allowing the skin to glide over the tissue beneath rather than driving inward toward the cavity. Sensible pressure registers as pleasant dullness or mild spreading warmth; anything sharper is the signal to ease off. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per session, once or twice daily, matched where possible to long, slow exhales.

Quality outranks duration in this practice. Warm your hands first if they run cold — chilled muscle guards, and a guarded belly tells you nothing true. Choose a quiet corner rather than a screen, and let the minutes double as a pause: inhale through the nose, feel the lower belly swell under resting fingers, and let each small circle ride a slow breath out. People who keep such routines usually anchor them to something already fixed in the day — waking, bathing, an afternoon rest — because regularity, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask of a person. On the midline, symmetry of side matters less than symmetry of manner: the same gentleness every time.

Treat the exercise as a few minutes of attended rest rather than a treatment session. Over weeks, notice honestly what changes: perhaps the area feels less guarded, perhaps comfort through the day feels steadier when you practise, perhaps nothing shifts except that you now spend two minutes daily in quiet contact with your own body. Every one of those outcomes sits inside the honest scope of self-acupressure. What sits outside it is escalation — pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a result, especially over an organ cavity. When curiosity pulls in that direction, answer it with gentleness, or set the practice down for the day.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

This point carries a warning in its licensed record, and it belongs at the centre of any conversation about Yinjiao, not in footnotes. The dataset cautions practitioners to work with care in pregnancy, recording that deep insertion may injure the uterus. The warning follows from simple geography: the lower midline of the abdomen is a thin, yielding wall over a soft cavity, with the uterus and loops of bowel resting near beneath. Professional training manages that risk with exact technique and judgement; home self-care manages it differently and far more safely — by never leaving the surface, and by declining the site altogether whenever pregnancy is a factor.

For fingertip acupressure, translate that record into conservative house rules. If you are pregnant, or pregnancy is possible, skip this point entirely — this page treats it as off-limits, not merely cautious. Pause the practice during any feverish illness, over broken or irritated skin, during any episode of unusual or heavy bleeding, and whenever abdominal pain has appeared without a settled explanation. Use finger pads only: no tools, devices, elbows, or knuckles; no sustained driving pressure toward the depths. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation ends the session at once and for the rest of the day. Anyone living with pelvic conditions, or recovering from abdominal surgery, should ask a qualified clinician before adding any new point practice.

And the larger frame stands over everything. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for severe or worsening abdominal pain, pain accompanied by fever, blood in stool or urine, inability to pass urine, persistent vomiting, or a groin bulge that turns painful, firm, or irreducible. Persistent symptoms of any kind belong with a licensed practitioner, whether physician or East Asian medical clinician as suits the complaint. This page is educational only, and self-acupressure is comfort care, never diagnosis or therapy. The safest thing Yinjiao offers most visitors is not a technique but an acquaintance: a calmer, better-informed relationship with their own lower belly, treated kindly and lightly.
