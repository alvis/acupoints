Jingming, the Bright Eyes, opens the bladder channel at the inner corner of the eye — the threshold where classical practice said the long vessel of the little toe begins. Old records reached for it when eyes ran red, watered, itched, or clouded. Because it lives closer to the eye than most named points, this guide writes every step gently and draws every limit firmly.

## Where Jingming Sits on the Face {#where-it-sits}

The dataset locates the point on the face, in the small depression slightly above the inner canthus of the eye. The inner canthus is simply the anatomist’s name for the inner corner — the spot where the upper and lower eyelids meet beside the bridge of the nose. From that corner, drift your attention upward by a few millimeters, staying close to the side of the nose, and you arrive at the recorded neighborhood: a shallow hollow where soft tissue settles against the bony angle between the nasal bridge and the inner rim of the eye socket. It is a modest little place, easy to miss unless you know to look, yet classical tradition granted it a position of unusual honour.

Very little separates the surface here from delicate territory. The skin is among the thinnest on the body; beneath it lie the small muscles that blink and squint, then the bony rim with which the skull frames the opening of the eye. Immediately to the side rests the eyeball itself — cushioned, but close enough to inspire respect. This geography explains the unusually careful language the licensed records carry for this entry, and it should shape every expectation a home practitioner brings: whatever happens at Jingming happens lightly, briefly, and strictly on the surface, or it does not happen at all.

The point also holds the honour of being the bladder channel’s opening address. Classical descriptions trace the vessel from the inner corner of the eye, up and over the crown of the head, down the entire length of the back, and onward to the little toe — the longest single course on the traditional map, carrying sixty-seven points. Tradition additionally records Jingming as a crossing-point, a place where neighboring channels serving the head and face were described as converging on the bladder vessel’s path. A doorway, a crossroads, and the first step of a long road: the classical imagination gave this small hollow three jobs at once.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Begin with clean hands and a well-lit mirror, seated comfortably. Look straight ahead and find the inner corner of your left eye — the small moist triangle where the lids meet beside the nose. Rest the pad of your index finger there, feather-light, then let it drift upward a couple of millimeters, keeping contact with the side of the nose bridge until the pad settles into a slight hollow just above the corner, where skin meets bone. That hollow, sitting a breath above the inner canthus, is the location the dataset records. Repeat the search on the right side with the same unhurried patience; matching sensations on the two sides confirm you found the same neighborhood twice.

Confirm before anything else happens. Contact should stay light enough that the fingertip barely deforms the skin — imagine a raindrop’s weight, not a fingertip’s push. Many people notice a faint, dull awareness at the true hollow and almost nothing a few millimeters away; comparing the two teaches your finger the difference faster than any diagram. Throughout, the pad remains pressed toward the bony side of the hollow, never drifting sideways toward the roundness of the eye. If you feel lashes, a lid margin, or the globe’s smooth curve under your finger, you have wandered off course: lift away completely, take a breath, and begin again from the corner of the eye.

Three slips account for nearly every mis-finding. The first and most serious is sliding onto the eyeball itself — the correction is always the same: leave, return to the inner corner, climb up again. The second is dropping below the corner onto the lower lid; remember the record places Jingming slightly above the canthus, not beneath it. The third is climbing too far, ending up halfway to the eyebrow, when the true hollow sits within a thumbnail’s width of the corner. When doubt wins, stop pressing altogether — the point will keep, and your eye deserves the caution.

## Reading the Name: Brightness of the Eye {#name-and-meaning}

Two characters, one image. Jing 睛 means the eye’s dark centre — pupil and eyeball together, the seeing orb itself. Ming 明 means bright, luminous, clear; its written form sets sun beside moon, the oldest picture of pooled light. Laid side by side, Jingming reads naturally as the brightness of the eyes, or eyes made bright. Few names in the classical collection state their ambition so openly: this point was named for the thing the tradition hoped it would watch over — clarity and light in the organ of sight.

The name doubles as a job description. Because the point sits at the very corner of the eye where the bladder vessel surfaces, classical writers treated it as the front gate for conversation about the eyes — the natural first reach whenever the old records turned to redness, watering, itching, or dimming of sight. Its additional identity as a crossing-point deepened the imagery: several channels were described as meeting here, pooling their influence at the eye before scattering onward, which made Jingming less a lone sentry than a busy junction where the head’s pathways exchanged traffic.

Pinyin renders the name Jīngmíng. Checked character by character against a dictionary: jing 睛 carries the first tone, a high level note held flat and steady; ming 明 carries the second tone, rising from mid pitch like the tail of a question. The hanzi pair 睛明 is plain enough to memorise at a glance, and like many channel points named for the organ they serve rather than the place they hide, the metaphor does real work: it tells you the tradition counted this small hollow among the guardians of sight.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: red swollen painful eyes, dizziness, blurred vision, tearing, eye itching, cataract, night blindness, optic atrophy, acute lumbar sprain, and rapid heartbeat. Take the first cluster as tradition, not promise: classical practice traditionally reached for Jingming when eyes appeared red, swollen, and painful, when they watered, when they itched, or when vision seemed to blur. In the traditional frame these complaints shared one story — qi and blood failing to brighten the eye — and the point standing at the eye’s own corner stood first in line for such discussions.

The next cluster asks for a steadier hand in reading. Cataract, night blindness, and optic atrophy are structural conditions of the eye. The old records name them alongside the point because classical catalogues aimed at completeness, not because pressing the skin was understood to undo them. Nothing in this page suggests otherwise, and the licensed record itself states plainly that eye disease belongs in ophthalmic care — a sentence worth underlining twice. Anyone noticing real changes in vision needs an eye-care professional promptly; historical lists are context, never a detour around diagnosis.

The final cluster puzzles modern readers pleasantly: dizziness, acute lumbar sprain, rapid heartbeat. Dizziness fits the classical habit of pairing the eyes with the head’s sense of balance. The low-back entry follows the channel map: because the bladder vessel walks the whole length of the back, injuries along the lumbar stretch of that road entered the portfolios of its points, and the channel’s opening point appears in the old discussions. Rapid heartbeat resists tidy explanation and stands as a reminder that classical categorization followed its own logic rather than modern anatomy. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

Settle in slowly. Wash your hands thoroughly — the eye region forgives less than most neighborhoods — and if you wear contact lenses, remove them first. Sit upright before the mirror, shoulders released, breathing unhurried. Choose the pad of a single index finger; tools, knuckles, and elbows have no business anywhere near an eye. Rest the pad in the hollow found by the walkthrough above, touch first with no pressure at all for two or three slow breaths, and let the tissue learn that your intention is friendly. Only then consider adding the lightest possible pressure — and consider is the operative word, because many sessions are complete before any pressing starts.

When pressure comes, keep it feather-light: enough to notice warmth and a faint dull awareness, never enough to blanch the skin or ache. Small slow circles — or simple still presence — for a handful of breaths make a full session. Count seconds, not minutes; once a day is generous, and alternating sides keeps the practice symmetrical. Direction matters more than duration: the pad presses into the bony hollow beside the nose, never sideways toward the globe. Close the eyes softly or keep a lowered gaze, whichever lets the face relax. Any sting, ache, or flicker of disturbed vision ends the session immediately, and the day’s practice is done.

Treat the exercise as a small ceremony of attention rather than a treatment. Perhaps the eye area feels less tense afterward, perhaps a screen-weary afternoon feels kinder, perhaps nothing shifts except that you spent ninety quiet seconds caring for your own face — every one of those outcomes sits honestly within self-acupressure’s scope. What sits outside is escalation: harder pressure, longer holds, novelty tools. Children and older adults have thinner, more delicate skin; for them, lighter still, or not at all. When curiosity pulls toward force, the tradition’s own answer is the gentlest one — stop for today.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

Jingming carries one of the starkest warnings in the licensed record, and it deserves to be heard first. The dataset directs that moxibustion not be applied here, noting that classical literature warns moxibustion at this site causes loss of sight. Whatever one makes of moxa elsewhere, the rule for this entry is absolute: no smoldering herb, no radiant heat, no warmed device approaches this hollow — or, truthfully, the eye region at all. Heat and flame belong nowhere near the organs of sight, and a home reader needs nothing more elaborate than that sentence to stay safe.

The record’s second caution governs professional hands: needle work here demands extreme caution — steering clear of the eyeball above all — and the fine judgments involved belong to trained practitioners, not to kitchen tables. Home practice honours the warning by staying on the surface entirely: skin and bone only, never the globe. Skip the point outright during any eye infection, stye, or flare of redness; after any eye surgery, injury, or procedure until ordinary activity is cleared; and while wearing makeup that could travel into the eye. Anyone living with a diagnosed eye condition should ask their eye-care provider before adding any new point practice, and pregnant readers should consult a qualified practitioner before beginning unfamiliar routines.

And the frame around everything: this page is educational, acupressure is self-care, and neither diagnoses nor treats anything. Seek prompt professional care — not a pressure routine — for sudden changes in vision, flashes or floating shadows, pain in or around the eye, direct injury, persistent redness or discharge, or anything that frightens you. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner, medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. The name promises brightness, but the brightest thing Jingming offers most visitors is a slower, kinder acquaintance with their own eyes — handled rarely, gently, and with respect.
