Pucan — the Servant’s Respect — waits low on the outer side of the heel, below and behind the outer ankle bone, on the flank of the heel bone where two colours of skin meet. The bladder channel passes here near the end of its descent toward the little toe; classical practice reached for this station when heels ached and legs grew weak. This guide shows how to find it by touch and press it gently.

## Where Pucan Sits Below the Outer Ankle {#where-it-sits}

The dataset places Pucan on the lateral side of the foot, below and behind the lateral malleolus — the outer ankle bone — directly below Kunlun (BL60), on the lateral side of the calcaneus at the junction of the red and white skin. Every phrase of that address earns its keep. Below and behind fixes the point low on the heel and tucked toward the back, not forward along the arch. Directly below Kunlun hands you the one relationship worth memorising: whatever else shifts, this station hangs straight beneath its neighbor above. The calcaneus is the heel bone proper, the largest bone of the foot, whose rounded outer flank lies close beneath the skin. And the junction of the red and white skin is one of the tradition’s recurring locators: the uneven line where the paler, finer skin clothing the top and side of the foot gives way to the deeper-toned, tougher skin that rounds down toward the sole.

What lies under the fingertips there rewards gentle attention. Behind the malleolus, the strong cord of the Achilles tendon sweeps downward to grip the back of the heel bone; forward and below, the bony flank of the calcaneus curves away under a thin blanket of soft tissue. The point sits in the seam country between these structures, where bone lies near the surface and the padding is modest. That geography carries a practical lesson: heavy pressure buys nothing here, because there is simply nowhere for force to go — the landmarks announce themselves to patient, feather-light fingertips and stay silent before anything harder. It also explains why this stretch of heel feels every step a person takes, and why the tradition gave the territory a respectful, attentive name.

Neighbors confirm the street number. Up the slope rests Kunlun (BL60), which the same catalogue locates in the depression between the tip of the lateral malleolus and the Achilles tendon — the mountain station of the channel’s descent. Lower still, in the hollow directly beneath the malleolus tip, waits Shenmai (BL62). Pucan keeps lower than either and leans slightly back, out on the heel bone’s flank where the colour boundary runs. All three hang on one descending thread: the bladder channel, longest meridian in the body, running from the inner corner of the eye over the head and down the whole length of the back before crossing the back of the knee, descending the calf, rounding the outer ankle, and making along the lateral border of the foot for the little toe. Find the heel bone’s side and you have found a waystation on the channel’s final approach.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit on a firm chair and cross your right ankle over your left knee, letting the outer side of the heel face you; if crossing feels awkward, plant the foot flat and lean forward instead. Begin with the outer ankle bone and trace its pointed tip with a fingertip. Slip just behind that tip into the soft corridor between bone and the thick cord of the Achilles tendon — the hollow you find there is Kunlun (BL60), the very neighbor this point hangs beneath. From inside that hollow, walk straight down along the side of the heel until the tendon’s corridor ends against the rounded flank of the heel bone. The spot you want waits on that flank, at the place where the two tones of skin meet.

A few confirmations earn their keep before any pressing begins. Look first: on most heels, the boundary between the paler side skin and the redder, sturdier skin curving toward the sole draws an uneven line around the lower edge of the bone — the recorded junction of red and white. Touch next, feather-light, comparing sides: many people notice a faint, dull awareness at the true spot and little sensation a finger-width away. Then test stability: rest your fingertip on the candidate point and rock the ankle gently — soft tissue shifts with the movement while bone stays put, and the true site keeps faith with the bone through every small swing. If what you feel is a taut cord under the finger, you are still inside the tendon’s corridor; slide farther down until your fingertip rounds the bone’s curve and settles on its flank.

Three classic misses account for most failed searches. The first is stopping too high, in the neat hollow immediately below the malleolus tip — that address belongs to Shenmai (BL62), and Pucan waits distinctly lower, out on the heel bone. The second is wandering forward, up under the front border of the malleolus, where the record places Jinmen (BL63) near the cuboid bone; forward country is not heel country. The third is sliding too far under the sole proper, past the colour boundary into the padded underside. Hold the two directions the dataset insists on — below and behind — start at Kunlun, travel straight down, and stop where bone meets the colour line. Finish by repeating the whole search on the other side, unhurried, so both heels receive equal attention.

## Reading the Name: The Servant’s Respect {#name-and-meaning}

Two characters, one small ceremony of rank. Pu 僕 means servant — the attendant who serves, walks behind, and stands below; dictionaries assign its rising second-tone reading pu to exactly this sense of service, keeping a separate reading for the quite different idea of falling headlong. Can 參 contributes its face of formal attendance: the character of ceremonial visits and respectful salutation, the same one that leads the compound for paying homage to a superior. Read together, Pucan is the servant who attends, the attendant’s salutation — and the oldest commentators tied the title straight to the map: this station stands just below stately Kunlun, itself named for the great mountain, like an attendant positioned respectfully beneath his lord.

Classical commentators kept more than one image in play; report them generally, quote none of them. When a servant bows low in salute, they observed, the outer edge of the heel is precisely what presents itself to the master — this exposed flank of the heel is the bow made visible, a gesture of respect written in bone. An older image adds motion: in the age of carriages and retinues, an attendant rode alongside his lord, and this station attends alongside the channel’s mountain as the road descends. Old catalogues even preserve an alternative name for the point, a quiet reminder that the tradition’s memory of a station could shift across centuries even as its place on the body never moved.

Pinyin renders the name Púcān. The syllable pú rides the second tone, which climbs the way a question lifts in English; cān holds the first tone, high, level, and sustained, like a note held steady. The hanzi 僕參 are simple enough to memorise at a glance. And the name joins a family the tradition favoured alongside its mountains, valleys, and streams: stations named for rank and role — ministers, envoys, gates and their keepers, servants and their lords. Here status serves terrain rather than competing with it: the mountain keeps its station above, and the servant keeps his below. The name is a miniature set of instructions. Find the lord; stand below him; pay respects lightly.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: atrophy and paralysis of the lower limbs, heel pain, swelling and pain of the ankle, epilepsy, and convulsions. Five entries, and the middle pair sit closest to the point’s own ground. Classical texts describe the bladder channel rounding the outer ankle and traveling along the side of the foot toward the little toe, and the tradition reasoned along routes: complaints arising at the heel and ankle belonged naturally to stations on that final stretch of road. A point living on the heel itself was therefore consulted first among equals for heel pain, and ankle swelling and pain drew on the same local logic. Modern honesty arrives promptly beside the tradition: heel pain has many possible origins, some of them needing proper assessment, and discomfort that persists deserves a clinician’s evaluation rather than a self-pressure routine alone.

The opening entry — atrophy and paralysis of the lower limbs — speaks the language of another era’s diagnosis. In the traditional frame it gathers states the classics described as withering and obstructed: legs gone heavy, thin, or unresponsive; strength draining out of the stride; steps that falter where they once flowed. Classical practice approached such conditions through the channel network, choosing stations along the affected roads and pairing them with movement, rest, and general care. Translated into today’s obligations, the honest reading is narrower and firmer: new weakness, numbness, or trouble bearing weight is a symptom calling for prompt professional assessment, because its causes deserve proper investigation and no fingertip routine addresses them.

The closing entries ask for the most care. Epilepsy and convulsions reflect the diagnostic world in which the old records were written; seizures in our era belong to emergency medicine alone, and nothing done at home — least of all pressing a point on someone mid-seizure — has any part in responding to one. Taken together, the five entries sketch tradition’s portrait: a low, deferential station on the heel, consulted for the ground it stood on and for troubles the channel-road passed on its way to the toes. They are recorded traditional uses, offered for cultural understanding. They are not promises; evidence specific to any single point remains thin, and where research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

Settle in first: seated with the ankle crossed comfortably over the opposite knee, or the foot planted flat with a slight forward lean. Locate the spot by the walkthrough above, then rest the pads of two fingers — or the pad of a thumb — on it. Begin with contact alone for a few slow breaths; add only small, patient circles afterward, letting the skin glide over the bone’s flank rather than grinding into it. Sensation should register as pleasant dullness or a mild spreading warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, matched where possible to unhurried exhales. Swap sides and finish symmetrically, giving the quieter heel its equal share of attention.

Quality matters more than duration, and on the heel a few specifics help. Finger pads only — never elbows, knuckles, or tools — because bone this close to the surface turns blunt enthusiasm into bruising. Warm your hands first; chilled tissue guards. Choose a quiet corner over a screen, and consider folding the practice onto an existing habit — after an evening shower, before bed — because consistency rather than intensity is what self-care traditions actually reward. People who spend long days standing sometimes give the heels their minute as a small act of thanks; that spirit, humble and regular, suits a point named for service better than any regimen of force.

Hold the session as a small ceremony of attention rather than a treatment appointment. Over weeks, notice honestly what changes: perhaps the heels feel less tight on morning stairs, perhaps standing through a long afternoon leaves less heaviness behind, perhaps nothing shifts except that a few minutes a day went to the parts of you that carry everything and rarely get thanked. Every outcome on that list fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — heavier thumbs, longer sessions, pressing through pain to force a result. When curiosity pulls that way, return to lightness or stop for the day. The servant has kept his post for centuries; he will keep it until tomorrow.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

Honesty first: the licensed dataset records no caution entry for this point — no warning line accompanies its record. An empty caution field is a fact about the dataset, not a dare. The heel’s own geography counsels restraint regardless: bone lies close beneath thin tissue, the great tendon works nearby, and the colour boundary marks a real transition within the skin itself. Gentle surface contact respects that landscape completely; force does not. Everything that follows flows from a single principle: on the heel, as everywhere on the foot, gentleness is not timidity but accuracy.

Practical rules for home practice, then. Keep contact light and strictly superficial — skin and surface tissue only, never driving inward toward bone. Skip the point entirely over broken, cracked, or irritated skin, over blisters and callused splits in the area, and over any heel that is swollen, hot, tender, or painful without explanation. Leave the point alone after recent foot, ankle, or heel injury or surgery until a clinician clears ordinary activity. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once and do not retry that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with circulatory, nerve, or joint conditions affecting the feet — diabetes-related foot changes above all — should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for sudden severe heel pain, particularly if it arrived with a snap during activity or makes weight-bearing impossible; for spreading redness, warmth, or swelling with fever; for numbness or weakness climbing the leg; and for any seizure, which belongs to emergency services alone. Persistent heel or ankle complaints deserve proper assessment too, whichever gentle practices accompany them. The heels carry a person faithfully for decades; treat them as trusted infrastructure, and let genuine symptoms travel the genuine road to genuine clinicians.
