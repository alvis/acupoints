Chengjin — Supporting the Sinew — rests in the fullest part of the calf muscle, midway down the back of the lower leg on the bladder channel’s long road from the eye to the little toe. Classical practice reached for it when calves cramped, backs stiffened, and legs grew heavy. This guide walks you to the spot by touch and shows how to press it with care.

## Where Chengjin Sits in the Calf {#where-it-sits}

The dataset places Chengjin on the back of the lower leg, on the line connecting Weizhong (BL40) and Chengshan (BL57), at the centre of the belly of the gastrocnemius muscle, five cun below Weizhong. Three details unpack that sentence. First, the line: behind each knee sits Weizhong at the middle of the crease, and further down the calf waits Chengshan near the place where the muscle tapers toward the Achilles tendon; a straight vertical road joins them, running down the middle of the calf. Second, the distance: five cun below the crease — a cun being the proportional body-measure of Chinese point location, close to the width of your own thumb, so five cun is roughly the distance your thumb covers walking down the leg in five steps. Third, the terrain: the centre of the muscle belly itself, not a groove beside it and not the tendon below.

That belly belongs to the gastrocnemius, the twin-headed muscle that gives the calf its familiar shape. Its two heads arise behind the knee and blend into one full, rounded body, which narrows into the Achilles tendon at the heel; every time you rise onto the balls of your feet, this is the muscle doing the lifting, bunching into a firm oval under the skin. Chengjin occupies the crest of that oval — its fullest, central reach. Beneath lie deeper layers of calf muscle and the corridor of vessels and nerves travelling toward the foot, which is why everything about touching this region argues for patience: light, listening fingertips find far more than forceful ones ever do.

Neighbors confirm the address. Above stands Weizhong at the crease; below waits Chengshan in the pointed depression that forms where the belly tapers when the heel lifts or the leg straightens; and further up, the same vertical thread continues toward Chengfu on the back of the thigh below the buttock fold. All of these hang on one line — the bladder channel descending the back of the leg — and Chengjin sits at its most muscular mile. If you can find the middle of the crease and trace the midline of the calf downward, the channel’s own geometry has already checked your work before any pressing begins.

## Finding Chengjin by Touch {#finding-it-by-touch}

Begin standing, facing a wall or the back of a chair for balance, feet about hip-width apart. Rise slowly onto the balls of both feet and watch the back of each lower leg: the calf gathers into a firm, rounded prominence, highest and fullest around the middle of the leg between knee and heel. That swollen crest — the top of the hill the muscle makes — is the neighbourhood where Chengjin lives. Lower the heels and let the muscle soften completely; a relaxed muscle reveals landmarks that a tensed one hides. Rest your fingertips on the spot and register the dense, springy bulk under the skin before applying anything you would call pressure.

Now measure from above. Find the midpoint of the crease behind the knee — Weizhong — then walk your thumb down the midline of the calf, one thumb-width at a time, counting five steps. Around the fifth step your thumb settles into the fullest part of the softened belly, and there it should rest. Compare sides with two hands at once: the contours differ slightly from leg to leg, and matching them by feel beats trusting either measurement alone. Most people notice a faint, dull awareness when the true spot is found and little sensation a thumb-width away — the same quiet confirmation traditional location practice has always leaned on.

Two classic slips are worth naming. The first is landing too low: slide past the belly’s crest and the muscle thins quickly toward Chengshan’s tapering hollow, so if the fingertip finds a groove rather than fullness, climb back up half a hand-width and search again. The second is drifting sideways off the midline; the point keeps to the centre of the calf, not out along either edge. When in doubt, return to the crease, recount five thumb-widths slowly, and rise onto the toes once more to re-check the crest. Slow repetition builds the map faster than force ever could.

## Reading the Name: Supporting the Sinew {#name-and-meaning}

The characters are a job description in miniature. Cheng 承 means to receive, to bear, to hold up from beneath — the posture of hands accepting a weight. Jin 筋 names sinew: tendon, muscle, the tough elastic tissue that ties strength to bone. Together, Chengjin reads as supporting the sinew, and the location explains the metaphor: the point dwells in the fullest flesh of the calf, the place where the muscle gathers its power before handing the load down to the tendon and heel. Classical etymology read the station as the one that bears up the sinews of the lower leg — a name describing both what lives here and what this spot quietly does all day.

The verb cheng recurs across the channel’s stations like a refrain. Chengfu carries it on the back of the thigh below the buttock fold; Chengshan takes it further down, where the belly pours into the tendon; Chengguang bears it high on the head. One word, many offices — a reminder that the tradition named points for the roles they play in the body’s working landscape rather than for abstract coordinates. Here the role is bearing weight in the most literal sense: the muscle under this point lifts the whole body with every step, every stair, every rise onto tiptoe.

Pinyin renders the name Chéngjīn: a rising second tone on cheng, sweeping upward like a question finding its answer, and a high, level first tone on jin held steady as a sung note. The hanzi pair 承筋 is simple enough to memorise at a glance. Where many channel points borrow architecture — gates, valleys, courtyards, storehouses — this one borrows an act of service, and the difference suits it. The name is less a picture of a place than a small instruction about attitude: arrive here to support, not to conquer.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: haemorrhoids, rectal prolapse, constipation, spasm and pain of the lower back and legs, and calf cramps. Read in the traditional register, the last three follow the channel’s geography almost automatically. The point sits inside the very muscle that knots into cramps, so spasm of the calf was approached on its own doorstep; and because the bladder channel threads down the whole back of the body, stiffness and pain along the lower back and legs were traditionally met at stations along that route — of which Chengjin is one of the muscular waypoints on the descent.

The anorectal trio looks more surprising until the classical channel map is consulted. Classical channel doctrine describes a branch of the bladder channel descending toward the anal region, and that teaching is the traditional reason calf stations on this channel appear so often in old discussions of haemorrhoids and prolapse: where a channel travels, its points may be asked to speak for conditions along the way. Constipation joined through the same logic of movement — the tradition understood stuck things as things whose flow needed encouraging, and the long descending course of the bladder channel made its stations natural candidates for such discussions.

It bears repeating plainly: these are traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point treats disease, and nothing here replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Modern research on self-applied pressure at any single point remains thin, and reviews suggest effects, where they exist, are modest and context-dependent. Hold the tradition lightly and hold the safety rules firmly; the two attitudes protect each other.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle in: sit with both feet flat on the floor, or lie face-down with a pillow under the ankles so the calf hangs loose. Bring the pads of both thumbs together on the spot and begin with contact alone for a few breaths. Then add small, slow circles, letting the skin glide over the muscle beneath. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or spreading warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, matched to slow exhales so each circle rides the breath out; finish with the other side and keep the sessions symmetrical.

Quality outranks duration. Warm your hands first, because chilled muscle guards itself; choose a quiet corner over a screen; let the practice be a pause rather than a task. After heavy exercise, keep the touch lighter than usual — tired muscle appreciates company more than persuasion. And never press hard into a cramp in full spasm: let the knot release on its own, helped if you like by gentle stretching, and save the circles for afterwards, once the tissue has settled. People who keep the habit often anchor it to something already routine — an evening stretch, the wind-down before bed — because regularity, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Treat those few minutes as a ceremony of attention rather than a treatment session. Across weeks you might notice the muscle feeling less guarded, walking feeling easier, or nothing changing except that you spent ninety seconds attending to your own leg — and every one of those outcomes sits honestly within what self-acupressure offers. What sits outside it is escalation: pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a result. Curiosity that pulls that way can simply be answered with gentleness, or with stopping for the day and coming back tomorrow.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

The licensed record lists no specific cautions for Chengjin, and honesty requires saying what that does and does not mean. It means the dataset flags nothing unusual about this site — unlike some points on the chest wall or the face, no special professional hazard is recorded here. It does not mean force becomes welcome: questions of professional technique belong to trained practitioners and stay outside this page entirely, while home acupressure follows its own simpler law — contact stays on the surface, however thick and inviting the muscle feels. The absence of a caution is a quiet fact, not an invitation.

The practical rules for home practice are short. Skip the point over broken or irritated skin, fresh bruises, and prominent veins that are painful or inflamed. Never apply firm massage to a calf that is swollen, hot, red, or newly and unusually painful — those signs call for medical assessment first, not pressure of any kind. After a recent strain, tear, or surgery in the area, wait until ordinary activity is cleared again. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once and do not try again that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with circulatory problems, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Persistent or one-sided swelling, unexplained bruising, numbness, cramps that grow frequent or severe — including night cramps that keep returning — belong with a licensed clinician rather than a pressure routine. An appointment costs an hour; a missed warning sign can cost far more. The safest gift this friendly muscle-station offers most people is a better acquaintance with their own calf, met patiently and treated kindly.
