Wuchu, the Fifth Place, rests on the forehead one cun above the front hairline, set a thumb-and-a-half clear of the centreline. The bladder channel passes here on its climb from beside the eye toward the crown, and classical practice reached for Wuchu in headache, dizziness, and dimming sight. This guide covers finding it by touch and pressing it gently.

## Where Wuchu Sits on the Forehead {#where-it-sits}

The dataset locates Wuchu on the head, one cun above the midpoint of the anterior hairline and one and a half cun lateral to it. Unpacked, that means two measurements taken from your own face. First find the front hairline and its midpoint — where the line where hair meets skin crosses the vertical centre of the forehead. From there, measure straight upward one cun, then step outward from the midline one and a half cun. A cun, as throughout this site, is the traditional proportional unit built from the person being measured: about the width of your own thumb at its knuckle-to-tip stretch. Nothing here depends on a ruler; the numbers scale with the head they describe, which is why the same written location serves every age and every shape of face.

Beneath the fingertip, this is forgiving country. The scalp skin here moves freely over a thin layer of forehead muscle, and beneath both lies the firm, unyielding curve of the skull — there are no delicate deep structures between finger and bone at this spot, unlike some chest points where caution dominates. The area is nonetheless richly supplied with nerves and small vessels, as anyone who has knocked a forehead knows, so contact registers clearly even when kept feather-light. Mild tenderness under firm pressure is common along the upper forehead line and simply tells you the finger has arrived somewhere meaningful; sharpness is never part of the picture.

The catalogue itself draws the ladder Wuchu climbs. The entry below records Qucha (BL4) half a cun above the same hairline midpoint at the same sideways distance, and the entry above records Chengguang (BL6) two and a half cuns up — so the bladder channel stations march up the forehead in a vertical file, close together at first, then spaced wider as they rise. The channel’s own story explains the arrangement: classical maps send the bladder channel up from beside the inner corner of the eye, across the brow and over the crown toward the back of the head before it descends the whole body to the little toe — the longest single course in the traditional atlas. Wuchu stands early on that climb, in the territory just above the hairline where the channel first spreads across the top of the face.

## Finding Wuchu by Touch: A Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit facing a mirror in good light, shoulders easy, and begin with the hairline. Place a fingertip at the middle of your front hairline — if the line dips or waves, take its central meeting with the vertical midline of the forehead as your anchor. Now walk that fingertip straight up the skin by one thumb-width, keeping to the midline as you go; the pad should come to rest about a thumb-width above hairline level. Next, slide sideways away from the centreline by a thumb-width and a half — most people find it easiest to work both hands at once, one to each side, so the pair stays symmetrical. Press gently into the scalp where you land: that is the recorded location of Wuchu.

A few quiet checks confirm the spot before any real pressing begins. Compare sides with light simultaneous pressure: the true pair usually answers with a faint dull awareness or mild tenderness, while the scalp just above, below, or further out stays quieter. Trace an imaginary line between your two fingertips — it should run parallel to the hairline, level and even, because both points share the same height above it. Check the distance from the centreline too: one and a half cun puts each point plainly off the midline without approaching the temple. If your fingers have drifted high toward the crown or wide toward the temples, return to the hairline midpoint and measure again, slowly.

The classic slips are three. Drifting upward happens when the fingertip walks past one thumb-width; keep the first stop honest by laying your measuring thumb down twice in sequence and stopping at the end of the second span’s start. Drifting inward lands you near the midline, where different stations keep watch; remember the point lives clearly off-centre. And searching only one side invites imbalance — hands mirrored together make left and right impossible to confuse. Hair complicates everything: let fingertips work through the strands rather than smoothing them aside, and accept that the search may take a patient minute. When in doubt, drop back to the hairline and begin the climb again; the landmarks do not move.

## Reading the Name: Fifth Place {#name-and-meaning}

Two spare characters build the name. Wu 五 means five — among the first characters any learner meets. Chu 處 carries the sense of a place, a spot, somewhere something abides: dictionaries give chu in the falling fourth tone for exactly this noun meaning, and note a separate reading, chu in the dipping third tone, for the verb “to dwell” or “to be situated.” Read together, Wuchu is simply the fifth place — and the natural explanation repeated across generations of point-name commentary ties the number to the office: this is counted the fifth station the bladder channel holds on its climb through the head, and the name marks the address like a milestone on a road.

The second character rewards a longer look. Because chu names a dwelling place, the name can also be heard less arithmetically — not merely station number five but a place where something settles. Traditional explanations of the head points often speak this way: channel qi climbing from beside the eye pauses and disperses at each resting place along the forehead, and the name-givers marked those stops with homely words for abodes and courtyards. Whether the five reads as pure ordinal or carries more, the image underneath is consistent — the forehead as a road with milestones, and Wuchu as one stop where the walker catches breath. Like gates, valleys, and storehouses elsewhere on the channels, the metaphor does quiet work: it says the tradition thought of points as places, not switches.

Pinyin writes the name Wǔchù. The first syllable rides the dipping third tone, the contour that falls and rises again like a slow nod; the second takes the falling fourth tone, spoken decisively downward. Say them together and the name keeps a small rhythm of its own — down-up, then down. The hanzi pair 五處 is short enough to memorise at a glance, and it repays the glance: five, place. This page writes the pair in its traditional form; readers of simplified texts will meet it as 五处, but the meaning travels unchanged.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The licensed record lists five actions for Wuchu: headache, dizziness, failing vision, epilepsy, and convulsions. Read as tradition, the list sketches a coherent portrait. Headache comes first, and classically head points were gathered for pain traveling routes beneath them — the bladder channel crosses exactly the upper forehead where Wuchu sits, so ache centred there, or tracking from brow toward crown, was naturally addressed along these stations. Dizziness, in the old records a sensation of swaying, spinning, or unsteadiness often paired with headache, shares the same territory. Failing vision — dimmed, clouded, or weakening sight — belongs to the family of eye complaints that classical head-point practice attended closely, the eyes being served, in the traditional map, by the channel rising past this very region from beside the inner eye.

The final pair — epilepsy and convulsions — asks for the clearest possible framing. Classical literature grouped many sudden-alarm conditions under such heads, and head points appear throughout those old discussions; that is the historical fact, and it is all this guide asserts. What the record does not license is any suggestion that self-applied pressure bears on seizures today. A seizure, a first seizure, or any change in an existing seizure pattern is a medical emergency belonging with urgent professional care, full stop. We preserve the traditional list because understanding what classical practice attended to is genuinely interesting and honestly reported; we fence it off because the distance between a historical indication and a modern home remedy is exactly where careless writing does harm.

It bears repeating plainly, as on every page of this site: these are traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing Wuchu treats any condition. Where modern research touches acupressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence gathered around any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition lightly and hold the safety rules firmly; persistent headache, dizziness, or visual change belongs with a licensed practitioner, whatever else a self-care routine includes.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

Once located, settle in: seated, spine tall but unforced, jaw loose. Bring the pads of both index or middle fingers onto the two points and rest them there through a few unhurried breaths before adding any pressure at all. Then begin small, slow circles, letting the scalp glide minutely over bone — a polishing motion, never a grinding one. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness, warmth, or mild tenderness blooming and fading, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, timed where possible to slow exhales so each circle rides the breath out. Finish by smoothing the forehead lightly with flat fingers, as though tidying the surface you have been tending.

Quality outweighs duration, as everywhere in self-care. Light, patient, regularly repeated touch suits the wellness register this page practises; force teaches nothing and bruises easily. Warm your hands first on cold days, because chilled fingertips meet a guarded scalp. Prefer a quiet corner to a screen, letting the minutes double as a breathing pause: inhale through the nose, soften on the long exhale, keep the circles slow enough to follow. Readers who keep routines succeed by anchoring them — after morning washing, before the afternoon slump — because consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask. If the scalp feels sore afterward, tomorrow use lighter circles for less time; the practice adapts to you, never the reverse.

Treat the few minutes as a small ceremony of attention rather than a treatment session. Across weeks you may notice the forehead feeling less clenched, the habit of furrowing interrupted, or simply the pleasure of ninety seconds spent attending to yourself — all of that fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. Escalation does not: pressing harder, longer, or more often to force a result turns comfort into coercion. When curiosity pulls that way, return to gentleness or close the day’s practice altogether. The point has waited centuries; it can wait until tomorrow.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

Honesty begins with what the record says — and with what it does not say. The licensed dataset records no specific cautions for Wuchu, and this module echoes that empty record verbatim rather than inventing warnings. An empty caution field is not, however, an all-clear for anything imaginable; it simply means no point-specific warning was handed down in the sources behind this page. General sense still governs: the forehead lies just above the eyes and the brain-case, so practice here stays strictly superficial, gentle, and brief, exactly as it would for any point on the head.

For fingertip work the rules follow directly. Use finger pads only — no nails, knuckles, elbows, or tools. Keep contact on intact skin: skip the point over cuts, rashes, flares, recent scars, or any irritated patch of scalp, and pause entirely during fevers or illnesses that leave the head sensitive. Anyone with a recent head injury, recent surgery on the head or face, a bleeding tendency, or neurological symptoms should ask a qualified practitioner before adding new point work; pregnant readers customarily consult before beginning any new practice at all. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation at any moment means stop at once and leave the point alone for the day. None of this is elaborate; all of it matters.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care — never diagnosis, never therapy. Seek prompt medical attention, not a pressure routine, for sudden severe headache; for headache with fever, stiff neck, confusion, vision change, or weakness; for dizziness that persists, worsens, or comes with fainting; for any change in vision; and above all for any seizure, first or familiar, which is always an emergency. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner, medical or East Asian medical as the complaint suggests. The Fifth Place offers most people a modest, kindly acquaintance with their own forehead; keep its ambitions equally modest and it serves well.
