Futonggu — the Through-the-Valley of the abdomen — sits high on the upper belly, half a thumb-width from the centre line and five cun above the navel, on the kidney channel’s climb toward the chest. Classical practice reached for it when distension, queasy stomach, or a stalled appetite settled in the middle of the torso. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it gently.

## Where Futonggu Sits on the Upper Abdomen {#where-it-sits}

Futonggu occupies the upper floor of the front torso. The licensed dataset locates it on the upper abdomen, five cun above the centre of the umbilicus and half a cun to the side of the anterior midline. A cun is the traditional measuring unit of Chinese point-location: proportional lengths taken from the person being examined rather than from a ruler, so a cun across a broad-framed adult differs from a cun on a slight teenager. Five cun above the navel is a substantial climb — well clear of the waistline, into the soft territory where the belly begins its rise toward the rib cage.

The classical proportional scheme makes that height easy to picture. Traditional teaching divides the span from the centre of the navel up to the shallow notch where the two lower rib margins meet at the base of the breastbone into eight equal parts. Five of those eight steps up from the navel brings you past the midpoint of the upper belly, close to the level where the ribs begin to gather — and half a cun off the centre line means just half a thumb-width to one side, a narrow strip indeed. The point therefore hides in plain sight: not on the midline itself, but immediately beside it, on the innermost slope of the upper abdominal wall.

What lies under the fingertips here deserves gentleness. The surface belongs to the abdominal wall — the strap of the rectus muscle runs close to its inner border along this line, and the covering tissue varies from firm to soft depending on the person and the season of their life. Deep to that wall lies the upper stomach’s territory, which is precisely why classical texts located digestive gathering-points here, and equally why any pressure practiced here should stay superficial and unhurried. Nothing about self-palpation needs force; the landmarks announce themselves to patient, light touch.

Neighbours confirm the address. On the anterior midline at exactly the same height rests Shangwan (CV13), one of the classic gathering stations of the upper digestion. One cun directly below, in the same half-cun lane beside the midline, sits Yindu (KI19); one cun above stands Youmen (KI21). The kidney channel’s abdominal points form a neat vertical ladder, each rung a thumb-width from the next, all half a cun off the centre line. Losing track mid-search simply means returning to the navel and the rib-base notch and measuring again, slowly.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit upright on a firm chair with both feet down, shoulders released, belly unclenched — a held stomach hides its own landmarks. Rest two fingertips on the centre of your navel, then walk them straight up the midline toward the base of the breastbone until you find the shallow V where the lower rib margins converge. Picture the distance between navel and that notch divided into eight equal steps. Now climb five of those steps back down from the notch, or up from the navel — whichever feels easier to pace — and from that height slide half a thumb-width to one side of the centre line. Under your fingertips should lie soft abdominal wall, neither bone nor gristle: that is the neighbourhood of Futonggu. Repeat the search on the other side, because the point exists in mirrored pairs.

A few confirmations help before any pressing begins. Touch feather-light and compare the two sides: many people notice a faint, dull awareness at the true spot and little sensation half a width away. Let a slow breath move under your fingertips — the abdominal wall rises on the inhale and settles on the exhale, and a genuine point-location feels that tide. Check the height twice against your two anchors: the spot should sit clearly above the navel’s wide open country yet below the crowded slopes of the rib margins, roughly five-eighths of the way between them. If your fingers land on the lower edge of the ribs themselves, come down a step; if they drift out toward the waist, start again from the navel.

The classic mistakes are few and worth naming. Drifting outward past half a thumb-width leaves the kidney channel’s narrow lane entirely — the spot you want hugs the centre line closer than intuition expects. Landing one rung too low finds Yindu, one rung too high finds Youmen; both are respectable points, but they are different addresses, and the walkthrough loses its meaning if the ladder is climbed carelessly. Counting from the wrong anchor reverses everything: five steps is measured upward from the navel, never downward. When in doubt, flatten the palm over the upper belly, breathe once, and begin the search again from the very start.

## Reading the Name: A Valley That Passes Through {#name-and-meaning}

The three characters sketch the point’s character. Fu 腹 means the abdomen, the belly of the trunk. Tong 通 carries the sense of passing through, opening, keeping unobstructed — the opposite of stuck. Gu 谷 is a valley: in point-naming, low ground where waters gather and flow onward. Read together, Futonggu is the through-valley of the abdomen — an image of a channel in the belly’s landscape along which food, drink, and qi are meant to move downward and onward. When passage stalls, the old frame speaks of fullness and distension; when the valley runs clear, digestion follows its course. The name is itself a small set of instructions.

The prefix deserves its own word. Another, older homonym exists on the map: Tonggu, written with the same pair 通谷, marks the very last point of the bladder channel on the outside of the little toe — known as Zutonggu, the foot-through-valley (BL66). Classical naming custom resolved the collision geographically, adding the region to the name: the belly version takes fu, the abdomen. So the full name Futonggu is partly wayfinding — it tells you not merely what kind of place this is but which of the two valleys you stand in. It also quietly announces membership of the kidney channel’s orderly abdominal ladder, whose stations march up beside the midline.

Pinyin renders the name Fùtōnggǔ, syllable by syllable: fù carries the falling fourth tone, tōng rides the flat high first tone, and gǔ dips through the third tone before rising. The hanzi trio 腹通谷 is compact enough to memorise at a glance. Like many channel points named for landscape and architecture — gates, courtyards, storehouses, ravines — the metaphor does real work. A treasury gathers and keeps; a gate admits and releases; a valley, in this naming tradition, gathers only to pass along. Tradition treats this spot as a place of transit rather than storage: whatever pools here is meant, in the old image, to keep moving.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications read: abdominal distension, abdominal pain, vomiting, and poor appetite. Framed as tradition rather than promise, each maps onto the valley image. Distension, in the classical account, is qi pooling without passage — the valley dammed. The abdominal pain these records describe belongs to the dull, blocked, cramping family that old texts associated with obstruction, not the sudden severe kind, which has always belonged to urgent care. Vomiting the tradition reads as qi reversing its proper downward course and surging upward instead — the valley running backwards. Poor appetite closes the set: the middle burner’s fire banked low, food left uninvited.

The dataset also files Futonggu among the crossing points — places where more than one pathway meets. Classical texts describe several of the kidney channel’s abdominal stations, this one included, as ground where its route crosses and shares company with another deep pathway of the trunk, the one later theory named the penetrating vessel. In the traditional reading, that meeting let this narrow strip beside the midline serve as a hinge between the body’s deep root and the digestive middle: the channel rising from below, the middle burner working at its side. Whether or not one follows the old physiology, the geography explains the historic portfolio — every recorded indication lives within reach of this one patch of upper-belly wall.

It bears repeating plainly: these are the traditional uses recorded in a licensed dataset and classical literature, offered here for cultural and historical understanding. They are not claims that pressing this point settles stomach trouble, and nothing here replaces assessment by a qualified clinician. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition lightly, and hold the safety rules firmly.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle in: seated, spine tall but unforced, belly soft. Curl your index and middle fingers together and rest the pads — never the tips — on the spot. Begin with contact alone for a few breaths, then add small, slow circles, letting the skin glide over the tissue beneath rather than pushing inward. Pressure should register as pleasant dullness or mild warmth, never sharpness. A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, matched where possible to slow exhales so each circle rides the breath out. Swap hands and repeat on the other side, keeping the two sessions symmetrical.

Quality matters more than duration. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the wellness register this page practises; hard jabbing teaches the body nothing and can leave the upper belly guarded for days. If your hands are cold, warm them first, because chilled muscle braces. Choose a quiet corner over a screen, since the practice doubles as a breathing pause: inhale through the nose, feel the upper abdomen expand under still fingertips, press gently on the long exhale. People who keep a routine often anchor it to an existing habit — after lunch, before the afternoon walk — because consistency, not intensity, is what self-care traditions actually ask for.

Treat the exercise as a small ceremony of attention rather than a treatment session. Notice what changes across weeks: perhaps the upper belly feels less clenched after meals, perhaps appetite behaves more sociably, perhaps nothing shifts except that you spend ninety seconds attending to your own middle. Every one of those outcomes fits the honest scope of self-acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — pressing harder, longer, or deeper to force a result. When curiosity pulls that way, return to gentleness or simply stop for the day.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

The licensed record for Futonggu lists no point-specific caution, and it is worth being precise about what that silence means. It is a fact about the dataset, not a licence: absence of a recorded warning is never permission to press harder, and the universal rules of gentle surface work govern here as everywhere. The upper abdominal wall shelters busy territory — the stomach’s own neighbourhood — and the body keeps its inner rooms private. Home acupressure honours that boundary by never leaving the surface at all.

For fingertip practice the rules follow plainly. Keep pressure light and strictly superficial, with finger pads only — no elbows, knuckles, or tools. Skip the point entirely during any episode of acute abdominal pain or active vomiting, during a feverish illness, and over broken or irritated skin; pause around heavy meals and return when digestion has quieted. Be cautious after recent abdominal injury or surgery until a clinician clears ordinary activity. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once and do not try again that day. Pregnant readers, and anyone living with a diagnosed digestive condition, should consult a qualified practitioner before adding any new point practice.

And the wider rule stands over everything: this page is educational, and acupressure is self-care, not diagnosis or therapy. Seek prompt medical care — not a pressure routine — for vomiting that persists beyond a day or two, severe or worsening abdominal pain, blood anywhere it should not be, unexplained weight loss, or any symptom that frightens you. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner, medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. The valley imagery is lovely, but the surest gift Futonggu offers most people is a calmer acquaintance with their own upper belly, treated kindly.
