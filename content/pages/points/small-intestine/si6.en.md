Yanglao — Nourishing the Aged — sits on the back of the forearm near the wrist, tucked into a bony cleft that opens beside the ulna’s rounded end when the palm turns toward the chest. Classical practice reached for it when eyesight dimmed and when ache settled into shoulder, back, elbow, arm, or lower back. This guide walks through finding it by touch and pressing it safely.

## Where Yanglao Sits on the Forearm {#where-it-sits}

Turn your attention to the back of the forearm, little-finger side, and you are standing at the right address. The dataset places Yanglao on the dorsum of the forearm, on the ulnar side, in the bony gap radial to the head of the ulna — language worth unpacking slowly. The forearm carries two long bones: the radius on the thumb side, the ulna on the little-finger side. Near the wrist, the ulna ends in a rounded knob — its head — plainly visible and palpable on the back of most wrists. The point sits not on the knob itself but against its rim facing the thumb, in the narrow cleft between that rounded end and whatever lies beside it.

The second half of the recorded location is a small piece of choreography: the gap is found by turning the palm to the chest so the gap opens. Hold the hand flat, back up, and the tissues on either side of the ulnar knob sit snug; rotate the hand until the palm faces your chest and the soft structures ease aside, uncovering a shallow groove along the knob’s thumb-facing edge. Practitioners have used this trick for centuries because it needs no ruler and no memorised distances: the body performs its own retraction. Many people can watch the groove appear rather than merely feel it, which makes this among the more satisfying points in the classical repertoire to locate unaided.

The terrain beneath is forgiving. Nothing delicate rides close to the surface here: under the skin lie tendon, bone, and working muscle, and the forearm’s back is among the least vulnerable stretches of the whole limb for gentle surface touch. Consistent with that geography, the licensed dataset records no specific cautions for this point — an emptiness this page reads carefully later, since a silent record describes the dataset’s scope rather than an exemption from care. For now it simply means the stakes of patient, superficial contact are low, and the search itself costs nothing but attention.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit comfortably and rest the back of your left forearm on a table or cushion, elbow softly bent. Begin with orientation rather than searching: with the right hand, feel for the prominent rounded knob at the lower end of the ulna on the back of the wrist, little-finger side. Trace its edges slowly; most wrists display it clearly, especially with the fingers relaxed. Once the knob is fixed under your fingers, slide to its edge facing the thumb and rest the fingertip there, in the shallow valley between bone and neighbour.

Now the signature move. Keeping the fingertip parked in that valley, turn the left hand slowly until its palm faces your chest. As the hand rotates, feel the neighbouring soft tissue step away and the groove deepen under your finger — many people see it happen. Compare sides afterward: find the mirror spot on the right wrist and notice how both grooves answer light pressure with the same faint, dull awareness, while a finger-width away there is little to feel. If the first attempt finds only flat bone, nudge the fingertip slightly further toward the thumb side and repeat the rotation, unhurried.

Two slips account for most misses. The first is landing on the knob itself instead of beside it: the point hugs the rim facing the thumb, not the summit facing up. The second is rotating too far — past the moment when the gap stands widest — so the tissues crowd back in; the groove is easiest to hold at roughly the halfway point of the turn. When in doubt, reset: hand back down, find the knob again, slide to its thumb-facing rim, and turn once more, slowly. Patience does the work here; force has nothing to add.

## Reading the Name: Nourishing the Aged {#name-and-meaning}

The name reads like a small benediction. Yang 養 means to nourish, to care for, to sustain — the character behind everyday words for feeding, tending, and providing for elders. Lao 老 means aged, old, elder. Together Yǎnglǎo is conventionally rendered Nourishing the Aged, and the pairing is affectionate rather than clinical: this is a point named for looking after people in their later years. Commentators through the centuries connected the name directly with the complaint the records list first — dimming vision — since failing eyesight stood, in every century before recent ones, among the commonest troubles of age.

Pinyin renders the name Yǎnglǎo. As written, both syllables carry the dipping third tone — the tone marks sit over the vowels of each syllable exactly so, and the registry records them that way. In flowing Mandarin speech, two third tones meeting in sequence do not stay put: routine tone sandhi lifts the first syllable toward a rising note, so the name sounds out beginning higher than it is written. None of this needs mastering to use the page; the marks are simply an honest transcript of the melody, and the two characters 養老 are compact enough to recognise at a glance.

Names of this kind repay slow reading. The tradition names points for gates, valleys, pools, courtyards, and gathering places, and every name quietly argues a theory of what the spot is for. Nourishing-the-Aged argues stewardship: a place that supports a system under long-term load rather than rescuing it mid-crisis. Read that way, the record falls into step — why a forearm point appears in discussions of aging eyes, and why ache across shoulder and back dominates its list. One classical office, described below, adds a surprising twist to so tender a name.

## A Xi-Cleft Point: Where Channel Qi Dives Deep {#the-xi-cleft-office}

Classical teaching assigns each of the twelve principal channels one xi-cleft point — the catalogue files Yanglao under exactly that label — and describes such stations as places where the channel’s qi gathers and dives deep. The image commentators favoured is geological: where a stream runs over open rock, most of the current stays near the surface, but at a crevice the water drops and continues underground. A xi-cleft point, in that picture, is a skylight over the deep channel — access to the current where it runs strongest and least visible.

Tradition reserved these deep-access stations chiefly for sudden complaints. Where a channel’s territory flared abruptly — pain arriving sharply rather than smouldering — the classical art of selecting points reached first for its cleft, reasoning that trouble riding high and fresh answers best where qi plunges lowest. Long-smouldering conditions belonged elsewhere in the old repertoire. This is framework logic, offered as cultural context rather than physiology; but it organises the record neatly, and readers who know the label can predict much of any cleft point’s historic portfolio before reading it.

For the small intestine channel, that office belongs to Yanglao — the sixth of the channel’s nineteen recorded stations. Knowing this reframes the indications below: they read like the complaints classical writers expected a cleft point to meet, sudden aches along the channel’s own route and abrupt troubles within its facial reach. It also explains why the point turns up less often in discussions of slow, chronic patterns than its tender name might suggest. Two vocabularies — care and acuteness — share one address here, and the tension between them is part of what makes this station interesting.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The licensed record lists three indications for Yanglao: blurred vision; aching pain of the shoulder, back, elbow, and arm; and lower back pain. Read as tradition rather than promise, the list sorts cleanly. The aching cluster belongs to the doorstep: classical texts draw the small intestine channel beginning at the little finger and climbing the back of the arm — past the wrist where this point lives, along the forearm and elbow, up into the shoulder region — before turning toward the neck, cheek, and face. Discomfort anywhere along that mapped route fell naturally within the channel’s traditional jurisdiction, and its cleft point inherited the file.

Blurred vision looks like the outlier until two threads join. First, the map: the classical course carries the channel onward past cheek and ear to the inner corner of the eye, placing sight inside the territory it surveys — the same map that lets the channel appear in old discussions of neck and ear complaints. Second, the name: a station called Nourishing the Aged was always going to be consulted about aging eyes. Whether the name grew from the indication or the indication gathered around the name, commentators have treated the two as one story for centuries. Neither thread is modern evidence; together they explain why the record reads as it does.

Lower back pain completes the trio, and here commentators leaned on kinship between same-named channels. The small intestine channel belongs to the taiyang family of the hand; drawn along the spine and back is its foot-side partner of the same name, whose territory is the bladder channel’s. Classical writers habitually treated channels sharing a name as colleagues sharing a district, so the hand-taiyang’s cleft point could be consulted for trouble in the taiyang country of the lower back — especially trouble arriving suddenly, the register cleft points were kept for. Again: traditional cartography and commentary, not anatomy, and not a claim about outcomes.

And the standing honesty clause applies here as everywhere on this site. These indications are transcribed from a licensed dataset and classical literature for cultural and historical understanding; they are not claims that pressing this point treats disease. Where modern research has touched self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and heavily context-dependent, and evidence tied to any single point remains thin. Blurred vision deserves particular sobriety: changes in sight belong with an eye professional promptly, whatever else a person chooses to do alongside. Hold the tradition as a lens, never as a prescription.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle in: seated, forearm resting back-up on a cushion, shoulders easy. Bring the pad of a single finger — never the tip, never a knuckle — onto the groove and begin with contact alone, letting the hand learn the texture beneath. After a few slow breaths, add small circles, keeping the skin gliding rather than dragging. Aim for a sensation of pleasant dullness or mild spreading warmth, holding the rotation loosely in mind: some people find the groove answers best with the palm half-turned toward the chest during the session itself.

A common home rhythm is one to three minutes per side, once or twice daily, matched loosely to the exhale so each circle rides the breath out; then swap forearms and give the other side its turn. Quality outranks quantity throughout this register: light, patient, regularly repeated contact is the entire art, and harder pressing adds risk without teaching the tissue anything. Warm cold hands first, since chilled muscle tightens under touch. Choose a quiet corner rather than a screen, and let the few minutes double as a pause — inhale through the nose, notice the wrist beneath still fingers, lean the smallest degree of pressure into the long exhale.

Keep expectations sized to the practice. Self-acupressure is a ritual of attention with a modest, honest scope: perhaps the forearm feels looser afterward, perhaps the pause itself is the benefit, perhaps nothing measurable happens at all — every one of those outcomes is legitimate. What falls outside the scope is escalation, pressing harder or longer to force a result, and any hope of the point substituting for professional care, above all for anything involving the eyes. When curiosity pulls toward escalation, return to gentleness, or close the day’s session early.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

Begin with what the record says — and with what it does not. The licensed dataset records no specific cautions for Yanglao, and the point’s geography supports that calm: the back of the forearm offers tendon and bone to the fingertip, with no fragile structure close beneath the surface. An empty caution field, though, describes the dataset’s silence rather than an exemption from care; the universal rules governing every point on this site apply here in full, and the guidance below leans deliberately conservative.

The rules themselves are simple. Keep contact strictly superficial: finger pads only, light pressure, no tools or devices, nothing pressed through pain. Skip the point wherever skin is broken, rashy, bruised, or recently injured, and skip it entirely during feverish or acute illness. Readers who are pregnant, or managing conditions or taking medicines that affect clotting or skin fragility, should ask a qualified practitioner before adding new point work. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation under the finger means stopping immediately and leaving the point alone for the day. None of this is unique to Yanglao; together these lines form simply the floor beneath every self-care practice on this site.

Finally, the referral lines, drawn firmly and especially around the eyes. New or worsening blurred vision, floaters, flashes, eye pain, or any sudden change in sight belongs with an eye-care professional promptly — urgently if the change comes suddenly. Persistent or severe low back pain, pain following injury, numbness or weakness spreading down a leg, and shoulder pain after trauma all call for proper assessment rather than home measures. Acupressure may sit alongside such care as a comfort practice; it cannot precede or replace it. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.
