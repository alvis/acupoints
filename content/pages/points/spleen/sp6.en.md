Sanyinjiao, the Three Yin Intersection, sits on the inner side of the lower leg, a hand-width above the knob of the inner ankle, just behind the shinbone’s inner ridge. Classical practice named it the meeting ground of the body’s three yin channels and reached for it widely, from women’s cycles to digestion and sleep. It carries one of the tradition’s weightiest warnings: pregnancy.

## Where Sanyinjiao Sits on the Inner Leg {#where-it-sits}

The dataset gives the address in one measured sentence: on the medial aspect of the lower leg, 3 cun above the tip of the medial malleolus, posterior to the medial border of the tibia. Each term earns a translation into ordinary language. Medial means inner — the side of the leg that faces its partner, so the two inner sides nearly touch when you stand with your feet together. The medial malleolus is the familiar bony knob at the inner ankle, the downward end of the shinbone, or tibia. Three cun above its tip sets the height, and posterior to the medial border of the tibia sets the line of approach: not atop the bone, but just behind its inner ridge, on the soft side where the calf begins. Assembled, the address reads: find the inner ankle knob, measure straight up, then slip behind the shinbone’s inner edge into the groove between bone and calf.

A cun, as throughout this literature, is not a ruler measurement but a proportional one, taken from the body being examined — a thumb-width on a broad-framed adult differs from one on a slight teenager. Tradition preserves a handy approximation built for exactly this height: the four-finger measure, in which the four fingers of one hand are placed together at their widest and their combined breadth is taken as roughly three cun of the person being measured. Set the stacked fingers just above the inner ankle knob, and the top of the stack marks the height where Sanyinjiao waits. Every leg carries its own mirror copy of the point, and the classical maps treat the pair as a single address written twice, worked symmetrically in practice.

What lies beneath rewards a gentle hand. The fingertip settles into a shallow channel between the tibia’s inner ridge and the fullness of the calf, over skin, fascia, and soft muscle — structures that answer patience far better than force. Sensation here runs characteristically deep and dull, a spreading heaviness that many people notice further along the leg than under the finger itself, and that quality has long been part of the point’s reputation among practitioners of manual technique. Nothing about locating or contacting this spot requires strength; the landmarks are clear, and the tissues prefer courtesy.

## A Self-Palpation Walkthrough {#finding-it-by-touch}

Sit comfortably and rest one ankle across the opposite knee, or simply lean forward and bring the leg within easy reach. Find the tip of the inner ankle knob first, and park a fingertip on it. Stack the four fingers of your other hand — index through little finger, held together at their widest — directly above the knob, and note where the top of the stack crosses the leg: that line stands in for three cun. Now slide your searching fingertips horizontally backward until they meet the hard inner edge of the shinbone, then let them settle into the soft groove immediately behind that edge. A faint, dull awareness, quite unlike the mute feel of nearby tissue, usually confirms the spot. Repeat the whole search on the other leg, unhurried.

A few checks steady the result before any real pressing begins. Compare sides: the awareness should sit at the same height and depth on each leg, symmetrical as bookends. Wiggle the foot gently under a resting fingertip — the point travels with the skin rather than hiding deeper down. Try feather-light contact at the candidate spot and a thumb-width away: the true location typically answers with more of that spreading dullness while its surroundings stay quiet. If you find yourself on bare bone, slide backward off the ridge half a finger-width; if you are buried in the dense belly of the calf, come forward until the bone edge reappears and settle behind it again.

The classic mistakes are worth naming because they are common. Measuring from the outer ankle knob instead of the inner one leads astray twice over: the lateral malleolus sits lower and farther back than the medial one, so a measurement taken from it lands both short and displaced. Starting the search without first fixing the ankle tip leaves the guesswork free to wander up the calf, stranding fingers among look-alike grooves. And pressing squarely on the bone crest rather than behind it buys bruised soreness but no confirmation. Whenever doubt creeps in, return to the ankle knob, remeasure once, slowly, and let the four-finger stack do its quiet work again.

## Reading the Name: Three Yin Intersection {#name-and-meaning}

Three characters, plainly spoken. San 三 means three. Yin 陰 is the shaded half of the yin-yang pairing — in the body’s traditional cartography, the inner surfaces, the solid organs, the cooling and nourishing currents. Jiao 交 means to cross, to join, to intersect. Read together, the name announces itself: the place where three yin lines meet. Few point names state their geography so baldly, and commentators across the centuries have treated this one less as metaphor than as map legend.

The map it annotates runs along the inner leg. Classical channel theory draws three yin channels climbing that inner territory — the spleen, kidney, and liver channels of the foot — and the oldest descriptions say the three converge at this spot, which the catalogue duly records as a crossing-point. Even the dataset’s own safety note speaks of “this crossing of the liver, spleen, and kidney channels.” Each partner brings its portfolio to the meeting: the spleen governing transformation, transportation, and the holding of blood; the liver storing blood and keeping its flow smooth; the kidney governing water, essence, and the deep reserves. A triple junction joining three such offices explains, in the tradition’s own terms, why one point on the lower leg gathered so many duties.

Pinyin renders the name Sanyinjiao, and the dictionary confirms what the catalogue prints: every syllable rides the first tone — san on a steady high level, yin likewise level, jiao closing on another high, flat note. Three level tones for a name about threefold meeting; even the sound is symmetrical. The written form 三陰交 is spare enough to take in at a glance, and like the gates, valleys, and courtyards scattered along other channels, it compresses a chapter of theory into three characters. The name does not merely label the point; it recites the reason classical practice kept returning to it.

## Traditional Indications, Explained {#traditional-use}

The recorded indications open with the widest cluster in the list: irregular menstruation, painful menstruation, uterine bleeding, vaginal discharge, absent menstruation, prolapse of the uterus, difficult labour, and infertility. The traditional logic gathers quickly around the name. Classical teaching made the spleen the steward of blood, holding it within its pathways; the liver stores blood and keeps its movement easy; the kidney anchors the womb’s nourishment. A point where all three channels meet therefore stood at the natural crossroads for discussions of the cycle and its disorders, and the old literature treats Sanyinjiao accordingly. Framed honestly for modern readers: these are recorded traditional uses, not outcomes promised by pressing a point. Several entries map onto matters that belong promptly with licensed care — unexplained bleeding, periods that vanish, struggles to conceive, prolapse — and childbirth belongs in modern medical hands, whatever the old records describe.

The middle of the list turns to water and to the male reproductive sphere: nocturnal emission, impotence, premature ejaculation, bedwetting, retention of urine, then edema, abdominal distension, and diarrhoea. Again the junction explains the company. In the traditional frame the kidney governs water and the lower body’s gates, the spleen moves fluids and runs the digestive transformation called transportation, and the liver keeps the body’s flows unimpeded — so their shared crossing was read as a control room for waterways and for the urinary and reproductive functions clustered nearby. Bedwetting and retention, distension and loose stool, swelling and emission: the old records filed them all under one roof, because the channels were thought to share one wall.

The remaining entries close the circle: weakness of the legs, eczema, urticaria, insomnia, headache and dizziness. The first is almost literal — the channel climbs the very limb whose heaviness or weariness it was consulted for. Skin and sleep connect in classical thinking through blood: when the spleen’s stewardship ran well, blood was plentiful and settled, skin was nourished, and the mind could rest at night; when it faltered, itch and restless nights appeared on the same page of discussion. It bears repeating plainly: all of these are traditional uses preserved in a licensed dataset and older literature, offered here for cultural understanding. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. Hold the tradition as a lens, never a prescription.

## Self-Care Acupressure at Home {#self-care-practice}

With the point located by the walkthrough above, settle in: seated, the leg supported, hands warm — cold fingers grip and rush. Rest the pad of a thumb, braced by curled fingers, on the groove behind the shinbone’s inner edge, and begin with still contact for a few slow breaths. Then add small, unhurried circles, letting the skin glide over the tissues beneath rather than grinding against bone. Aim for a pleasant, spreading dullness — the sensation this point is traditionally known for — never sharpness. One to three minutes per side is a generous session; many people repeat it once or twice a day, timing the firmer moments of each circle to the exhale. Finish by giving the other leg its identical minutes.

Quality outweighs duration, as with every practice on this site. Light, patient, regularly repeated contact fits the register of self-care; hard digging teaches nothing and risks sore tissue. Anchor the habit where it will survive busy weeks — after the evening wash, beside the morning tea — because consistency is what these traditions actually ask for. An evening slot suits Sanyinjiao particularly well in the traditional rhythm of things: the day set down, the legs tired, a few quiet minutes paid to the channel that climbed them. Treat the exercise as attention given, not treatment received.

Keep expectations the size of the practice. Perhaps the leg feels warmer; perhaps breathing slows and shoulders drop; perhaps nothing announces itself beyond ten calm minutes of looking after your own body. Every one of those outcomes fits the honest scope of acupressure. What does not fit is escalation — pressing harder or longer to force a result, working through pain, or turning a comfort ritual into a project. When curiosity pulls toward more, return to gentleness, or stop for the day; the point will still be there tomorrow.

## Cautions and Safe Practice {#cautions-safety}

This section opens with the loudest entry in the licensed record, because it deserves the headline. The dataset cautions practitioners to needle with caution during pregnancy, recording the classical experience that stimulating this crossing of the liver, spleen, and kidney channels may induce miscarriage. Whatever one makes of the mechanism, the instruction home practice inherits from that sentence is categorical rather than nuanced: if you are pregnant, do not press this point at all — not lightly, not briefly, not experimentally. If you are trying to conceive, or pregnancy is possible, ask a qualified practitioner before adding Sanyinjiao to anything. No suggested benefit of tradition outweighs a warning written this plainly, and the warning travels with the point wherever it appears in the old books.

Away from pregnancy, the ordinary disciplines of surface self-care carry the load. Use finger pads only — never elbows, knuckles, or tools — and keep pressure light enough that skin and superficial tissue do all the work. Skip the point over broken, irritated, or infected skin, during feverish illness, and after recent injury or surgery on the leg until a clinician clears ordinary activity. Stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation, and do not retry that day. People living with diabetes, poor circulation, or reduced sensation in the legs should ask a clinician before making leg work a habit. And heavy menstrual bleeding, severe pelvic pain, inability to pass urine, or any symptom that frightens you belongs with prompt professional assessment — never managed at the shin with a thumb.

And the wider rule stands over everything, as on every page of this site: this material is educational, acupressure is self-care rather than diagnosis or therapy, and persistent symptoms belong with qualified practitioners — medical or East Asian medical as suits the complaint. The honest gift Sanyinjiao offers most readers is modest and real: a better acquaintance with the inner landscape of the lower leg, a few calm minutes of practiced attention, and a vivid reminder that some points carry histories heavy enough to deserve handling with both interest and restraint.
