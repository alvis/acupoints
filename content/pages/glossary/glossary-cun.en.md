The cun is East Asian medicine’s body-proportional unit of distance: counted not in tape-measure centimetres but in widths taken from the person being measured. This page explains where the idea comes from, how the finger-width shortcuts work, and how to read the cun counts you will meet across this site.

## A Ruler You Were Born With {#a-unit-cut-to-size}

Every craft that works on the human frame eventually needs to say how far, and East Asian medicine answered with an unusual instrument: the body itself. A cun (寸 — pronounced cùn, on a falling tone) is a proportional unit of distance, roughly the width of the recipient’s own thumb at the knuckle. It is not a fixed length stamped onto rulers everywhere; it grows and shrinks with whoever is being measured. The same cun narrows on a child, widens across a broad-shouldered adult, and quietly adjusts again as bodies change through life.

On this site the unit does quiet work in nearly every point page. Location strings such as “2 cun above the wrist crease” or “1.5 cun lateral to the midline” look like geometry homework at first glance, yet they are really invitations: take a landmark, head in the stated direction, and stack that many of your own hand-widths in between. This page unpacks the habit — where the idea came from, how the shortcuts work, and how to decode the counts waiting across the catalogue.

## “Same-Body” Inches: Tóngshēn Cùn {#same-body-inch}

Traditional teaching gives the principle a name of its own: tóngshēn cùn (同身寸), usually rendered as “the body’s own inch”. Measuring tool and measured body belong to the same person. Classical sources are credited with developing the thought along two complementary tracks. One track fixes proportions between surface landmarks: old bone-measure tables (gǔdù) divide whole regions into equal parts — twelve between the elbow crease and the wrist crease, five between the navel and the pubic bone — so any reader can rebuild the map on their own frame. The other track borrows a tool nobody can leave at home: the fingers.

Both tracks serve one purpose: keeping location talk portable between bodies. Advice pinned to a fixed scale would wander badly between a seven-year-old and a six-foot adult; a count expressed in the patient’s own widths travels intact across ages and builds. That portability is why the convention endured, and why it still organises modern catalogues — including the one behind this site’s point pages.

## Finger-Width Shortcuts, Worked Through {#finger-width-shortcuts}

Four handy equivalents cover nearly everything a home reader meets. One cun: the width of your own thumb at the knuckle. One and a half cun: the index and middle fingers held together. Two cun: three fingers — index, middle, and ring — side by side. Three cun: all four fingers gathered snugly in a row. None of this is laboratory equipment; each is a friendly approximation, and the worked examples below show how far the friendliness stretches.

Set against real entries, the shortcuts earn their keep. Pericardium 6 sits 2 cun above the wrist crease: lay three fingers across the inner wrist starting at the crease, and the point waits just beyond the topmost finger, between the two central tendons. Spleen 6 rests 3 cun above the tip of the inner anklebone: four fingers laid flat mark the height, with the point tucked against the back border of the shin bone. Lung 7 hides about 1.5 cun above the wrist crease on the thumb side — a two-finger journey upward. Stomach 36 lies 3 cun below the hollow under the outer knee, the four-finger measure once again.

Fractions are ordinary company. Narrow territory invites halves: several entries around the forehead sit 0.5 cun inside the front hairline — half a thumb-width into delicate ground. The long ladder of back points stands 1.5 cun to either side of the spine, which is precisely the two-finger width. Whenever a location mixes a whole number with a fraction, the little finger kit above already holds the right tool.

## Reading Cun Distances on This Site {#reading-cun-on-this-site}

Catalogue sentences follow a steady grammar: a region, a landmark, a direction, a count. “On the lower abdomen, on the anterior midline, 4 cun below the centre of the umbilicus” decodes to: start at your navel, face down the midline, and walk four thumb-widths. Direction words do the steering — above and below along the limbs, lateral to for sideways departures from the body’s centre line.

Beginners fare best with a fixed order of operations: find the landmark first, confirm the direction second, stack the widths last. Most misses come from rushing straight to the counting. And when one person helps locate a point for another, the measuring hand should belong to the person being measured — the whole scheme presumes that ruler and body know each other.

Every point page on this site restates its recorded location in plain steps and adds palpation hints of its own, so the raw cun strings are never the last word. Treat this page as the decoder ring those pages quietly assume you are already holding.

## Why Proportions Beat Tape Measures {#proportions-beat-tape-measures}

The deeper logic is that landmarks travel with movement. Tendons slide when a wrist bends; the anklebone’s tip rides higher the moment the foot points; the navel stays put while everything around it rises and settles with posture and breath. An external ruler cannot follow that choreography, but a proportional unit inherits it automatically. Tradition anchored its counts accordingly: to creases, tips, hollows, and centres that announce themselves on the surface before any counting begins.

Honesty requires the limits too. Finger widths are estimates, not edicts; hands relate only loosely to the frames they belong to, and traditional teachers seem to have known it — the landmark tables remained the reference, with fingers serving as quick field tools alongside. Modern teaching passes the same caution along: treat finger counts as first guesses, then let tendons and bones cast the deciding vote.

## Measuring Is Harmless; Pressing Takes Care {#measuring-is-harmless}

Locating a point costs nothing and risks nothing; the care questions begin the moment pressure does. Keep presses light and comfortable, avoid broken, inflamed, or numbed skin, and stop at once on any sharp, electric, or radiating sensation. During pregnancy, or alongside an ongoing condition or regular medication, ask a qualified practitioner before adding self-pressure routines of any kind.

Precision deserves one final boundary. Knowing exactly where a point sits does not turn touching it into treatment. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and no refinement of coordinates changes that. Enjoy the deliberate slowness for what it is. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.
