The yuan-source point is classical East Asian medicine’s idea of a doorway: one spot on each of the twelve principal channels where the channel’s own vitality was pictured surfacing, close enough to touch. This page unpacks the idea and shows where beginners meet it.

## Springs and Sources: What the Name Carries {#springs-sources}

In the river-and-spring imagery through which classical texts described the channels, qi was imagined moving through the body the way water moves across a landscape — rising, gathering, deepening, running toward the sea. Somewhere along every channel, the old writings place a spot where the flow wells up from a deeper spring: the yuan-source point, whose name means exactly that, source or origin. Tradition treats such a spot as belonging to the organ the channel serves — less a way-station on the route than the place where the organ itself was thought to put in an appearance at the surface.

It helps to hold this as a piece of classical cartography rather than anatomy. Pre-modern authors organised observation of the body around correspondences — organ to channel, channel to season, emotion to element — and the source point gave each correspondence a single address a fingertip could rest on. Nothing here is offered as measurable physiology. It is a coherent, centuries-old way of thinking, and meeting it on its own terms is the whole purpose of this glossary page.

## One Window on Every Channel {#twelve-windows}

The classical scheme is tidy. Each of the twelve principal channels carries exactly one yuan-source point, making twelve in all, and they gather near the wrists and ankles, where the channels run close to bone and tendon. Classical physiology pictured the body’s deeper vitality, the yuan qi, as reaching the surface through these twelve openings. Finer questions of placement and commentary belong to the individual point pages; this overview keeps to the shape of the idea.

On the six yin channels — lung, pericardium, heart, spleen, liver, and kidney — the source point does double duty, sharing its address with the channel’s shu-stream point, one of the five classical transport stages. The site’s catalogue reflects this plainly: taixi and taichong both carry the two tags side by side in their recorded categories. On the six yang channels the two roles sit at separate addresses.

Classical teaching hung further labels on these stations, elemental affinities among them, and the registry records those labels wherever the dataset carries them. None of it changes the feel of the concept: twelve small windows, one per channel, each traditionally read as a glimpse of the organ standing behind it.

## How Tradition Read These Stations {#reading-the-window}

How did practitioners use the window? Classical theory and case-writing alike describe attending to the source points — noting what the finger found there and reading it against everything else in the examination. Several channels run close to arteries at the wrist, so this attention merged with pulse-taking itself, and refined traditions of reading grew around that small stretch of ground. Told here, this is history: a portrait of how pre-modern medicine looked at the body, not a claim about what a modern fingertip can detect.

The names repay attention too, because the imagery lives in them. Taixi, the kidney channel’s source point, joins tai, great, with xi, a mountain stream; taichong pairs great with chong, a surge or thoroughfare; hegu — the joining valley — pictures the metacarpal bones rising like hillsides on either side of the soft web of the hand. Such glosses come down through layers of commentary rather than any single text, and they are offered as the poetry of naming, not as hidden instructions.

## Meeting Source Points on This Site {#meeting-them-on-this-site}

On this site the concept stops being abstract quickly, because several catalogued points carry the yuan-source tag outright. Three suit a first encounter especially well. Hegu (LI4) sits on the back of the hand, in the muscular web between thumb and index finger — the joining valley of its name. Taichong (LV3) occupies the top of the foot, in the hollow just ahead of where the first two long bones of the foot meet. Taixi (KI3) rests at the inner ankle, in the hollow between the tip of the ankle bone and the Achilles tendon.

Each of the three has a page of its own in the catalogue, carrying the precise location notes, the actions recorded in the licensed dataset, and any cautions the data includes. This glossary page deliberately keeps to the idea, and the point pages keep the particulars; reading one after the other is the intended route. Concept first, address second.

Beginners meet source points in a quieter way as well: they surface constantly in introductory acupressure material precisely because tradition rated them so highly. When a well-loved hand or foot point turns out to be a channel’s source point, the surprise usually runs backwards — the point was already familiar long before its classical title came into view.

## Exploring Them Gently at Home {#gentle-exploration}

Exploring a source point at home asks nothing beyond the site-wide rules of self-acupressure. Settle a fingertip or the pad of a thumb on the spot, let the pressure grow only until it feels comfortable, and stay awhile — measured by several slow breaths rather than a clock — then release gradually. Pleasant heaviness, or a warmth that spreads, is the sensation self-acupressure looks for; anything sharp, electric, or radiating is the signal to stop at once, because it means tissue rather than point.

Tradition supplies a pleasing habit for the curious: the hand-and-foot pairing. Older teaching often discusses hegu and taichong together, one on each pair of limbs, as a matched set, and readers who enjoy the classical logic sometimes settle on the two in turn during the same quiet sitting. Whether or not one follows the pairing, the method stays identical: light contact, patience, no force.

A few boundaries keep the practice friendly. Stay off broken, inflamed, or numb skin, and leave bruises and swellings alone. Treat pregnancy, ongoing illness, and regular medication — particularly anything that affects bleeding — as reasons to ask a qualified practitioner before adding self-pressure routines. Self-pressure accompanies care; it never stands in for assessment.

## Realistic Expectations {#expectations}

What may a reader honestly expect? First, the goods that arrive regardless: a few minutes of deliberate stillness, attention turned toward the body, slower breathing — value that stands whether or not the old geography proves out. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and nothing in that literature sets source points apart as special; the window idea belongs to tradition, not to trials. This page promises no effect and ranks no point above another.

As heritage, though, the concept repays the visit. Twelve windows, one per channel, each named for water finding the light — it is among the more elegant pieces of the old correspondence system, and knowing it turns a scatter of map-dots into a landscape with a story worth following.

## When to Seek Professional Care {#when-to-seek-care}

Symptoms belong with professionals, whatever any wellness page suggests. Anything urgent — chest pain, breathlessness, sudden weakness, severe pain of any kind — calls for prompt medical assessment, not a fingertip. Complaints that persist — fatigue, swelling, numbness, pain that keeps returning — deserve the same, sooner rather than later. Licensed practitioners of Western and East Asian medicine can offer informed perspectives alongside conventional care, and the sensible expectation is coordination rather than replacement.

Between emergencies and everyday comfort lies ordinary curiosity, which this site exists to serve: wander the catalogue, meet hegu, taichong, and taixi on their own pages, and let the classical idea of the source point sharpen what you notice along the channels. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.
