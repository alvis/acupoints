De qi, the arrival sensation of classical East Asian medicine, names the dull, heavy, spreading feeling tradition reads as a point answering your touch. This guide explains what the term means, which sensations are welcome, which are stop signs, and how to read your own body kindly.

## What De Qi Names {#what-de-qi-names}

De qi joins two small Chinese characters: de, to obtain or arrive, and qi, the circulating vitality that classical physiology placed at the centre of health. Written 得氣, the compound is usually rendered “the arrival of qi” — the moment, as classical texts describe it, when a treated point stops being an address on a map and becomes a place the body visibly answers. Pre-modern writings treat the experience as a familiar, almost matter-of-fact event: something a practitioner watches for, the way a carpenter watches for the bite of a plane into wood.

Inside the tradition, arrival mattered for a practical reason. A point, in the classical picture, is not merely a coordinate but a meeting place of channel activity, and de qi was read as confirmation that the place had been genuinely engaged rather than simply touched. Generations of teaching material therefore circle one question — how do you know you are truly on the point? — and return the same broad answer: the body tells you, through a quality of sensation distinct from ordinary pressure on skin or muscle. This page keeps that answer in its historical frame; whether arrival corresponds to anything measurable belongs to modern research, discussed honestly further down.

On this site the term keeps a modest, everyday job. The glossary defines de qi as the dull, heavy, spreading family of sensations that suggests a classical point has been located well, and the point pages assume you have met the word. What follows unpacks that definition gently: which sensations belong to the welcome family, which ones are always stop signs here, and how to practise telling them apart without turning your kitchen table into a clinic.

## Heavy, Dull, Spreading: The Welcome Family {#the-welcome-family}

Ask what arrival feels like and tradition answers with a small vocabulary of weight. A welcome sensation is usually dull rather than pointed: a spreading heaviness, a sense of fullness or gentle achiness, warmth pooling under the fingertip, sometimes a faint fizz of pins-and-needles that stays comfortable. The classic image is of something that arrives and then settles — a sensation that grows softer and rounder as you hold still, rather than sharper as you press harder.

Time and texture separate this family from ordinary touch. Pressing any spot on the forearm produces pressure; what marks the welcome family apart, in the traditional account, is character rather than intensity — heavy where plain pressure feels flat, diffuse where a miss feels like a single pinpoint, inclined to linger and deepen after you stop increasing force. Beginners often notice it most clearly where tissue changes texture under the finger: a groove meeting a tendon, the soft edge of a hollow. None of this requires belief; it is close attention to ordinary sensation, practised slowly.

## Sharp, Electric, Radiating: The Stop Signs {#stop-sign-sensations}

Three kinds of sensation are never welcome in self-acupressure, and this site treats all three as immediate stop signals. Sharp means a focused stab or sting — the feel of pressing a nerve or landing on a bone edge. Electric means a sudden jolt or buzz, bright and fast, quite unlike the slow weight of arrival. Radiating means a sensation that shoots or travels — down the arm, along a line, away from where your finger actually rests. Whichever of the three appears, the instruction is identical: stop, and take the finger away.

Older accounts complicate the picture, and honesty means saying so. Classical writings and later practitioner accounts describe sensations said to travel along a channel during professional treatment, and tradition often welcomed that travelling as a sign of strong response. Home practice runs under a deliberately stricter rule. Sitting alone, with no training in reading such events, you have no reliable way to tell a harmless spread from a warning; the only guide available is comfort. So the site draws the line firmly: anything sharp, electric, or clearly travelling is a signal to stop, whatever the old stories celebrate.

What to do after stopping matters too. Ease off completely, breathe, and notice whether the sensation fades within moments — it usually does. If you want to continue, shift position slightly, lighten your touch, and try again on a nearby spot; if the same sharpness returns even at feather-light pressure, take that as the day’s answer and choose a different point, or leave practice for today. Tenderness that lingers after a session deserves the same respect as any other persistent symptom.

## Practising the Difference {#telling-them-apart}

The vocabulary becomes useful only through unhurried comparison. Choose a point whose location you trust, rest a fingertip with almost no force, and simply wait through several slow breaths. Then move a centimetre or two away and press again with the same gentleness. Most people notice the difference not as a shout but as a change of character — flat here, rounded and heavier there — and that quiet contrast is precisely the skill the old term names.

Two habits keep the practice safe while you learn. First, climb in pressure like a dimmer, not a switch: add force only until something interesting appears, never past comfort. Second, respect the conditions that scramble sensation altogether — numbed or broken skin, areas you cannot feel clearly, days when exhaustion blurs everything. If you live with diabetes or another condition affecting sensation, or you are pregnant, ask a qualified practitioner before adding self-pressure routines; reading your own body is the heart of this craft, and some situations hand you a muted deck.

## Where You Meet De Qi on This Site {#where-you-meet-it}

Once named, the idea turns up everywhere in the catalogue. Point pages describe each location precisely enough for you to test the vocabulary yourself; region and meridian guides repeat the same stop rules you have read here; and the glossary entry you came from keeps the short version handy. The site never asks you to chase a dramatic experience. Its standing suggestion is smaller: press gently, notice what actually happens, and let the old vocabulary organise the noticing.

A good first experiment uses two neighbouring landmarks rather than one. Take a familiar forearm point, meet it with patient lightness, then compare a second spot a short distance along the same line. Holding both impressions side by side — one flatter, one fuller — teaches more than any definition could, and it costs nothing but a minute. If nothing distinctive appears, nothing is wrong; sensitivity varies enormously between people and between days, and the practice remains worthwhile as unhurried attention either way.

## Arrival Is Not a Promise {#honest-framing}

One caution closes the loop. Feeling de qi confirms nothing beyond sensation: not that a complaint is improving, not that a point is working, not that anything therapeutic is under way. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and nothing on this page asks you to read a particular feeling as a sign of benefit. Treat arrival as information about location and comfort, in the traditional sense — never as a certificate of effect.

Keep the boundary between comfort and care equally firm. Self-acupressure is a low-cost way of slowing down; it is not assessment, diagnosis, or treatment, and a pleasant sensation never licenses ignoring a symptom that persists, worsens, or frightens you. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.
