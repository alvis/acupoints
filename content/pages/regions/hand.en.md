The wrist, palm, and fingers form a small country with a busy border: six arm meridians arrive here or set out from here, and the catalogue keeps three well-loved points in the region — hegu (LI4), shenmen (HT7), and houxi (SI3). A tour of the landscape, the tradition behind it, and gentle ways to explore it at home.

## The Hand at a Glance {#hand-at-a-glance}

Open the classical map of East Asian medicine and look at its edges: wherever a limb runs out of road, a channel turns around. Nowhere is that turnover denser than at the hand. The registry describes the region simply — wrist, palm, and fingers, marked with the single character 手 — and calls it the distal terminus of the six arm meridians. Anatomy explains why the neighbourhood feels crowded: twenty-seven small bones share the space with rope-like tendons, delicate joints, and sheets of thin skin, so every square centimetre carries structure close to the surface. Classical writers found the crowding meaningful. They pictured the hand as a place of arrivals and departures, where the body’s deep currents surface, change direction, and hand themselves on.

This page is a companion to the whole region rather than to any single channel. It walks through the six arm meridians that meet here, introduces the three points the site’s catalogue files under this region, surveys the everyday complaints tradition brought to hand points, and sets out self-care adapted to small, intricate structures. It is educational material, not medical advice, and it teaches no diagnostic or therapeutic technique. One note on wording: phrases such as “traditionally used for” or “recorded indications” report what historical practice reached for — they describe an inheritance, not proven outcomes.

## Six Channels Meet at the Fingertips {#six-channels}

Three of the twelve principal channels climb each arm along its palm side — lung, pericardium, and heart — and three descend it on the back of the forearm: large intestine, san jiao, and small intestine. At the hand their stories converge. Each of the six arrives at, or departs from, a fingertip, and classical teaching stations a jing-well point at that outermost stop, where the channel’s flow is imagined barely to have begun or just to have finished. The fingers alone therefore gather six of these wells, the shallowest springs in the whole system. Palm side and back side face each other like two riverbanks, yin and yang running parallel within a few centimetres of skin.

Convergence made the hand useful in theory as well as in story. Channels were conceived in exterior–interior pairs, and several of those pairs complete their connection across the hand itself. Distance mattered too: because a channel was understood as one continuous line, tradition held that a point far from a complaint could still speak to it — a principle old verses applied boldly, pairing points on the hand with troubles of the head and face. That logic, whatever one makes of it medically, is why the registry’s summary describes this small region as hosting accessible command points for head, face, and systemic concerns rather than purely local ones.

## Three Points the Catalogue Files Here {#notable-points}

Hegu (LI4) is the region’s most celebrated resident. It sits on the back of the hand in the muscular web between thumb and index finger, rising into view as a small mound when the two digits press together — a setting its name honours, since he is conventionally glossed “joining” and gu “valley”. The large intestine channel owns the point and grants it the yuan-source title, read classically as a window onto the organ’s vitality. Its recorded indications lean heavily toward the head and face, headache and facial pain among them, which made hegu a standing example of the teaching that a point may reach complaints far from its own doorstep. The dataset also records a firm pregnancy caution at this point; the detail belongs to hegu’s own page, and pregnant readers are asked to leave the point unpressed.

Shenmen (HT7) keeps a quieter address. Turn a palm upward and find the crease at the wrist; on its little-finger side, snug against the cord of the flexor tendon, lies a small hollow — the heart channel’s gate. The name says exactly that: shen meaning spirit, men meaning gate. Tradition granted the point two titles, yuan-source and shu-stream, and reserved it for matters the classical heart governed beyond moving blood: sleep that would not come, a mind that would not quiet, unease with nowhere to settle. Of the three points gathered here, its recorded indications sit furthest from the hand itself — precisely what the far-reaching logic of the previous section predicts.

Houxi (SI3) completes the trio on the hand’s outer edge. Make a loose fist and a short transverse crease appears on the little-finger side just behind the knuckle; the point waits at the inner end of that crease, in the depression before the head of the fifth metacarpal bone. The small intestine channel claims it, and the record lists two roles for it: shu-stream of its channel, and one of the eight confluent points — a select group tradition connected with the extraordinary vessels, this one classically associated with the governing vessel that follows the spine. Suitably, its recorded indications include stiff neck and upper-back tension alongside headache: complaints of the body’s rear midline, addressed from the hand’s far margin.

## Complaints Tradition Brought to the Hand {#everyday-complaints}

Read the three records together and a pattern emerges. Heads ache and faces throb; hegu waits in the register. Necks stiffen over desks and long drives; houxi answers from across the wrist. Evenings refuse to settle; shenmen opens its gate. Around these headline entries cluster smaller ones — local wrist soreness, fatigue in the hand itself — the ordinary wear of a body part that grips, types, carries, and points all day. Traditional practice treated the hand accordingly: less as a destination for treatment than as a switchboard from which distant rooms could be reached.

What, then, should a modern reader expect from pressing these old addresses? At minimum, a pause: a minute of seated attention, slower breathing, hands doing something kind instead of scrolling. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and nothing in them elevates a fingertip into a therapy. Treat any ease that follows as a pleasant by-product of slowing down, never as treatment. A headache that changes character, a wrist that swells without injury, or a mind that will not settle night after night deserves proper assessment first; self-care accompanies good care, it does not replace it.

## Hands Working on Hands {#self-care}

The hand rewards a lighter toolkit than most regions do. Bones and tendons lie just under thin skin, joints sit proud of their surroundings, and nerve endings crowd everywhere — so the instrument of choice is a fingertip pad or a relaxed thumb moving slowly, rather than a firm kneading grip. Begin with travel rather than arrival: stroke lightly along the palm from the middle of the wrist crease toward the base of the fingers, then turn the hand over and repeat the pass on its back. Two unhurried sweeps wake the territory without pressing anything in particular, and they suit even readers who never learn a point name.

Meeting the residents takes a little more intention. For hegu (LI4), squeeze thumb and index finger together and let the opposite thumb settle onto the mound that rises between them, holding with only comfortable pressure while several breaths pass. For shenmen (HT7), rest a fingertip in the hollow beside the wrist tendon and stay feather-light — this address tolerates attention better than force. For houxi (SI3), curl the hand into a loose fist, find the crease-end behind the little-finger knuckle, and press gently into the soft spot there. Release gradually everywhere; the rhythm matters more than the strength.

Boundaries matter more here than almost anywhere. Skip any skin that is cut, inflamed, flaring, or numbed, hangnails included. Do not grind across knuckle joints or nail beds, where bone waits directly beneath a thin cover of skin. The hollows of the wrist hold arteries and nerves close under the surface, so those addresses take feather-light touches only — settling, never digging. Pregnancy removes hegu (LI4) from the menu entirely, on both hands, and anyone taking medication that affects bleeding should ask a qualified practitioner before adding point work at all. And as always, sharp, electric, or radiating sensation is a signal to stop at once.

## Boundaries and When to Seek Help {#when-to-seek-care}

Some signals belong promptly with a clinician, full stop. Seek assessment without waiting on self-care for sudden weakness, clumsiness, or numbness in a hand; a hand that discolours or swells without injury; deformity or severe pain after a fall; spreading redness together with fever; or tingling that wakes you night after night. Hands are intricate instruments, and these signs concern the wiring and plumbing beneath the skin — territory no amount of patient pressing should pretend to manage.

Between emergencies and everyday comfort stretches the familiar middle ground of ongoing trouble: wrists stiffened by arthritis, hands aching from repetitive work, sleep repeatedly disturbed by a busy mind. Licensed practitioners, whether conventionally trained doctors or East Asian medicine clinicians, can examine such patterns properly and fold gentle point work into wider care where appropriate. When you want detail on individual points, the catalogue pages for LI4, HT7, and SI3 carry precise locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions this overview deliberately leaves to them. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.
