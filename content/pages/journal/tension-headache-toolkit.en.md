Tight bands across the forehead, a dull squeeze at the temples, heaviness pooling at the base of the skull — ordinary head tension knows how to claim an afternoon. This toolkit collects the four points traditional practice reached for most often, a five-minute routine for ordinary days, and the honest limits of finger-pressure self-care.

## The Question You Brought With You {#the-question-first}

Can pressing a few spots on the head and hands genuinely settle a tension headache? Here is the honest answer before anything else: nobody can promise that, and anyone who does deserves your scepticism. What finger pressure offers is humbler — a few quiet minutes of comfortable touch, attention paid to a body that has spent hours at a desk, and a habit many people find steadying on difficult days. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and findings for headache remain unsettled. We think honesty is the best foundation a practice can stand on, so this article stands on it.

What this toolkit can offer more reliably than relief is a ritual: four documented points, a short sequence you can memorise, clear rules for stopping, and a realistic sense of what the tradition claimed. Every point below already has a full page on this site — hegu (LI4), fengchi (GB20), baihui (GV20), and taiyang (EX-HN5) — so whenever a sketch here leaves you curious, the deeper reference sits one click away.

A note on voice first. This is a journal piece rather than a clinical page, so we speak plainly and occasionally say “we”. The rules underneath, though, are the ones governing every reference page here: traditional-use framing throughout, promises attached to nothing, and the safety lines drawn before any technique is described.

## Knowing the Shape of an Ordinary Tension Headache {#know-the-shape}

Most readers can picture this headache without a diagram: a strap of dull pressure drawn evenly around the head, tightening the temples, settling across the forehead, or pooling where the skull meets the neck. It rarely throbs; it leans. Long reading sessions, screens, driving, deadlines running late, meals skipped at the desk, a jaw quietly clenched since breakfast — the triggers repeat themselves in every office and household, and the discomfort commonly brings stiff shoulders along as company.

Naming the picture is not diagnosing it. Headache has many faces, sorting one kind from another belongs to a clinician, and nothing in a self-care article should pretend otherwise. The picture earns its place by being practical: it explains why the points gathered below sit where they sit — at the temples, at the rim of the skull, at the crown, and one further off on the hand — and why nearly every culture that left us healing records left small rituals for easing the head by hand alongside them.

## Meet the Four Points {#meet-four-points}

Hegu (LI4) — the name pictures a joining valley — rests in the firm mound of flesh between thumb and index finger on the back of the hand. Press thumb-tip to index-tip and the mound rises; its crest, level with the middle of the index-finger bone, is the neighbourhood. Classical teaching ranked it the source point of the large intestine channel and reached for it across complaints of the head and face; old charts trace that channel from the hand up the forearm and arm, over the shoulder, and onto the cheek and face, which is how a hand point came to serve the head. One caution travels with it everywhere: traditionally avoided during pregnancy.

Fengchi (GB20) — wind pool — waits in the two hollows where the skull meets the neck, just outside the thick cords of muscle that run down beside the spine. Tilt the head slightly forward, slide the thumbs up the back of the neck until they meet the rounded bump at the centre of the skull’s base, step outward, and let the pads sink into the soft grooves either side of the midline. Tradition kept this address busy whenever the head, neck, or eyes felt strained; even the name imagines a pool where wind was thought to gather at the neck’s open doorway.

Baihui (GV20) — hundred meetings — crowns the top of the head where the front-to-back centre seam crosses a line drawn between the tips of the ears. Fold each ear gently forward to find its tip, rest a fingertip on each, and let them travel up and inward until they meet; the crossing sits about five thumb-widths back along the centreline from the front hairline. The du vessel climbs the body’s midline and crosses the summit here, classical texts describe channels converging at the crown, and tradition gave the spot a lifting, settling character.

Taiyang (EX-HN5) — great yang, the sun — sits in the soft hollow of the temple, about one finger-width behind the midpoint between the outer end of the eyebrow and the outer corner of the eye. It is an extra point, standing outside the twelve regular meridians with no channel address, recognised through fixed place and settled use alone. Few locations are easier to find, few gestures are older than resting fingertips there, and the record reaches for it around headache, tired eyes, and facial tension.

## Ground Rules Before Any Pressing Begins {#ground-rules}

Set the scene first, because conditions matter more than technique. Choose a chair that lets you sit upright, drop the shoulders, unclench the jaw, and warm cold hands before they go near a face — chilled fingers make tissue guard. Trim nails so pads meet skin, slip off rings that catch, and give the session the dignity of a pause rather than the status of a chore done alongside something else. Slow nasal breathing belongs to the method, not merely the mood: each exhale is the cue to soften one notch further.

Skin draws the outer boundary: nothing presses on broken, inflamed, numbed, or freshly scarred skin, and nothing presses during a feverish illness. Sensation draws the inner one. Comfortable dullness, mild warmth, a spreading achiness — welcome. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation — never. Stop means stop for the day, not stop until it stops hurting, and bruising afterwards is simply a report that force exceeded welcome; answer it with lighter hands next time, or with a day off.

Two groups edit this routine before starting it. Pregnant readers leave hegu (LI4) out entirely — the caution recorded for that point governs every kind of stimulation, however gentle — and treat the remaining work as something to raise with a qualified practitioner first. Readers living with chronic conditions, taking medication that affects bleeding, recovering from recent surgery or a head injury, or caring for young children do the same. Asking first is not timidity; it is what the tradition’s own culture of caution recommends.

## The Five-Minute Round, Step by Step {#the-five-minute-round}

The whole round asks for about five unhurried minutes and no equipment. Work seated, breathe slowly throughout, and give each point thirty to sixty seconds of contact — long enough for sensation to arrive and settle, short enough that fatigue never enters the picture. Once or twice a day is plenty; some people suit the morning, others the late-afternoon slump, and doubling up adds no virtue.

Step one begins away from the head: settle the pad of your opposite thumb into the hegu mound, let pressure grow across a few breaths until it registers as pleasant ache, hold steady for thirty to sixty seconds, then release gradually. Treat both hands alike if both wish a turn. Unless you are pregnant — in that case skip this step altogether and carry on.

Step two moves to the rim of the skull: rest both thumb pads in the fengchi hollows and hold a steady pressure aimed inward toward the neck rather than up beneath the bone, more patient than firm, for thirty to sixty seconds while the jaw stays loose.

Step three turns to the temples: lay the pads of index and middle fingers on the taiyang hollows and draw small, slow circles — the lightest touch of the whole round, light enough that the skin glides rather than drags. Thirty to sixty seconds per side, hands swapped, so the temples receive equal care.

Step four climbs to the summit: rest two fingertips, or all eight arranged around the crown, softly on baihui for a final hold of thirty to sixty seconds. Then roll the shoulders, take three slow breaths, and rejoin the day. Treat the order — a distant point first, local points after, crown last — as a habit rather than a law: traditional teaching regrouped these addresses freely according to where the discomfort sat, and when five minutes refuse to fit, a single unhurried point still closes the round honestly.

## What to Expect, Honestly {#what-to-expect-honestly}

Keep expectations level with the evidence and the habit tends to reward them. Finger pressure is a comfort practice — kin to stretching, warm showers, and quiet walks — not a treatment aimed at disease. Many people notice local warmth and softness under the fingers, a jaw that unclenches mid-hold, shoulders riding a notch lower, sometimes drowsiness afterwards. Those experiences are real, gentle, variable, and impossible to promise in advance.

Regularity outranks intensity here, as in most self-care. Five unhurried minutes on most days beat one heroic session followed by a silent week, and anchoring the round to something already in the day — the kettle boiling, a laptop closed for lunch — keeps it alive. Pair it with the ordinary friction-removers: water within reach, meals at roughly regular hours, eyes given distance from their tasks, lights low before bed. Tradition never expected the points to carry the whole weight, and neither should a busy week.

Hold the boundary bright while you enjoy the ritual: nothing here diagnoses, treats, or replaces licensed care. If the routine helps you feel steadier while you wait for an appointment, or helps an evening loosen after a hard day, it has done its job. If a symptom frightens you, follow that instinct and book the appointment.

## When to Put the Toolkit Down {#when-to-put-it-down}

Some headaches announce something that needs assessing promptly, and every kind of self-care waits outside that room. Seek medical attention without delay if a headache arrives sudden and severe, unlike any before it; follows a blow to the head; or comes with fever and a stiff neck. The same urgency applies when new weakness, numbness, slurred speech, confusion, or disturbed vision joins a headache, when one starts waking you from sleep or keeps changing its habits, or when a first headache appears during pregnancy. Urgency speaks its own language — leave the interpreting to a professional.

Between emergencies and everyday tension lies the wider country of headaches that come often — several days most weeks, or a pattern steadily deepening — and those deserve proper assessment too, from a licensed practitioner who can examine, explain, and plan. Registered acupuncturists and traditional medicine clinicians can join that conversation alongside conventional care rather than instead of it. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.
