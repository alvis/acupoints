Acupressure is the quiet art of pressing classical points with your fingers instead of needles. This guide covers what the practice is, where it comes from, how to find and press points gently, a three-point starter routine, and the safety lines every beginner should know.

## What Acupressure Is {#what-acupressure-is}

Acupressure is finger pressure applied to specific spots on the body — the same classical points that East Asian medicine has mapped for centuries — held steadily enough to invite a change in how the area feels. Where acupuncture reaches those points with fine needles, acupressure uses only hands: a thumb, a fingertip, a knuckle, sometimes the palm. Nothing pierces the skin, nothing is swallowed, and nothing needs to be bought. That simplicity is the practice’s great advantage for beginners: your hands are the entire toolkit.

In traditional framing, the body carries channels called meridians along which vital energy — qi — is said to move, and the points sit along these channels like waystations. Pressing a point is understood as a way of nudging the flow of qi and blood through its neighbourhood. You do not need to adopt any particular philosophy to try the practice; you can treat the points simply as places where people have long found interesting sensations and, often, welcome ease.

It helps to hold two ideas at once. First, acupressure belongs to a long craft tradition with its own maps, vocabulary, and habits of attention — treating it casually misses most of its character. Second, it is self-care, not medicine: a way of slowing down, noticing your body, and offering comfort, not a substitute for professional care when something is genuinely wrong.

## Where the Practice Comes From {#where-it-comes-from}

The points used in acupressure come from the classical healing traditions of East Asia — most prominently the Chinese tradition, with close relatives in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Over many centuries, practitioners observed which places on the body responded to touch and treatment, recorded them, and organised them into systems of channels. Classical texts describe hundreds of named points, each with a name, a location, and a traditional role; modern standardisation efforts, including the World Health Organization’s work on standard point locations in the Western Pacific Region, later gave the same points consistent international names.

Acupressure itself grew up alongside — not instead of — the needle arts. Teachers of self-care passed down simple routines that households could use for everyday comfort: a parent calming a child’s unsettled stomach, a scholar rubbing a stiff neck after long hours of study, travellers pressing a wrist point against queasiness on a long journey. Because the practice needs no equipment, it travelled well, and versions of it are now taught in wellness settings worldwide.

This site’s reference material draws on two kinds of sources: formal standards and manuals of classical points, and an open dataset of recorded point indications. We keep the framing traditional rather than clinical — describing what the practice holds and teaches, not making claims about outcomes.

## Safety First: The Lines Never to Cross {#safety-first-principles}

Acupressure is gentle by design, and its few rules matter more than its techniques. Never press on broken skin — cuts, burns, rashes, infections, bruises, sunburn, or recent scars. Skip areas with swelling you cannot explain, varicose veins, or anywhere a clinician is already treating a specific problem. Pressure belongs on healthy tissue only; the skin under your thumb should look and feel the same when you finish as when you began.

Stop immediately if anything feels sharply painful, electric, numb, or simply wrong. A well-placed press produces dullness, achiness, warmth, or a spreading heaviness — often described in the tradition as a satisfying sort of soreness — never shooting pain. Sensation that makes you instinctively flinch is information: ease off, shift position slightly, or leave that point for another day. Bruising afterwards means the pressure was too firm or held too long.

Some situations call for professional guidance before any self-care routine. If you are pregnant or trying to conceive, ask a qualified practitioner first — several well-known points, LI4 among them, are traditionally avoided during pregnancy. The same applies if you live with a chronic condition, take blood-thinning medication, have recently had surgery, or care for a young child. And whenever a symptom is persistent, severe, or worsening, the right next step is a licensed clinician, not a firmer thumb. Acupressure sits alongside good care, never in front of it.

## How to Find a Point {#finding-a-point}

Classical point locations sound precise on paper — “two finger-widths above the wrist crease” — but bodies vary, and the tradition itself expects you to search. The standard method is palpation: measure roughly to the described area, then explore with a fingertip, small circles, moderate pressure, and patience. Most classical points announce themselves. People commonly report a tender spot, a dull ache that feels oddly distinct from the surrounding tissue, a sense of warmth, or a heavy, drawing sensation that seems to spread slightly outward.

Use your own hand as a ruler. In Chinese teaching, distances are measured in units called cun — proportional body measurements, not fixed inches. One cun is roughly the width of your own thumb at the knuckle; four fingers held close together span approximately three cun. Because the units scale to your body, measuring with your own hands automatically adjusts for size.

When you think you have found the spot, press gently and compare sides. Points exist symmetrically, and beginners often find one side noticeably more responsive than the other. Trust responsiveness over geometry: a location that produces clear sensation half a centimetre from the textbook description is, for practice purposes, the point. Precision matters less than consistency — returning to roughly the same place, in roughly the same way, each time.

## How to Press: Technique, Breath, and Timing {#how-to-press}

Begin with less force than feels useful. Set your thumb or fingertip on the point and let the pressure grow slowly over several seconds until you feel that characteristic dull ache — meaningful contact, but nowhere near pain. Then hold steady. Good technique favours patient, sustained pressure over rubbing; if you prefer movement, slow deep circles work, but keep the pace unhurried. Let the sensation develop rather than muscling your way to it.

Breathe the whole time — slowly, through the nose — and let each exhale soften your shoulders and jaw a little further. In traditional teaching, attention is part of the technique: following the breath while holding a point turns a mechanical squeeze into something closer to sitting quietly, and most people find the minutes pass far more pleasantly that way.

For timing, plain ranges serve better than exact prescriptions. Hold a point for about thirty seconds to two minutes when exploring; for a settled daily routine, one to three minutes per point is a common habit. Once a day is plenty for beginners; twice is fine if it fits naturally into morning and evening. Release gradually rather than abruptly, rest a moment, and notice how the area feels — softer, warmer, quieter — before moving on.

Finger strength varies. If your thumbs tire, lean instead of squeezing — let a supported posture or gravity supply the force — or substitute a relaxed fist, a knuckle, or a pen capped with a rounded end. Tools are perfectly acceptable; sharp edges and hard metal tips are not.

## A Starter Routine: Three Well-Known Points {#starter-routine}

Three of the most famous points in the tradition make an ideal first routine. All three appear throughout this site’s catalogue with fuller detail; the sketches below are enough to begin. Work through them seated or lying down, in whichever order suits you, breathing slowly throughout.

LI4 — Hegu, “Joining Valley,” sits in the fleshy web between thumb and index finger. Rest the flat of your other thumb in the webbing and slide toward the place where the bones of the two fingers meet; the point lies in the muscle just off that line. It is traditionally used for complaints of the head and face — headaches, toothaches, facial tension — and for general stress relief, and it may be the tradition’s best-known single point. Press the more responsive side, or both, for one to two minutes each. Remember: traditionally avoided during pregnancy.

PC6 — Neiguan, “Inner Pass,” sits on the inner forearm, about two thumb-widths above the wrist crease, between the two central tendons. Turn your palm up, measure upward from the crease, and settle your thumb into the groove between the tendons. Classical practice reaches for this point to settle the stomach — queasiness, motion sickness, an unsettled belly — and to ease feelings of tightness in the chest. Two to three minutes of steady pressure per side is a common home approach.

ST36 — Zusanli, “Leg Three Miles,” sits below the outer side of the kneecap, about four finger-widths down the shin, in the muscle just outside the shinbone. Find it by feeling for the tender, dense spot that responds when you flex your foot. Its name is said to come from the old idea that pressing it let a weary traveller walk three more miles: the tradition treats it as a point of stamina, digestion, and general vitality, and some call it the point of longevity. One to three minutes per side rounds out the routine.

## What to Expect — and What Not To {#realistic-expectations}

Set honest expectations and the practice tends to reward them. Acupressure is a wellness habit, not a treatment: think of it alongside stretching, warm baths, and quiet walks — a way of tending to yourself, not a therapy aimed at disease. Many people describe pleasant effects after a session: local warmth and softness, easier breathing, a calmer mind, sometimes drowsiness afterwards. These experiences are real and worth having; they are also gentle, variable, and difficult to predict from person to person.

Where effects arrive at all, they usually come modestly at first — a headache loosening somewhat rather than vanishing, queasiness settling rather than disappearing outright. The tradition generally values regularity over intensity: five unhurried minutes on most days outperforms an occasional heroic session. Treat each session as time spent paying attention to your body, and treat any benefit as a bonus rather than a promise.

Keep the boundary bright: nothing in this guide diagnoses, treats, or replaces licensed medical care. Persistent pain, recurring symptoms, injuries, pregnancy questions, struggles with mood — these belong with professionals. If acupressure helps you feel steadier while you wait for an appointment or unwind after a hard week, it has done its job. If a symptom worries you, let the worry win and see a clinician.
