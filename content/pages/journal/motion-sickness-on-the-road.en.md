Motion sickness has a famous address on the classical map: neiguan (PC6), on the inner forearm between two tendons. This post gives an honest answer to whether wrist pressure helps, a step-by-step routine to follow on a coach or a ferry, zusanli (ST36) as a companion point, and the safety lines worth keeping.

## Does Pressing Your Wrist Really Help? {#does-wrist-pressure-help}

Ask anyone who owns a motion-sickness wristband which point it presses, and most will guess wrong; ask whether it works, and the answers split neatly down the middle. The spot beneath the strap is real enough — neiguan (PC6), the single most famous address on the whole classical map for queasy journeys — and the question deserves a straighter answer than advertising usually supplies. Ours runs thus: finger pressure at neiguan is a gentle, traditional comfort practice that many travellers would not sail without, that some people feel within minutes, and that leaves others entirely untouched. It asks for light fingers and low expectations, promises neither of them anything, and costs nothing to find out where you personally stand.

Fairness means keeping two registers apart. Classical teaching paired this point with unsettled stomachs across many centuries, and households passed the habit down long before pharmacy counters stocked elastic bands. Modern evidence speaks another language altogether. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and they leave most specific uses without a settled verdict. Neither statement cancels the other. A practice can be honourably old and honestly unproven at the same time, and this post assumes exactly that.

So our suggestion is the one seasoned travellers tend to reach on their own: rehearse on dry land. Try the whole routine on a short local errand — a twenty-minute city bus ride, not a nine-hour crossing — and watch what actually happens in your own middle. Whatever you learn there is yours to keep. What follows is the full kit: how to find the point, how to press it kindly, where its companion below the knee joins in, how the day fits together, and the boundaries that keep all of it safe.

## Why Journeys Upset the Stomach {#why-journeys-upset-the-stomach}

Sufferers know the geography by heart: the back bench of a car climbing switchbacks, the sheltered lower deck of a slow ferry, a coach leaning through hairpin bends. The body sends early warnings well before any crisis — the stomach drifts loose in the middle, the skin goes cold and tacky, saliva gathers thickly, yawns arrive uninvited, and a blunt pressure parks itself behind the eyes. The usual account runs as follows: the eyes, the balance organs of the inner ear, and the body’s own motion sensors each file separate reports about the road, and while the accounts conflict, the stomach takes the dispute personally. When the journey ends and the reports realign, calm returns.

It refuses to behave uniformly. One friend crosses channels without a flicker while another wilts stepping onto a river launch; the same passenger devours a novel through an entire rail journey yet droops within minutes inside a car. Anticipation joins early, too — for some people the stomach sinks in the queue, before a single wheel has turned. None of this amounts to diagnosis, and this post offers none; the sketches above simply help you recognise your own version. One boundary deserves drawing straight away: what follows is a comfort practice for ordinary journeys, not a remedy for symptoms a clinician ought to be seeing — recurring or severe sickness receives its proper attention near the end of this post.

## Meeting Neiguan (PC6), the Traveller’s Point {#meeting-neiguan-pc6}

Neiguan translates roughly as the Inner Pass: nei names the interior, guan the gatehouse planted where a mountain trail pinches narrow and every walker must slow. The body keeps the address faithfully. Turn a palm upward and the point waits on the centre line of the forearm, two cun above the wrist crease — a cun being the tradition’s proportional unit, roughly the width of your own thumb — tucked into the shallow valley between two tendons that stand up when you make a loose fist and bend the wrist inward. Because cun scale to the person rather than to any ruler, the distance shifts from arm to arm; the tendons and the crease are the trustworthy landmarks, and the steps in the next section lean on them alone. Pinyin writes the name Nèiguān — nèi sinking through the fourth tone, guān carried high and steady on the first.

Why this particular gate for this particular trouble? The pericardium channel, in classical description, sets out from the chest, travels down the inner arm, and finishes at the tip of the middle finger, with nine stations punctuating the descent — neiguan holds the sixth post, about halfway along. Classical physiology expected stomach qi to travel downward, and read nausea as that current reversing course; points along this channel were traditionally reached for to quiet the ascent, and whenever a rocking deck or a pregnancy turned the stomach, the old casebooks came knocking at this gate. Its formal titles — connecting bridge of its own channel, and one of eight points classically said to open deep vessels of the interior — complete a portfolio built for travel days. The wristbands sold beside the sunscreen press precisely here.

## Finding It and Pressing It, Step by Step {#finding-and-pressing-neiguan}

Settle comfortably and turn one palm to the ceiling. Make a loose fist and bend the wrist gently inward: two taut cords stand up from the base of the palm, and the valley between them is the road you want. Now stack the index, middle, and ring fingers of the free hand and lay the bottom edge of the stack against the midpoint of the wrist crease; the point sits level with the top edge of the three fingers, dropped straight down into the groove between the cords. Before trusting the spot, audition it: lay the pad down with no force at all, lift it a finger’s width to either side, and compare. The true neighbourhood tends to murmur — a faint, spreading dullness — while its neighbours stay mute.

Pressing follows the same gentle grammar. Use the pad of a finger, never the tip or a knuckle; let pressure grow over several seconds until the contact feels weighted and present yet nowhere near painful; then simply stay, about a minute, keeping the breath slow and unhurried the while. Ease off gradually, grant the wrist a breather, and hand the work to the other side. On the move, return whenever the stomach mutters — every few minutes is a natural rhythm; better still, begin in the boarding queue rather than negotiating with the first bend of the road.

One anatomical footnote spares confusion: some people lack the second tendon altogether — the middle cord simply never appears, an ordinary human variation and no obstacle whatever. For them the search narrows to the soft strip just toward the little-finger side of the remaining cord, same height, same gentle rules. Whoever you are, the valley should receive a fingertip softly; if it pushes back, you are resting on tendon rather than sinking into the groove, and the smallest shift settles the matter.

## Below the Knee: Zusanli (ST36) {#zusanli-below-the-knee}

Tradition rarely sent a troubled stomach to a single address. Its second recommendation for travel days waits below the knee: zusanli (ST36), whose name joins li — the old unit of walking distance — to the number three. An old story credits the spot with restoring a flagging walker for the next three li of road; formal teaching ranks it as the pooling-ground of the stomach channel, the he-sea station where a channel’s current gathers deepest, and treats it as a keeper of digestion, stamina, and general resilience. Company worth having aboard, by the tradition’s ledger, even before anyone feels queasy. Pinyin renders it Zúsānlǐ: zú rising on the second tone, sān high and flat on the first, lǐ dipping through the third.

Finding it starts at the knee. Bend the leg slightly and look for the small hollow just outside the firm band that runs from the lower edge of the kneecap toward the shin — an address the tradition labelled the outer knee-eye. From that hollow, descend the shin along its front-outside border for the span of four fingers held snugly together, which approximates the three cun the record asks for; then check sideways, stepping one finger-breadth outward from the sharp ridge of the shinbone onto the muscle. Flex the foot back toward you: the muscle beneath should bunch and harden, then melt away on release — working muscle answers, bare bone never does. A gentle minute per side is ample. Travel-day moments that suit it include the morning of departure, a long stopover with room to sit, and the wind-down after arriving.

## A Travel-Day Routine, from Packing to Arrival {#travel-day-routine}

The night before, the routine is mostly restraint: a plain, familiar supper rather than a rich or greasy experiment; an early night; bags gathered by the door. On the morning, eat something light, take water in sips rather than gulps, and grant yourself five unhurried minutes — two of them at neiguan, one per wrist, breathing slowly — so the day starts settled instead of sprinted. If the booking allows a choice, take the seat facing forward with a clear view of the road or horizon; generations of sufferers count it the kindest seat on any vehicle.

On the move, habits stay small and repeated: eyes resting on the horizon rather than a page or screen; a window opened a crack for moving air where the vehicle allows; a stand-and-stretch whenever a long crossing offers the chance. Diesel fumes, heavy perfume, a lunch box gone warm in the sun — such smells deserve a wide margin. Whenever the stomach mutters, spend an easy minute back at neiguan; on a long stopover with room to sit, add one calm round at zusanli on each leg.

Arrival belongs to the practice too. Once the ground stops moving beneath your feet, many people enjoy ten or so quiet minutes repeating the pair — forearms, then knees, unhurried throughout — partly as a wind-down and partly as reconnaissance: noticing which stretch of the day the routine seemed to help teaches next trip’s timing better than any advice could. Keep whatever proved pleasant; retire quietly whatever did not. The routine becomes yours after the first journey, not before it.

## What to Expect — and What Not To {#honest-expectations}

Set the dial to modest and the practice rarely disappoints. File acupressure beside stretching and a slow evening walk: ways of tending yourself rather than treatments aimed at disease. When effects come, they usually arrive quietly — the stomach settling rather than celebrating, a crossing passed tolerably rather than triumphantly — and they vary from person to person and from trip to trip. Judge the routine over several journeys, not one; a single rough crossing proves nothing in either direction.

Equally worth saying: sometimes nothing happens, and that outcome carries information rather than shame. Queasiness that ignores a well-found routine responds better to a changed seat, moving air, shorter stints between stops, or a frank word with a pharmacist than to firmer thumbs — escalating pressure is the one response the tradition never asked of anyone. And when sickness returns journey after journey, or arrives far from any road or boat, the pattern itself deserves professional eyes; self-care accompanies good assessment, never substitutes for it.

## Safety Lines Worth Keeping {#safety-lines}

Gentle as it is, the routine keeps a few hard edges. Pressure belongs on healthy, intact skin only — skip anywhere cut, burned, rashy, bruised, or freshly scarred, along with any swelling nobody can account for. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stopping at once, not pressing through; soreness afterwards means lighter fingers next time. Feverish days suspend the practice entirely. Anyone who is pregnant, managing a chronic condition, or taking regular medication does best to speak with a qualified practitioner before adding point work — and one boundary stands absolute: sanyinjiao (SP6), the ankle point some routines include, is recorded as contraindicated throughout pregnancy and stays out altogether.

Certain signals move sickness out of self-care’s jurisdiction entirely. When vomit will not settle and fluids refuse to stay down, a doctor should hear about it promptly; blood in the vomit, abdominal pain of real severity, a fever arriving alongside, dizziness that shakes your footing, or sickness following a bang on the head each changes the conversation. Chest pain needs no translator. In pregnancy, nausea climbing far past common morning queasiness calls for professional eyes, however stoic the old advice sounds. A pattern returning journey after journey is a message, and messages of that kind deserve trained readers. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.
