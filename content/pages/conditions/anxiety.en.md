Anxious feelings visit almost everyone. This page gathers what traditional East Asian practice reached for when the mind would not settle — shenmen (HT7) and neiguan (PC6), paired with slow breathing — and adds a gentle home routine, honest notes on habits and limits, and clear signposts towards professional care.

## How Anxious Feelings Show Up {#everyday-anxiety}

Almost everyone knows the texture of a nervous day: butterflies before a presentation, a heart that taps quickly in a queue, thoughts circling the same worry the way water finds the same groove. Mild anxious feelings sit inside ordinary life — before exams, interviews, difficult conversations, or arriving for no reason anyone can name. They come, they linger, they pass. Describing them is not the same as diagnosing anything, and this page never asks you to match your inner weather against a checklist or hands out labels.

What the feelings do to the body, though, is worth noticing, because that is where self-touch becomes relevant. Shoulders creep upward. Breathing turns shallow and quick, living mostly in the top of the chest. Hands stay busy or go cool. Nights stretch while the mind replays the day and previews the next one. Traditions of self-care, East Asian and otherwise, have long paid attention to this borderland where an unsettled mind lives in a restless body, and acupressure belongs to that family of quiet, hands-on habits.

## What Tradition Reached For {#traditional-reach}

Classical East Asian medicine organised the person as a landscape of channels and gave the heart a courtly position at the centre. Older writings describe the heart as the residence of the shen — a word gathering spirit, mind, and presence into one — and when the residence grew loud, the record speaks of a mind that would not settle, sleep that kept its distance, and a chest full of fluttering. Worry and fright were read through the heart’s household rather than treated as strangers to it, so the channels serving the heart drew practitioners’ attention first whenever an uneasy visitor arrived.

Two wrist-side points carry that inheritance into reach of your own hand. Shenmen (HT7) sits at the wrist crease on the heart channel, and its name tells the story plainly: shen means spirit and men means gate, a doorway through which the spirit was imagined to come and go. Classical lists rank it as the heart channel’s yuan-source point — the spot said to reflect the organ’s own vitality — and as its shu-stream point besides. Recorded indications cluster tightly around the theme: anxiety, insomnia, and a racing mind stand beside chest discomfort in the dataset this site draws on.

Neiguan (PC6) lies a little further along, on the pericardium channel, the line tradition drafted as the heart’s protective companion. Its characters sketch an inner pass: nei for the inward side, guan for a barrier or frontier post. The point holds two classical titles at once — luo-connecting point tying its channel to the san jiao partner, and one of the eight confluent points, traditionally paired with the yin wei vessel that sweeps the front of the trunk. Its recorded indications run wider, taking in queasiness, motion sickness, and palpitations, yet anxious feelings appear among them too, and home practice has long kept both points close whenever the heart felt crowded.

## A Gentle Self-Care Routine {#self-care-routine}

Begin with shenmen (HT7). Lay the hand palm-up in your lap and let the fingers soften. Run a fingertip along the palm side of the wrist towards the little-finger edge until a firm cord pushes back — flexor carpi ulnaris, though knowing its name changes nothing about finding it. The point waits in the small hollow immediately on the thumb side of that cord, level with the crease. Settle your fingertip into the hollow, let the pressure grow only as heavy as feels pleasant, and stay for four or five slow breaths before releasing gradually.

Move on to neiguan (PC6). Turn the same forearm over and lay three fingers of the opposite hand across the wrist, their lower edge touching the crease; the point sits about three finger-widths up from the crease — the classical measure calls it two cun — in the soft channel between the two sinews that rise when the fingers curl gently. Press with the pad of a thumb or fingertip, again only to comfortable depth, hold for four or five slow breaths, then swap wrists. A minute or two at each point, morning and evening or whenever the day offers a pause, is plenty; unhurried beats forceful every time.

Pair the pressure with breathing, as this site’s own introduction to the condition suggests. Let each exhale run a little longer than the inhale; count nothing, perform nothing, simply let the hands rest where they are. Stop rules keep the practice friendly: sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stopping at once; broken, inflamed, or numbed skin is left alone entirely; and pressure that has to be endured is pressure that has already gone too far. If you are pregnant, manage a health condition, or take regular medication, ask a qualified practitioner before making any of this a habit.

## Everyday Habits Around the Practice {#habits-context}

Points do not work alone, whatever the posters promise. The hours surrounding a practice shape it: steady waking times, some daylight and movement, meals arriving on something like a schedule, and gentler caffeine habits late in the day are the ordinary scaffolding that nearly every sensible discussion of nervous energy mentions. None of it is exotic, none of it is a promise, and none of it requires belief in any tradition — these are simply the conditions under which bodies, in most people’s experience, find settling easier.

Where does honest expectation land? Self-acupressure is best held as a ritual of attention: a few deliberate minutes in which hands, breath, and mind agree to slow down together. Some people find such pauses genuinely steadying through an ordinary nervous stretch; others notice little, which is perfectly normal and no fault of theirs. Treat the routine as a pleasant, inexpensive companion — something that keeps company with the morning coffee or the evening lamp — rather than a remedy, and let the fuller accounting of evidence wait for the questions below.

## When Feelings Outgrow Self-Care {#limits-and-red-flags}

Some signals move a complaint out of the self-care column altogether. Anxious feelings that visit most days for several weeks, dread that narrows a life down to a corner, sudden surges of panic with a hammering heart or gasping breath, leaning on alcohol to get through evenings, or any thought of harming yourself — each deserves a prompt conversation with a doctor or another qualified professional, not a longer session on the wrists. Physical companions that can mimic anxiety belong in the same conversation: unexplained weight change, trembling, unusual sensitivity to heat, or a pulse that races without obvious cause.

Between emergencies and everyday unease lies the wide ground where professional help earns its name. Talking therapies, prescribed treatment, and East Asian medicine each approach persistent worry from their own direction, and they combine far better than they compete; nobody should set aside prescribed treatment because a self-care page looked encouraging. If you choose to involve a registered acupuncturist or traditional medicine practitioner, expect them to work alongside your usual doctors. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

## Who This Page Can Help {#who-this-helps}

This overview suits the curious newcomer who wants a calm, bounded introduction before opening individual point pages; the wind-down builder assembling a small evening ritual of breath and touch; and the person already receiving professional care who wants a complementary comfort practice to use between appointments. It also suits the sceptic, because everything asserted here is either recorded in the site’s catalogue or attributed to general classical framing — and the page declines every invitation to promise relief.

It cannot help everyone, and saying so plainly is part of helping. Anyone whose symptoms are severe, worsening, or tangled up with thoughts of self-harm needs professional support first; acupressure is a companion to care, never a substitute for it. When you want more detail, the catalogue pages for shenmen (HT7) and neiguan (PC6) hold exact locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions, together with answers to the questions readers ask most often.
