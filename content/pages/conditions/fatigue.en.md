Everyday tiredness is the low, heavy feeling of running on empty. This page gathers five points Chinese medicine traditionally used for fatigue of the depleted kind — zusanli (ST36), sanyinjiao (SP6), qihai (CV6), guanyuan (CV4) and hegu (LI4) — with a gentle routine, honest evidence and clear warning signs.

## How Everyday Tiredness Shows Up {#everyday-picture}

Most people know this kind of tiredness from the inside. It is not the pleasant heaviness after a long walk but a flatness that lingers: waking up unrefreshed, an afternoon slump that arrives on schedule, limbs that feel heavier than they should, a voice that has gone quiet and a mind that reads the same line twice. Small tasks seem to cost more than they used to, and the urge to sit down outruns any reason for it.

Ordinary life supplies most of the causes. Short or broken sleep, long hours at a screen, meals skipped or eaten on the move, too little daylight and movement, worry that never quite switches off, and the slow drain of caring for others all wear people down. Recovery after a cold or a hard season can leave a long tail of low energy too. None of this is a diagnosis, and this page offers none; tiredness has many sources, and some of them need a doctor rather than a routine.

There is also a narrower kind of fatigue that sits at the centre of the research on this page: the tiredness that follows cancer treatment, and the drained, washed-out feeling many people describe after haemodialysis. Both are heavier and more stubborn than ordinary tiredness and belong first with the clinical teams involved. The page keeps the two pictures apart, because what helps one is not automatically proven for the other.

## What Chinese Medicine Reaches For {#traditional-reach}

Chinese medicine reads this low, heavy tiredness mostly as a pattern of deficiency, and above all as qi deficiency (氣虛): shortness of breath, a soft voice and little wish to talk, tiring quickly on effort, a pale face and easy sweating. The spleen and stomach (脾胃) sit near the centre of that reading. In the old model they turn food into the qi and blood that keep the body going, so a weak spleen and stomach — poor appetite, loose stools, a heavy body after meals — is one of the commonest routes to lasting fatigue.

The Chinese guideline on cancer-related fatigue files that condition under xulao, deficiency exhaustion, a category that goes back to the Han-dynasty classic Jingui Yaolue, and sorts it into patterns such as qi and blood both deficient, qi and yin both deficient, spleen and kidney yang deficient, and lung and spleen qi deficient. Its treatment principles keep returning to the same few verbs: boost qi, strengthen the spleen, support what is depleted. Everyday tiredness is far milder, but the tradition reaches for the same family of points.

Five of them stand out. Zusanli (ST36) on the shin is recorded in this site’s catalogue for deficiency fatigue and described as a principal point for strengthening and health preservation. Qihai (CV6), the sea of qi, carries weakness and emaciation from qi deficiency among its indications, and guanyuan (CV4) lists deficiency fatigue with cold exhaustion. Sanyinjiao (SP6) is where the spleen, liver and kidney channels meet, and hegu (LI4) is the source point of the large intestine channel, which pairs with the stomach channel in the yangming system. In this site’s language, each was traditionally used in this territory — a report of what practice reached for, not a promise of results.

## Finding the Five Points {#meeting-the-points}

Zusanli (ST36) — often glossed as leg three miles, after a popular story that pressing it let a tired walker go three more li — sits on the outer front of the lower leg. Find the hollow just below the kneecap on its outer side, measure four finger-widths straight down, then move one finger-width out from the sharp front edge of the shin bone. The standard description puts it three cun below that hollow. It is the he-sea point of the stomach channel.

Sanyinjiao (SP6) — three yin meeting — lies on the inner leg. Lay four fingers across the leg above the tip of the inner anklebone; the point sits at the top edge of that span, just behind the back border of the shin bone, three cun above the ankle. It is often tender, so arrive slowly. It is traditionally avoided during pregnancy.

Qihai (CV6) and guanyuan (CV4) both lie on the midline of the lower belly. Qihai, sea of qi, is about two finger-widths (one and a half cun) below the centre of the navel; guanyuan, gate of the origin, is about four finger-widths (three cun) below it. Both belong to the conception vessel, which old charts ran up the front midline of the body. Neither is pressed hard: a warm palm resting over the area does the work here. Both are traditionally avoided during pregnancy.

Hegu (LI4) — joining valley — is on the back of the hand. Close the thumb against the side of the index finger and a fleshy mound rises; the point sits at its highest spot, beside the middle of the second metacarpal bone. Tradition makes it the source point of its channel, and it too is traditionally avoided during pregnancy.

## A Gentle Routine for Low-Energy Days {#gentle-routine}

Choose a time when you can sit for about eight minutes, ideally in the morning or early afternoon rather than just before bed. Sit upright in a supported chair with both feet flat, empty the bladder first, and leave an hour or so after a large meal before working on the belly. Rub the hands together until they are warm; cold fingers make everything feel sharper than it is.

Start with zusanli (ST36): a thumb pad on the point, pressure firm enough to feel a dull, spreading ache but never sharp, held for about a minute on each leg while the breath stays slow. Move to sanyinjiao (SP6) with lighter pressure, half a minute to a minute a side, easing in because it is often tender. Then rest a warm palm on qihai (CV6) below the navel and make slow, small circles for about a minute, before sliding the palm down to guanyuan (CV4) for another minute of the same. Finish with hegu (LI4), a thumb pressing towards the bone of the index finger, half a minute on each hand.

The stop rules matter more than the order. Stop at once on any sharp, electric or radiating sensation; skip broken, bruised or inflamed skin; keep belly work to the gentle weight of a palm. During pregnancy press only zusanli (ST36) and leave the other four alone. If you bruise easily, take blood-thinning medication, or are in the middle of cancer treatment, ask your doctor first and keep the pressure light.

## What the Evidence Says {#what-the-evidence-says}

The Chinese source behind this page is a China Association of Chinese Medicine guideline on cancer-related fatigue, issued in 2023 as standard T/CACM 1522-2023 and published in Chinese General Practice in 2025. It recommends external therapies, especially tuina massage of the head, back and limbs along the governing, bladder and yangming channels; it also recommends needling and moxibustion, and its point lists for those include zusanli, sanyinjiao, guanyuan, qihai and hegu. The same association’s 2026 nursing protocols list guanyuan, qihai and zusanli for fatigue in lung cancer and in type 2 diabetes, applied with moxibustion, warm compresses or herbal patches rather than fingers. In short, the Chinese guidance names these points for needles, moxa and massage; the hand-pressing routine here is the gentlest home version of that tradition.

The research, by contrast, pressed points by hand. A 2021 meta-analysis in Cancer Nursing by Hsieh and colleagues pooled fourteen randomised trials and found that acupressure eased general, physical and mental fatigue in people with cancer compared with control groups. A 2022 review in Taiwan’s Journal of Nursing by Chou and colleagues reached a similar conclusion and singled out hegu, zusanli and sanyinjiao, pressed once a day for one to three minutes each. The largest single trial, by Zick and colleagues in JAMA Oncology in 2016, taught breast cancer survivors with lingering fatigue to press points themselves every day for six weeks; both of its point sets did better than usual care, and the stimulating set included hegu, zusanli, sanyinjiao and qihai alongside baihui and taixi. The 2024 guideline from ASCO and the Society for Integrative Oncology concluded that acupressure may be recommended for fatigue once cancer treatment is finished.

Beyond cancer, a 2024 meta-analysis in the Journal of Nursing Research by Chang and colleagues gathered eight trials in people on haemodialysis and found that acupressure eased their fatigue, more so when the ear point Shenmen was included, and without any special equipment. Reviews suggest acupressure can ease fatigue after cancer treatment and in people on haemodialysis. The largest trial compared pressing with usual care rather than with a sham, and the studies enrolled people with cancer or on haemodialysis, not otherwise healthy people with ordinary tiredness. Treat it as a comfort practice borrowed from that research.

## When Tiredness Needs More Than Self-Care {#when-self-care-is-not-enough}

Tiredness is one of the commonest reasons people see a doctor, and sometimes it is the first sign of something that needs testing. See a doctor rather than pressing points if fatigue lasts more than a few weeks without a clear reason; if it comes with weight loss you cannot explain, fever or drenching night sweats; if you are breathless, or have chest pain or a racing heart on light effort; if you notice unusual bleeding or bruising, very heavy periods, or a pale face and gums that could mean anaemia; or if you snore loudly and someone has seen you stop breathing in your sleep. Chest pain or severe breathlessness needs urgent care.

Low mood often travels with exhaustion, and the two are easy to confuse. If you have lost interest in things you used to enjoy, feel hopeless, or have any thought of harming yourself, talk to a doctor or a crisis line today; that matters far more than any routine. If you are having cancer treatment, tell your oncology team about new or worsening fatigue before trying acupressure, since it can signal anaemia, infection or a treatment side effect, and ask whether pressure is safe with your blood counts. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

## Rest, Food and Rhythm {#habits-and-rhythm}

Traditional teaching never expected points to carry fatigue alone. The spleen and stomach view puts regular, warm, simple meals at the centre — eaten sitting down, not too late, not too much, and with little raw or icy food — because the old model saw digestion as the daily source of qi. It also warns that overwork and constant worry wear the spleen down, and that lying about all day drains qi as surely as overwork does.

Modern advice lands in a similar place. A regular wake-up time, daylight in the morning, a short walk on most days and steady meals with enough protein all support energy, and for cancer-related fatigue the ASCO guideline recommends exercise, cognitive behavioural therapy and mindfulness programmes, with acupressure in a supporting role once treatment is over. Gentle movement traditions such as baduanjin and tai chi appear in the Chinese nursing protocols as well. A few unhurried minutes of pressing points can anchor that rhythm: a pause that reminds you to eat, breathe and move, rather than a substitute for any of them.
