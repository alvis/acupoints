Nausea after an operation or during chemotherapy is common, and anti-sickness medicine always comes first. This page shows a gentle add-on that traditional practice reached for — neiguan (PC6) on the inner wrist, with hegu (LI4) and zusanli (ST36) — and the signs that mean you should call your team instead.

## How Nausea After Treatment Shows Up {#everyday-picture}

Feeling sick after treatment has a few familiar settings. Some people wake from an anaesthetic with a churning stomach, or find that the first sip of water or the first walk to the bathroom brings a wave of queasiness. Others meet it around chemotherapy: on the day of an infusion, in the days that follow, or — for some who have had a hard cycle before — on the drive to the clinic, before any drug has been given. The feeling may come with sweating, extra saliva, a sour taste, poor appetite, retching or vomiting, and it saps the energy people need for recovery, eating and getting back on their feet.

Medical teams take this seriously, and they plan for it. Anaesthetists often give anti-sickness medicine around an operation, and cancer teams build a preventive plan into each chemotherapy cycle, matched to how likely that treatment is to cause sickness. That plan is the foundation. Nothing on this page replaces it, and nothing here should tempt anyone to skip, delay or reduce a prescribed dose. The pressing routine below is simply something extra that a patient or a family member can do by hand, in a hospital bed or at home, while the medicine does its work.

Nausea after treatment is also a symptom with many possible causes — the drugs themselves, pain relief, a bowel that is slow to wake after surgery, constipation, anxiety, infection, or a complication that needs attention. Sorting one from another belongs to the team caring for you, which is why the warning signs later on this page matter more than any point on the wrist.

## What Chinese Medicine Reaches For {#traditional-reach}

Chinese medicine describes this kind of sickness as the stomach losing its proper downward movement. In the traditional picture the stomach should carry food and drink downward; when its qi turns upward instead — stomach qi rebelling upward, in the classical phrase — the result is nausea, retching and vomiting. The 2021 Chinese expert consensus on acupoint stimulation for post-operative gut problems frames sickness after surgery in exactly these terms, and the national nursing protocol for lung-cancer care sorts patients' nausea by patterns such as a spleen and stomach that are weak and cold, stomach yin that has run thin, or liver qi pressing sideways onto the stomach.

Neiguan (PC6), the inner pass on the pericardium channel, is the point tradition reaches for first. Classical filing names it the channel's luo-connecting point and one of the eight confluent points, opening the yin wei vessel, and the consensus describes it as linking the upper, middle and lower burners so that it can open the way between top and bottom, harmonise the stomach and send rebellious qi back down. The catalogue's recorded indications for it read like a list made for this page: nausea, vomiting, hiccups and stomachache.

Zusanli (ST36), below the knee, is the stomach channel's he-sea point and the stomach's lower he-sea point, traditionally used for stomachache, vomiting, abdominal distension and the tiredness of depletion. Hegu (LI4), the source point of the large intestine channel on the back of the hand, earns its place through an old idea that the liver and the large intestine communicate, so that a hand point can settle a liver crowding the stomach. The consensus names both as the companions most often paired with neiguan, and the lung-cancer nursing protocol pairs hegu with neiguan for hand pressure.

## Finding the Three Points {#meeting-the-points}

Neiguan (PC6) sits on the inner forearm, two cun above the wrist crease, between the two tendons that stand up when you make a loose fist and bend the wrist slightly. To find it, turn the palm up and lay three fingers of the other hand across the wrist with the edge of the first finger on the crease; the point lies just beyond the third finger, in the soft groove between the tendons. A comfortable, spreading ache tells you that you are close. If a drip, a cannula or a hospital wristband is in the way on one arm, use the other arm.

Hegu (LI4) is on the back of the hand, in the fleshy mound between the thumb and index finger, beside the middle of the bone that runs up to the index finger. Press the thumb against the side of the index finger and the mound rises; the point is at its highest spot. It is often tender, so arrive gently. Tradition treats hegu as a point to avoid during pregnancy, and this page follows that rule without exception.

Zusanli (ST36) lies on the front of the lower leg, about four finger-widths below the small hollow on the outer side of the kneecap, and one finger-width out from the sharp front edge of the shin bone. Sitting with the knee bent, slide a finger down from that hollow and then sideways off the shin; the point often feels like a slight dip in the muscle. After surgery on the leg, or with a dressing, a clot or swelling there, leave this point out.

## A Gentle Routine Beside Your Medicine {#gentle-routine}

Think of the routine as a few calm minutes beside your medicine, not a stand-in for it. Take anti-sickness drugs exactly as prescribed, then settle into a supported position — propped up in bed, or seated with the feet on the floor — and let the shoulders and jaw loosen before you start. Slow breathing, with the breath out a little longer than the breath in, belongs to the routine as much as the points do.

Begin at neiguan (PC6). Rest the pad of a thumb or fingertip in the groove between the tendons and press only as firmly as feels pleasant, steady rather than digging, for about a minute while you breathe slowly; then swap wrists. Neiguan can be repeated whenever a wave of queasiness builds, and some people prefer a shop-bought acupressure wristband for hands-free pressure. Next, if you are not pregnant, hold hegu (LI4) on each hand in the same way for half a minute to a minute, squeezing gently between the thumb and fingers of the other hand rather than pinching hard.

Finish with zusanli (ST36) on each leg when you are sitting comfortably and the legs are free: a thumb in the slight dip beside the shin, about a minute, then release gradually. Two or three short rounds a day is plenty, and a round before eating or before a planned walk is a sensible time. If you are too tired or unwell to press, a family member can do it with the same gentle pressure — they should ask before starting and stop the moment you say so.

The stop rules matter more than the sequence. Avoid broken, bruised, swollen or numb skin, and keep clear of drips, drains, dressings and wounds. Chemotherapy can lower the blood cells that help clotting, so anyone who bruises easily, or has been told their platelet count is low, should keep the touch very light or ask the oncology team first. After breast surgery with lymph nodes removed, ask the team before pressing on that arm, and use the other side meanwhile. Stop at once on sharp, electric or radiating sensation.

## What the Evidence Says {#what-the-evidence-says}

Three Chinese sources name these points. The China Association of Chinese Medicine's 2026 nursing protocols include a lung-cancer section whose advice for nausea and vomiting lists hand pressure at hegu and neiguan, alongside anti-sickness drugs given promptly as the doctor orders. The 2021 expert consensus from the Chinese Society of Anesthesiology calls neiguan the accepted standard point for preventing post-operative nausea and names zusanli and hegu among its most common companions; it describes finger pressure itself as mainly helping comfort, and prefers needling or skin-surface electrical stimulation where they are available. The Chinese Pharmaceutical Association's 2026 draft guideline on chemotherapy sickness supports needling at zusanli, neiguan and zhongwan as an add-on, and says that pressing points at home may ease mild sickness but should not replace drug treatment.

The research centres on neiguan. A 2025 Cochrane review found that non-invasive stimulation of neiguan — mostly wristbands, finger pressure and skin-surface devices — may mean fewer people feel sick or vomit after surgery than with sham pressing; its earlier 2015 version found that neiguan stimulation, much of it by needle or electrical device, roughly matched anti-sickness drugs. For chemotherapy, a 2023 review in Supportive Care in Cancer found that pressing neiguan made nausea less frequent and both nausea and vomiting less severe, and a 2017 review in the International Journal of Nursing Studies found a small easing of nausea but no clear effect on how often people vomited. A 2026 review of breast-cancer trials found that acupressure helped when added to standard anti-sickness drugs, but not when used on its own in their place.

Taken together, reviews suggest that pressing neiguan may ease nausea a little alongside standard medicine, with a less consistent effect on vomiting. The case for zusanli and hegu rests mainly on Chinese guidance and on trials that combined several points, often with needles or electrical devices, rather than on reviews of hand pressure alone, so treat them as traditional companions.

## When Self-Care Is Not Enough {#when-self-care-is-not-enough}

Some signs mean the problem is no longer one for self-care, and you should contact your medical team or seek urgent care rather than press any point. Vomiting blood, or material that looks like coffee grounds, needs prompt attention. So does being unable to keep fluids down, or signs of dehydration: very little or very dark urine, a dry mouth, dizziness on standing, or unusual sleepiness or confusion. After an operation, severe belly pain or a belly that keeps swelling and tightening, especially when no wind or stool has passed, can mean the bowel is blocked or has stopped working, and it needs assessment the same day.

Fever matters at every stage, and during chemotherapy it can be an emergency, because the treatment can lower the body's defences against infection; follow the instructions your cancer team gave you and call the number they provided. Chemotherapy sickness that the prescribed medicine does not control is also a reason to call the oncology team, not something to endure: there are other drugs and other ways to time them, and the team needs to know before the next cycle. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

## Habits and Who This Page Serves {#who-this-serves}

Small habits help alongside the points. Sip fluids little and often rather than in large gulps, eat small, plain meals when appetite allows, and keep strong cooking smells and perfumes at a distance. Fresh air, a cool cloth and a quiet room help many people, and both the nursing protocol and the pharmacy guideline mention ginger as a traditional aid; ask your team before adding anything you swallow, since some foods and supplements interact with cancer drugs.

This page serves patients and families who want something calm to do with their hands while the medical plan does its work, and readers curious about why a wrist point turns up on hospital wards. If your sickness comes on a moving boat, car or plane rather than after treatment, the site's travel nausea page covers motion sickness. The catalogue pages for PC6, LI4 and ST36 hold precise locations, recorded actions and cautions, and the surgical or cancer team that knows your case remains the right first call for anything that worries you.
