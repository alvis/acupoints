The du channel — the governing vessel — runs the length of the spine from tailbone to crown and finishes inside the upper lip, gathering the yang channels to it along the way. This page walks its twenty-eight points, the classical ideas attached to the midline, and gentle, realistic ways to explore it at home.

## The Du Channel at a Glance {#channel-at-a-glance}

In classical East Asian medicine, twelve principal channels were traced over the arms, legs, and torso — and two further lines were drawn where no paired channel travels: down the front of the body and up the back. The ascending line is the du channel, often called the governing vessel, and classical writers gave it rank accordingly: supervisor of the body’s yang side, meeting ground of the yang channels, the ridge pole of the whole meridian house. On this site the channel is drawn with twenty-eight catalogued points, numbered GV1 through GV28, beginning below the tip of the tailbone and closing just inside the upper lip.

This page is a whole-channel companion to the individual point pages. It follows the pathway as pre-modern sources described it, introduces a few points whose names carry the channel’s old story, and keeps every self-care suggestion inside the territory of light touch and quiet attention. It is educational material, not medical advice, and it teaches no diagnostic or therapeutic technique. Wording matters here: “traditionally used for” and “classically associated with” report what historical practice reached for — they say nothing about proven outcomes.

## Counting Up the Midline: Tailbone to Upper Lip {#course}

The journey starts low. Changqiang (GV1), the channel’s first point, sits between the tip of the tailbone and the anus; from there the line climbs the back of the sacrum, crossing yaoshu (GV2) exactly over the sacral hiatus, between the two little horns of the bone. It continues up the lower back along the spinous processes: yaoyangguan (GV3) lies in the depression below the fourth lumbar vertebra, level with the highest points of the hip bones; mingmen (GV4) sits below the second lumbar vertebra, directly opposite the navel; and xuanshu (GV5) stands one step higher, beneath the first lumbar vertebra. Three lumbar landmarks in a row give the channel its foothold in the waist.

Above them the line works up the spine level by level, skipping some vertebrae where no point was placed. Counting the spinous processes down from the top of the shoulders, jizhong (GV6) lies below the eleventh thoracic vertebra, zhongshu (GV7) below the tenth, jinsuo (GV8) below the ninth, and zhiyang (GV9) below the seventh — the level of the lower angles of the shoulder blades, the friendliest landmark on the whole back. Lingtai (GV10) waits below the sixth, shendao (GV11) below the fifth, shenzhu (GV12) below the third, and taodao (GV13) below the first. Just above sits dazhui (GV14), under the prominent seventh cervical vertebra — the large knob at the neck-shoulder junction that slides beneath a fingertip when the head turns.

The nape carries the line into the head. Yamen (GV15) lies on the back of the neck, half a cun above the midpoint of the hairline; fengfu (GV16) rests one cun above that same midpoint, in the hollow beneath the external occipital protuberance — the bony bump at the back of the skull; and naohu (GV17) perches on its upper border. Over the crown the points string together like beads: qiangjian (GV18) halfway between naohu and the vertex, houding (GV19) a little behind the top, and baihui (GV20) at the summit, five cun above the midpoint of the front hairline, where the midline crosses a line joining the tips of the ears. The descent then runs down the forehead — qianding (GV21), xinhui (GV22), shangxing (GV23), and shenting (GV24), half a cun above the hairline — before suliao (GV25) reaches the very tip of the nose, shuigou (GV26) settles into the philtrum groove where its upper and middle thirds meet, duiduan (GV27) arrives at the tip of the upper lip, and yinjiao (GV28) closes the line inside the lip, at the join of the frenulum and the gum.

## Four Points That Carry the Channel’s Story {#signature-points}

Mingmen (GV4) wears the most evocative name on the channel: the gate of life. Classical teaching placed the body’s vital fire in the space between the kidneys, directly opposite the navel, and treated the point as its doorway. Recorded indications gather around the lower back and the reproductive sphere — lumbosacral pain, irregular menstruation, nocturnal emission — all expressed in the old language of warming and supporting rather than the modern language of treatment.

Dazhui (GV14) is the crossroads of the upper back. Tradition counts it among the channel’s crossing points and pictures the yang currents of the whole body funnelling through the base of the neck at this one station. Its classical territory is broad: feverish illness, cough and shortness of breath, headache with a stiff neck, and skin complaints such as hives and acne all appear beside its name in the catalogue. Anyone who has felt a cold settle as heaviness across the neck and shoulders will recognise the landscape.

Baihui (GV20), the hundred meetings, crowns the head at the crossing of the midline and the ear-to-ear line. It is another catalogued crossing point, and the classical roles read like a job description for the summit: lifting what has sunk — prolapse in the old records — and steadying what scatters, from poor memory to insomnia. Shuigou (GV26), the point in the philtrum groove, completes the quartet. Classical practice kept it close as a first-aid point for fainting and collapse, and the catalogue still records it as a principal emergency point. The modern footnote matters: an unresponsive person belongs with emergency services first, and no fingertip substitutes for that call. As home knowledge, it is simply good to know the body’s map.

## Sea of Yang: What Tradition Made of the Line {#yang-sea}

Classical texts call the du the sea of the yang channels. Of the twenty-eight points on this line, the catalogue marks nine as crossing points — stations where other channels were said to climb aboard — including dazhui at the neck and fengfu and naohu at the skull’s edge. The picture is of a waterway collecting tributaries: the six yang channels of the arms and legs each send a thread toward the midline, and the governor receives them all. Governance was the metaphor the tradition favoured: a supervisor walking the ridge of a roof, keeping every slope in order.

The same writings tie the channel to the body’s deepest structures. Pathway descriptions have the du rising inside the spine and entering the brain, and the region around fengfu and naohu was handled with corresponding respect. The gate-of-life fire between the kidneys connected the vessel to the kidney system, whose stored essence classical physiology called the root of what it named pre-heaven endowment. None of this is anatomy as modern science draws it; it is a coherent pre-modern map of vitality, offered here as cultural context rather than claim.

Finally comes the partnership with the ren vessel, its mirror along the front of the body. The two midlines were read together as the body’s yin and yang seams — the ren attending the yin front, the du governing the yang back — and classical circulatory diagrams let the daily circuit of qi close its round through the pair. Readers who know this site’s ren channel pages will find the counter-image waiting line for line, and the two make a natural pair of pages to read side by side.

## Everyday Complaints the Channel Circles {#everyday-context}

Because the channel supervises the back, much of its traditional territory feels familiar to anyone with a desk and a deadline. Stiffness gathered along the spine after long sitting, heaviness across the neck and shoulders around dazhui, an aching lower back near mingmen and yaoyangguan, the foggy flatness people describe sitting at the crown — classical practice reached for these stretches of the midline again and again, and the recorded indications reflect it. Seasonal colds belong here too: the old books filed winter sniffles and feverish heaviness partly under the yang governor’s care.

Two honest notes sit beside that list. First, these are traditional associations, recorded centuries before modern clinical categories existed; they describe what historical practitioners attended to, not what any intervention achieves. Second, everyday complaint and medical warning can look alike from the inside. Stiffness after a long drive is one thing; stiffness after a fall, or stiffness with fever, numbness, or weakness in the limbs, is another entirely — and the second kind calls for assessment rather than acupressure.

## Gentle Practice Along the Midline {#self-care}

Honesty first: most of this channel runs where you cannot see or easily reach it. That is less of a problem than it sounds. The midline responds well to modest attention, and the parts within reach — crown, forehead, face, lower back — are plenty for a satisfying home exploration. Some people enjoy inviting a partner to walk two fingertips lightly up the spine from waist to shoulders; done with feather-light contact and no pressing on bone, that shared trace is the closest most of us ever come to feeling the whole line at once.

Solo, begin at the crown. Rest the flat pads of the fingers of both hands on the top of the head around baihui and draw slow, weightless circles, letting the scalp move beneath the fingers rather than pressing into the skull. Move to the forehead points with a single fingertip and even lighter contact. The nose and philtrum need no pressure at all — knowing their place on the map is enough. For the lower back, stand and let the backs of your hands rest against the waist opposite the navel, around mingmen, adding no force beyond the weight of the hands themselves, and breathe slowly for a minute or two. Warmth of contact, not push, is the whole technique.

A few boundaries keep the practice kind. Skip any patch of broken, inflamed, or numb skin, and leave the nape almost untouched — the points there sit close to structures the tradition itself marked out for professional care. If you are pregnant, live with a spinal condition, or manage a chronic illness, ask a qualified practitioner before adding self-pressure routines. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation means stop at once, wherever it appears.

## Realistic Expectations {#expectations}

What does midline self-care honestly offer? At minimum, a few minutes of posture undone: standing tall, breathing out slowly, resting warm hands against the low back, giving the neck-shoulder bridge some attention — small pleasures with value even before any question of channels arises. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and they say nothing settled about whole-vessel traditions like this one. Nothing on this page is a promise of effect, and no efficacy claims are made.

It helps to hold three frames apart. As heritage, the governing vessel is a striking piece of pre-modern system-building — a single line asked to supervise every yang current in the body, worth understanding on its own terms. As self-care, light contact along the reachable stretches is a low-cost way some people unwind. As medicine, none of it replaces assessment, and the channel’s old authority is not evidence. Keeping the frames separate is the most realistic stance available.

## Who This Page Helps {#who-this-helps}

This companion suits several kinds of reader. Desk-bound workers curious about the whole stretch of themselves they never see — the posterior midline — will find a map and a few reachable practices. Learners of meridian theory meet the yang half of the body’s central axis here, the counterpart to the ren channel pages. And anyone arriving from a single point page, perhaps baihui or dazhui, can step back far enough to see how their one point sits on a longer story running from tailbone to upper lip.

Some things lie beyond this page, always. New weakness or numbness in the limbs, changes in bladder or bowel control, pain after a fall or accident, unexplained fever alongside back pain — these call for prompt medical assessment, not self-care. Between emergencies and ordinary comfort lies the wider ground of persistent complaints, where licensed practitioners of Western and East Asian medicine alike can offer informed perspectives alongside conventional care. And when you want the finer detail, the catalogue pages for GV1 through GV28 hold the precise locations, recorded actions, and cautions this tour deliberately leaves in their keeping.
