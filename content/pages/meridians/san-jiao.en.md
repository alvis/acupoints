The hand shaoyang San Jiao channel gathers twenty-three points along one long diagonal: from the corner of the ring fingernail, up the back of the arm, over the shoulder, and around the ear to the outer end of the eyebrow. This page walks its classical route, reads its curious name, and collects gentle ways to explore the line at home.

## The San Jiao Channel at a Glance {#channel-at-a-glance}

In classical East Asian medicine, twelve principal channels were traced over the arms, legs, and torso, and each carried its own element, partner, and watch of the day. The San Jiao channel — the hand shaoyang channel — belongs to the fire element and travels the outer face of the arm, the side tradition counted among the body’s yang surfaces. Its registry on this site runs to twenty-three points, coded TH1 through TH23: it begins beside the corner of the ring fingernail and finishes at the outer tip of the eyebrow, a route that crosses arm, shoulder, neck, and face before it stops.

The channel’s name announces its strangest feature. San jiao means “three burners”, yet no anatomy lesson will find a burner to dissect: classical writers described the san jiao as a division of the body’s interior rather than an organ with edges. This page unfolds that idea gently, traces the pathway landmark by landmark, singles out five points worth knowing, and keeps every self-care suggestion firmly within light-touch territory. As everywhere on this site, wording matters: phrases such as “traditionally used for” report what historical practice reached for, not proven outcomes, and nothing here is medical advice.

## Reading the Name: Three Burners {#reading-the-name}

Classical physiology sorted the trunk’s interior into three working storeys. The upper burner, above the diaphragm, was pictured as a realm of mist, where breath and moisture disperse lightly. The middle burner, around the stomach’s territory, was imagined as a steeping, churning pool where food was transformed. The lower burner, below, was drawn as channels and ditches guiding fluids onward and out. The san jiao, in this reading, is less a thing than a division of labour: the coordinating scheme by which the body’s waterways were said to keep moving. Classical writings accordingly grant it a post close to a minister of irrigation — a manager of passages rather than a resident organ.

Tradition also assigns the channel a quiet hour of its own. In the old daily cycle of qi, the san jiao’s watch falls in the late evening, the stretch when the day is meant to wind down toward sleep. Its element is fire, shared with the pericardium channel that runs the inner arm opposite it, and the two are treated as an exterior–interior pair: distinct lines with separate point families, linked where they meet, and customarily discussed together in classical case-writing. All of this is offered as cultural context — a way of seeing the body that organised observation for centuries — not as anatomy and not as claim.

## The Course of the Channel: Ring Finger to Eyebrow {#course}

Classical pathway descriptions draw the hand shaoyang line as one continuous climb. It opens at guanchong (TH1), a pinpoint spot on the side of the ring finger nearest the little finger, right beside the nail. From there the route crosses the back of the hand: yemen (TH2) sits in the web between the ring and little fingers, zhongzhu (TH3) just behind the ring finger’s knuckle, and yangchi (TH4) rests in the hollow on the back-of-wrist crease itself.

The forearm leg keeps to the channel between the two forearm bones, roughly on the line joining yangchi to the tip of the elbow. Waiguan (TH5) lies two cun above the back-of-wrist crease — about three finger-widths. Zhigou (TH6) follows three cun up, huizong (TH7) sits about a finger-breadth to its little-finger side, sanyangluo (TH8) stands four cun above the crease, and sidu (TH9) lies five cun below the bony point of the elbow. Bend the elbow, and tianjing (TH10) settles into the hollow one cun directly above that bony tip.

From the elbow the line ascends the outer arm — qinglengyuan (TH11), xiaoluo (TH12), naohui (TH13) on the deltoid’s lower rear border — to the shoulder at jianliao (TH14), the depression below and behind the shoulder’s bony summit that appears when the arm lifts. Tianliao (TH15) crosses to the upper angle of the shoulder blade, and classical accounts add an inward branch reaching toward the chest and the three burner regions the channel is named for. The visible line then climbs the neck — tianyou (TH16) on the side of the neck, below and behind the mastoid bone, level with the jaw angle — and circles the ear: yifeng (TH17) hides behind the earlobe, chimai (TH18) marks the centre of the mastoid bump, luxi (TH19) steps one place higher along the ear’s rim, and jiaosun (TH20) sits just inside the hairline above the ear’s tip. Ermen (TH21) waits in the hollow in front of the ear’s upper cartilage flap, erheliao (TH22) occupies the temple hairline ahead of the ear, and sizhukong (TH23) closes the line at the outer end of the eyebrow.

## Five Points Worth Knowing {#signature-points}

Guanchong (TH1) is the channel’s jing-well point — in classical imagery, where channel flow seeps upward like a spring’s first drop. Its size belies its billing: older texts reach for it in urgent moments, listing febrile disease, fainting, headache, red eyes, deafness, and sore throat among its recorded indications. At home it is simply the channel’s front door, easy to find and best left to the lightest possible touch.

Yangchi (TH4), on the back-of-wrist crease, is the channel’s yuan-source point — the spot tradition reads as a window onto the burner system’s own vitality — and its classical lists lean toward wrist comfort and shoulder-arm ease. Just above, waiguan (TH5) carries two titles at once: the luo-connecting point that bridges to the pericardium channel, and one of the eight confluent points, traditionally said to open the yang wei vessel sweeping the body’s side and surface. Its recorded indications cluster around head and neck: headache, cheek pain, red swollen eyes, ear complaints, sore throat, and discomfort along the side ribs.

Higher up, tianjing (TH10) is the channel’s he-sea point, where classical imagery pictures flow gathering deep as a river reaching the sea; older texts associate it with weakness of the arm and one-sided headaches. And yifeng (TH17), tucked behind the earlobe in the hollow between the skull’s bony prominence and the jaw, guards the channel’s ear-and-cheek territory, with tinnitus, deafness, toothache, and swelling of the cheek in its classical listings. Several head-and-face points on this channel carry recorded classical cautions; the individual catalogue pages keep those details, and this overview defers to them.

## Everyday Comfort and Old Associations {#associations}

Read together, the channel’s classical indications sketch a map of the body’s sides: the ear and hearing, the temples and one-sided headaches, the throat, the cheeks and jaw, the side ribs, and the shoulder-arm corridor the line physically travels. Water runs through the theme as well, inherited from the burner physiology inside the name. When classical writers wanted a point for sudden deafness or a swollen cheek, the hand shaoyang line was a natural place to look — which is why so many of its points sit where the face meets the ear.

Modern life writes its own footnote along the same line. Hours at a keyboard load the outer forearm and the shoulder’s rear; long screen evenings leave the temples tight and the ears tired of noise. None of this turns a channel into therapy, and this page makes no efficacy claims. Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, with study quality varying widely across applications. What tracing and gentle holding honestly offer is quieter: a few minutes of deliberate attention along a line the hands can reach without help.

Keeping the frames apart makes expectations fair. As heritage, the channel is a centuries-old way of organising the body’s sides, waters, and evening hours into one story. As self-care, it is a low-cost relaxation ritual some people enjoy. As medicine, neither replaces assessment: ear symptoms, jaw pain, or shoulder trouble that persists call for a clinician, whatever a wellness routine suggests.

## Gentle Self-Care Along the Channel {#self-care}

The simplest exploration asks no anatomy lesson. Turn the forearm so the back of the hand faces up, rest the opposite palm on the wrist, and draw a slow, unhurried line between the two forearm bones toward the elbow tip — the same sweep the classical pathway takes. Repeat a few times on each arm, letting the pace follow the breath rather than the clock. Pressure stays feather-light throughout; the aim is attention, not manipulation.

Three specific stops forgive beginners well. Yangchi (TH4) sits in plain sight on the back-of-wrist crease and welcomes a soft fingertip hold. Waiguan (TH5), a little higher between the forearm bones, takes a gentle sustained press kindly when the forearm is supported on a table. Yifeng (TH17) behind the earlobe asks for the lightest contact of all — a resting fingertip, never digging. In every case the home rhythm is patient: settle a fingertip, apply only as much pressure as feels pleasant, hold through several slow breaths, then release gradually.

Boundaries keep the practice friendly. Skip broken, inflamed, or numbed skin; give the face, ear, and neck points particular restraint; and never probe firmly into the hollows behind the ear. If you are pregnant, manage a health condition, take medication that affects bleeding, or feel unsure, ask a qualified practitioner before adding self-pressure routines. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation is always a signal to stop at once.

## Who This Page Helps {#who-this-helps}

This overview suits readers who want the whole channel before its parts: newcomers meeting the meridian system through an arm line that finishes at the eyebrow; desk workers curious about the outer-forearm corridor their days quietly wear; and the historically minded, who often find the san jiao the most philosophically interesting entry in the classical catalogue precisely because it names a division rather than an organ. Readers already working through the point pages can use it as a map showing how twenty-three individual entries join into one route.

It is equally honest about its edges. Anyone with ear symptoms that persist or worsen, new one-sided ringing, facial changes, or shoulder and jaw trouble that lingers belongs with a clinician first; self-care follows assessment rather than replacing it. For everyone else, the catalogue pages for TH1 through TH23 carry precise locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions, ready whenever a single point deserves a closer look.
