A walking tour of the foot taiyin Spleen channel: twenty-one catalogued points climbing from the inner corner of the big toenail, up the whole inner leg, and through the flank to the ribs; the earth-element ideas of nourishment, blood, and steadiness attached to them; and gentle, honest ways to explore the line at home.

## The Spleen Channel at a Glance {#channel-at-a-glance}

Of the twelve principal channels mapped by classical East Asian medicine, the spleen channel — the foot taiyin channel — is the quiet workhorse of the group. It keeps to the body’s inner landscapes: beginning at the inner corner of the big toenail, it climbs the entire medial line of the leg, crosses the groin, spreads across the lower abdomen, and finishes high on the flank beside the ribs. The registry draws it with twenty-one catalogued points, numbered SP1 through SP21, from yinbai at the toe to dabao on the side of the chest. In the five-phase framework it carries the earth element — the phase of late summer, harvest, and the centre — associations that shaped centuries of thinking about digestion, steadiness, and care.

Tradition assigned this channel a household portfolio. The spleen system was imagined as the body’s granary office: it received food and drink, transformed them into qi and blood, and moved nourishment outward to flesh and limb. It was also called the steward of blood, trusted to keep the bloodstream moving within its pathways, and classical writers tied it closely to women’s cycles. Its theoretical partner is the stomach channel, the exterior–interior companion running the leg’s front line; the two were read together whenever meals, appetite, or strength came up for discussion.

This page is a whole-channel companion to the individual point pages. It describes the pathway as older sources understood it, introduces the points and the roles tradition gave them, and gathers self-care suggestions that stay strictly within light-touch territory. One reading key before anything else: phrases like “traditionally used for” describe where historical practice turned its attention — they record orientation, never outcome. Nothing here teaches diagnosis or treatment, and the catalogue pages for SP1 through SP21 remain the authority on exact locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions.

## Tracing the Course: Big Toe to Rib-Side {#course}

The journey can be walked landmark by landmark. It starts at yinbai (SP1), on the inner side of the big toe right beside the toenail’s corner. Crossing the inner foot, dadu (SP2) sits just beyond the big toe’s knuckle joint and taibai (SP3) just behind it, both along the border where the darker skin of the foot’s side meets the paler sole. Gongsun (SP4) waits farther back, ahead of the base of the long bone running to the big toe, and shangqiu (SP5) settles into the hollow in front of and below the inner ankle knob, midway between that knob and the boat-shaped bone at the foot’s inner arch.

Then comes the climb. Sanyinjiao (SP6) stands three cun — one four-finger stack — above the tip of the inner ankle knob, tucked just behind the shinbone’s inner ridge. Lougu (SP7) repeats the address six cun up, and diji (SP8) holds the same line below the knee. At yinlingquan (SP9) the shinbone swells toward the joint, and the point hides in the soft hollow behind and below that flare. Above the knee, xuehai (SP10) rests two cun above the inner end of the kneecap’s upper border, on the rise of the thigh muscle, and jimen (SP11) continues the ascent another six cun toward the groin.

The trunk crossing is brief but busy. Chongmen (SP12) sits in the groin crease beside the artery’s beat; fushe (SP13) lies four cun below the navel’s level and four cun out from the midline; fujie (SP14) follows; daheng (SP15) lines up level with the navel itself, again four cun to the side; and fu’ai (SP16) completes the abdominal arc three cun above the navel. The finish is a ladder up the flank: shidou (SP17), tianxi (SP18), xiongxiang (SP19), and zhourong (SP20) occupy the fifth, fourth, third, and second intercostal spaces respectively, each six cun from the midline, before dabao (SP21) closes the channel on the side of the chest in the sixth space along the midaxillary line. Classical descriptions add interior branches that enter the trunk’s middle to bind the channel to the stomach and spleen organs themselves and rise toward the heart — geography belonging to the old texts, retold here as context.

## Storied Stops Along the Line {#storied-stops}

Gongsun (SP4) carries a founding ancestor’s name and a double classical title. As the channel’s luo-connecting point it was said to bridge toward the stomach partner; as one of the eight confluent points it was assigned to open the chong vessel, the extraordinary vessel tradition linked with blood and the body’s deep currents. Old records reached for it around stomachache, vomiting, and abdominal pain — a measure of how much traffic ran between this one spot on the inner foot and the middle of the torso.

Higher up, sanyinjiao (SP6) may be the channel’s most famous address: a crossing where the spleen, liver, and kidney channels were said to meet, which explains a recorded list stretching from the menstrual cycle to digestion, sleep, and skin. Weight follows fame. The registry attaches a prominent warning to this crossing concerning pregnancy, so anyone pregnant should leave the point alone entirely and raise the topic with a qualified practitioner first; its own catalogue page tells the fuller story. Nearby, diji (SP8) serves as the xi-cleft point — the position classical theory set aside for moments when a line’s activity flares suddenly — with cycle-related entries leading its old records.

Three more stops earn their reputations. Yinlingquan (SP9) holds the he-sea rank, the grade given to the place where a channel’s current settles into depth — the old image is inland waters finally meeting the sea — and its records lead with fluid-themed entries: oedema, distension, difficult urination. Xuehai (SP10) answers to a name that translates plainly as sea of blood, and the logic shows in its recorded company of menstrual and skin complaints, since both were read through blood in the old framework. Dabao (SP21), the final point, bears a grander title still: the great luo of the spleen, imagined by early commentators as the master junction gathering the network of connecting channels — with generalised aching and weakness of the limbs among its recorded concerns.

## Earth, Granary, and Blood: Classical Associations {#classical-associations}

Earth, in the five-phase scheme, is the hinge of seasons: late summer, harvest, the moment when growth turns into stored provision. The spleen system inherited the whole metaphor. Classical physiology made it the office of transformation and transportation — receiving food and drink, cooking them down into qi and blood, dispatching the result to muscles and limbs. Writers spoke of the spleen and stomach together as the foundation of life after birth, the supply line every other system leaned on, and much traditional advice about eating calmly, seasonally, and in moderation grew from exactly this soil.

Two further duties round out the portrait. The spleen was named the steward of blood, charged with keeping it flowing inside the vessels rather than straying — the reason so many bleeding-themed and cycle-themed entries cluster at this channel’s points. Its emotional assignment was pensiveness: worry, rumination, the mind chewing one thought over and over. Classical texts described excess brooding as knotting the middle, dulling appetite and weighing down the limbs — an observation about temperament told in the vocabulary of channels.

These framings arrive as cultural and historical context, attributed generally to pre-modern sources rather than quoted or tested. They explain why the channel surfaces in seasonal-wellness conversation, harvest-time routines, and reflective writing about slowing down. They are not statements about anatomy as modern medicine describes it, and this page draws no clinical claims from them; the modern study of digestion and blood proceeds by entirely different methods, and nothing here substitutes for it.

## The Channel in Everyday Conversation {#everyday-lens}

Read through the old lens, the spleen channel collects the vocabulary of ordinary heaviness. Traditional discussions reached for its points around fullness or sluggishness after meals, wavering appetite, loose stools, tired or weighty limbs after long days upright, mild puffiness of the lower legs, and the monthly cycle in its irregular moods. The catalogue’s recorded indications repeat the theme: distension, diarrhoea, oedema, weakness of the legs, and a long column of cycle-related entries at the crossing points. Framed honestly, these are the situations historical practice brought to this line — not a promise of what pressing it delivers.

Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and evidence specific to any single point remains thin. That candour sets the realistic frame: a few unhurried minutes of light pressure may feel settling and may mark out a pause in a crowded day, at little cost — but it is comfort work, not therapy, and it serves worst precisely where symptoms are loud. Persistent digestive trouble, cycle changes that worry you, new or one-sided leg swelling, or any symptom that frightens you belong with proper assessment first; the calendar of wellness routines can wait.

## Gentle Self-Care Along the Channel {#self-care}

The simplest practice is a slow trace. Seated comfortably, rest the fingers of one hand at the inner ankle and draw a feather-light line straight up the inner calf, around the bend of the knee, and partway up the inner thigh — the channel’s own road, travelled in its own direction. Measure the pace by a few long exhales rather than by the clock, then swap sides. Pressure stays light enough that you could hold a conversation throughout; the purpose is attention, not manipulation.

Two stops welcome beginners warmly. Yinlingquan (SP9): slide a fingertip up the shinbone’s inner edge from the ankle until the bone swells toward the knee; the soft hollow just behind and below that swell is the spot. Xuehai (SP10): rest the fingertips two cun above the inner end of the kneecap’s upper border, on the gentle rise of the thigh muscle — many people simply cup the knee and let the hand land there. In both places settle a fingertip pad, press only to pleasantness, hold through several slow breaths or trace small circles, then ease off gradually.

Several boundaries keep the practice on the right side of gentle. Skip any point over broken, inflamed, or varicose skin, and pause during feverish illness. Pregnancy changes the map materially: leave sanyinjiao (SP6) untouched altogether, and ask a qualified practitioner before building any routine on this channel. People managing circulation problems, diabetes, or reduced leg sensation do well to check with a clinician first, and sharp, electric, or radiating sensation always ends the session on the spot.

## Who This Page Helps {#who-this-page-helps}

Map-minded readers get a coherent tour of one of tradition’s most quietly important channels. People assembling a low-key wind-down routine gain a leg-friendly sequence that asks only ten patient minutes. Students of the classical framework can watch the earth element’s themes — nourishment, blood, steadiness, harvest — run from cosmology down to particular points. And anyone who simply enjoys structured attention to their own body may find the inner-leg line a natural fit: every stop on it is reachable, nameable, and undramatic.

Others should route elsewhere first. Severe or sudden abdominal pain, unexplained bleeding, a possible pregnancy concern, marked or one-sided leg swelling, or any symptom that frightens you calls for prompt professional assessment — not a pressure routine. For ongoing matters such as recurrent digestive complaints or cycle difficulties, licensed practitioners, including registered acupuncturists and East Asian medicine clinicians, work alongside conventional care rather than instead of it; bring your questions and your medication list. And for the fine print of any individual point — exact location, recorded actions, dataset cautions — the catalogue pages for SP1 through SP21 hold everything this overview deliberately leaves to them.
