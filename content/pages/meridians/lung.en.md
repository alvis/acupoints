An evergreen tour of the hand taiyin Lung channel: the classical route from chest to thumb, the eleven named points along it, the old associations linking breath, skin, and grief, and gentle, realistic ways to explore the line at home.

## The Lung Channel at a Glance {#channel-at-a-glance}

In classical East Asian medicine, twelve principal channels were mapped along the arms, legs, and torso, and the lung channel — the hand taiyin channel — holds a distinguished place among them. Traditional texts give it the opening position in the daily cycle of qi, the channel where circulation was imagined to begin its rounds each day. It carries the metal element in the five-phase framework, an association that ties it to autumn, to dryness and clarity, and to the idea of gathering what is valuable and releasing what is not. On this site the channel is drawn with eleven catalogued points, numbered LU1 through LU11, beginning at zhongfu high on the chest and finishing at shaoshang beside the thumbnail.

This page is a whole-channel companion to those point pages. It describes the pathway as classical sources understood it, introduces the points and the roles tradition assigned them, and collects self-care ideas that stay firmly in the territory of light touch. It is educational material, not medical advice, and it deliberately avoids teaching any diagnostic or therapeutic technique. Where wording matters: phrases such as “traditionally used for” or “classically associated with” report what historical practice reached for — they are not statements about proven outcomes.

## The Course of the Channel: Chest to Thumb {#course}

Classical pathway descriptions draw the hand taiyin channel as a single graceful sweep. The channel is said to originate in the middle of the torso, rise toward the throat region, and surface on the upper outer chest — where zhongfu and yunmen, its first two points, sit close together. From there the pathway descends along the front of the shoulder and continues down the soft inner line of the upper arm and forearm, crosses the front of the wrist near where the pulse is usually taken, crosses the muscular pad at the base of the thumb, and ends at the thumb’s outer corner. A slender connecting branch leaves the main line just above the wrist and reaches toward the index finger, the starting ground of the paired large intestine channel.

That geography matters for practice as much as for story. Nearly the whole channel runs along surfaces you can reach with your own opposite hand: the chest points with a light flat palm, the arm points with easy fingertip contact, the wrist and thumb points within a few centimetres of each other. It is one reason the lung channel appears so often in introductory acupressure teaching, and one reason tracing it makes such a natural first exploration of the meridian system.

The partnership with the large intestine channel is worth pausing on. The two channels are treated in classical theory as an exterior–interior pair: distinct lines with separate point families, yet linked through the branch near the wrist and believed to influence each other deeply. Many traditional practitioners read symptoms across the pair — a throat complaint discussed alongside large intestine points, for instance — which is a reminder that the channels were always conceived as a connected landscape rather than isolated threads.

## Eleven Points, One Line {#eleven-points}

Zhongfu (LU1) leads the channel. Classically ranked as the front-mu point of the lung — the point tradition treats as closest to the organ’s own sphere — it sits high on the upper chest and historically drew attention whenever chest and breath were the concern. Yunmen (LU2), just above it beneath the outer end of the collarbone, shares much of the same classical territory. Tianfu (LU3) and xiabai (LU4) carry the line down the inner upper arm; both appear in older texts around cough and breath, with occasional notes about the arm itself.

At the elbow crease lies chize (LU5), the channel’s he-sea point — in classical imagery, the place where channel flow gathers and deepens like a river reaching the sea. Kongzui (LU6), further along the forearm, is the xi-cleft point, a slot tradition reserves for moments when a channel’s activity suddenly intensifies. Lieque (LU7) is doubly titled: the luo-connecting point that links this channel to its large intestine partner, and one of the eight confluent points, traditionally said to open the ren vessel that runs up the front of the body. Jingqu (LU8), just above the wrist fold, completes the forearm group as the channel’s jing-river point.

Taiyuan (LU9) may be the most storied of the eleven. Sitting at the front of the wrist, right where the radial pulse beats, it carries three classical titles at once: the channel’s shu-stream point, its yuan-source point — the spot said to reflect the organ’s own vitality — and the eight-influential point associated with the vessels throughout the body. Just beyond it, yuji (LU10) fills the fleshy pad at the base of the thumb, and the line closes with shaoshang (LU11), the tiny jing-well point beside the thumbnail, where classical physiology pictures the current handing over to the next channel in sequence.

## Breath, Skin, and Sadness: Classical Associations {#breath-skin-grief}

Classical texts assign the lung system a governing role over breath: taking in what the tradition calls clear qi from the air, mingling it with the body’s fundamental energies, and sending it on its way. The same writings extend the lung’s domain outward to the surface — skin and body hair are described as the lung’s exterior expression, and defensive qi, the surface-circulating aspect of the body’s protections, is said to be spread under the lung’s direction. This is why so many classical discussions move fluidly between breathing, skin, and resilience against outside influences as one continuous topic.

The most humanly resonant association, though, concerns emotion. Classical literature repeatedly pairs the lung with sadness and grief, describing sorrow as an emotion said to collect in the chest and weigh on breath — and, conversely, depicting free, settled breathing as a sign of emotional ease. Seasonally the pairing extends to autumn, the metal element’s season, when leaves fall and the mood of letting go pervades poetry and medicine alike.

These framings are offered here as cultural and historical context, attributed generally to pre-modern sources rather than quoted or verified. They explain why the lung channel appears in contemplative breathing practices, seasonal wellness routines, and reflective writing about transition. They are not clinical claims, and this page makes none: emotion, breath, and skin interact in ways modern medicine studies with its own methods, and nothing here substitutes for that.

## Gentle Self-Care Along the Channel {#self-care}

The simplest practice needs no anatomy lesson. Rest one palm lightly on the upper chest near the shoulder, breathe out slowly, then draw the hand in a light, unhurried line down the inner arm toward the thumb — the same sweep the classical pathway takes. Repeat a few times on each arm, letting the pace follow the breath rather than the clock. Pressure stays feather-light; the goal is attention, not manipulation.

When you want to meet individual points, two suit beginners well. Lieque (LU7), on the forearm a little above the wrist crease, is easy to reach across the body and tolerates a gentle sustained hold well. Chize (LU5), at the inner end of the elbow crease, responds nicely to a soft hold with the arm supported on a table. In both places the classic home technique is patient: settle a fingertip, apply only as much pressure as feels pleasant, hold while taking several slow breaths, then release gradually. Traditional practice reaches for these points around seasonal changes; you can simply enjoy the quiet they invite.

A few boundaries keep the practice friendly. Avoid broken, inflamed, or numbed skin; skip deep prodding entirely; and treat the chest-area points with particular restraint — a light flat-palm touch is plenty there, and pressing on the ribs is unnecessary. If you are pregnant, live with a health condition, take medication that affects bleeding, or are simply unsure, ask a qualified practitioner before adding self-pressure routines. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation is always a signal to stop.

## Realistic Expectations {#expectations}

What can channel-based self-care honestly offer? At minimum, a few minutes of deliberate slowing: quiet attention, longer exhalations, and a break from screens have value even before any point is pressed. Reviews of acupressure research suggest that stimulating certain well-known points may ease some symptoms in some contexts, with motion-related nausea the most consistently discussed example; the same reviews are candid that study quality varies and results remain unsettled for most applications. Nothing on this page should be read as a promise of effect.

It helps to hold tradition and evidence in different frames. As heritage, the lung channel is a coherent, centuries-old way of organising observation about breath, surface, and feeling — worth understanding on its own terms. As self-care, tracing and gentle holding are low-cost relaxation practices some people enjoy. As medicine, neither replaces assessment: a lingering cough, new breathlessness, or skin changes call for a clinician, whatever the wellness calendar suggests. Holding all three frames at once is the most realistic stance of all.

## When to Seek Professional Care {#when-to-seek-care}

Some situations belong promptly with a doctor, full stop. Difficulty breathing, chest pain or pressure, a cough that persists or worsens, coughing blood, high fever, sudden skin changes, or swelling of the face or limbs are signals to seek medical assessment without waiting to try self-care. The same applies to any symptom that frightens you or interferes with sleep, work, or exercise — urgency is information, and it deserves a professional audience.

Between emergencies and everyday comfort lies the wider territory of ongoing conditions — asthma, recurring respiratory infections, eczema, persistently low mood — where licensed practitioners, including registered acupuncturists and traditional medicine clinicians, can offer channel-informed perspectives alongside conventional care. If you consult one, bring your questions and your medication list, and expect coordination with your regular doctors rather than replacement of them. And if you simply want to know more about a single point, the catalogue pages for LU1 through LU11 carry the precise locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions this overview intentionally leaves to them.
