A whole-channel tour of the hand taiyang Small Intestine channel: the classical road from the corner of the little finger up the arm and around the shoulder blade to the ear, the nineteen catalogued points along it, the old fire-element partnership with the heart, and gentle, realistic ways to explore the line at home.

## The Small Intestine Channel at a Glance {#channel-at-a-glance}

In classical East Asian medicine, twelve principal channels were mapped along the arms, legs, and torso, and the small intestine channel — the hand taiyang channel — draws the sunlit outer line of one arm. It belongs to the fire element in the five-phase framework, and it travels as the outward-facing partner of the heart channel, with which it forms an exterior–interior couple. Classical physiology pictured the small intestine organ itself as the body’s sorting house: it receives what the stomach prepares, separates the clear from the turbid, and passes each onward. On this site the channel carries nineteen catalogued points, numbered SI1 through SI19, beginning at shaoze beside the little finger’s nail and closing at tinggong in the hollow directly in front of the ear.

This page walks the whole line, the way a walking tour precedes any visit to a single room. It traces the pathway as classical sources understood it, introduces the stations and the roles tradition gave them, and keeps every self-care suggestion on the near side of gentle. Nothing here is medical advice, and nothing here teaches diagnosis or treatment. Two phrases recur by design: “traditionally used for” and “classically associated with” record what historical practice reached for, not what has been proven to happen.

## The Course of the Channel: Little Finger to Ear {#course}

The journey starts small. Shaoze (SI1) sits on the little finger’s outer edge, right at the corner of the nail; qiangu (SI2) and houxi (SI3) follow along the creases that rise across the side of the hand when a loose fist closes. Wangu (SI4) waits in the hollow at the base of the fifth metacarpal bone, and yanggu (SI5) settles in the gap between the ulna’s styloid process and the triquetral bone at the wrist. From there the line climbs the ulnar side of the forearm’s back: zhizheng (SI7) lies five cun above the dorsal wrist crease, on the straight line joining yanggu and the elbow. Flex the elbow slightly and xiaohai (SI8) appears in the hollow between the ulna’s tip — the olecranon — and the humerus’s medial epicondyle. Jianzhen (SI9) then lifts the channel onto the shoulder: it lies below the joint’s back aspect, one cun above the rear end of the armpit’s posterior fold.

The middle passage crosses the shoulder blade in a wide arc. Naoshu (SI10) rests directly below the lower border of the scapula’s spine; tianzong (SI11) claims the centre of the infraspinous fossa, level with the fourth thoracic vertebra; bingfeng (SI12) sits in the supraspinous fossa immediately above tianzong, and quyuan (SI13) guards the fossa’s inner end, midway between naoshu and the spinous process of the second thoracic vertebra. The upper back adds a pair: jianwaishu (SI14), three cun lateral to the lower edge of the first thoracic vertebra’s spinous process, and jianzhongshu (SI15), two cun lateral to the seventh cervical vertebra’s. Then the channel rises along the neck — tianchuang (SI16) on the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, level with the Adam’s apple, and tianrong (SI17) tucked behind the angle of the jaw — swings across the cheek to quanliao (SI18) on the cheekbone’s lower border beneath the eye’s outer corner, and finishes at tinggong (SI19), the hollow in front of the ear that opens when the mouth opens.

As classical pathway descriptions tell it, the story also runs inward. An interior branch was said to descend from the shoulder region to the supraclavicular fossa, connect with the heart, follow the gullet down across the diaphragm past the stomach, and enter the small intestine, the organ the channel serves. Another branch crossed the cheek upward toward the inner corner of the eye, where the taiyang baton passes to the bladder channel, next in the daily sequence. Practically, almost the whole route lies within reach of your own hands: fingers walk the hand and forearm easily, the elbow and shoulder need only a relaxed posture, and even the shoulder-blade group can be met with a flat palm reached across the chest.

## Five Stations Worth Knowing {#signature-points}

Houxi (SI3) is the station most traditions single out. Classically ranked as the channel’s shu-stream point — in the old imagery, a place where the current widens like a hillside brook — it wears a second title as well: one of the eight confluent points, traditionally said to open the du vessel, the channel imagined rising up the spine. Old records accordingly reach for it around stiffness and pain of the head and neck, and around the lower back. Nearby, wangu (SI4) holds the channel’s yuan-source title, the spot classical theory reads as a window onto the organ’s own vitality.

Yanglao (SI6) — the name carries something close to nourishing the aged — serves as the channel’s xi-cleft point, a slot tradition reserves for moments when a channel’s activity suddenly intensifies. The catalogue records it around blurred vision and aching shoulders, backs, and arms, and its location hides a small trick: turn the palm toward the chest and a bony gap opens beside the head of the ulna. Xiaohai (SI8), whose name means small sea, is the channel’s he-sea point, where flow was thought to gather and deepen like a river meeting the tide.

On the shoulder blade itself, tianzong (SI11) occupies the very centre of the bone’s flat wing and heads the classical shortlist for scapular and shoulder-back complaints. And the terminus keeps the channel’s oldest promise: tinggong (SI19), the palace of hearing, sits before the ear and drew classical attention whenever ringing or muffled hearing entered the conversation. Together the five tell the channel’s story in miniature — a hand point for the neck and back, a forearm point for ageing eyes and tired shoulders, an elbow point of gathering, a blade-centre point, and an ear-side finale.

## Fire, the Heart’s Partner, and the Sorting House {#fire-heart-partnership}

The five-phase framework assigns this channel fire, the element of warmth, circulation, and exchange. Taiyang channels were imagined as the body’s outward-facing ministry, the tier that meets the world at the surface — and the small intestine channel spends nearly its whole length on display: hand, forearm, elbow, shoulder, shoulder blade, neck, face. Classical courtly metaphors extended the organ’s work into character: texts styled the zang–fu system as a government, and the small intestine received the role of receiving what arrives and refining it, keeping what nourishes and dispatching what must be carried away.

The partnership with the heart supplies much of the pairing’s flavour. The heart channel, hand shaoyin, runs the arm’s soft inner line while its partner lights the outer; classical theory bound them as an exterior–interior couple believed to influence each other deeply. Where the heart was said to house the spirit and hold the mind’s clear lantern, commentators liked to give the small intestine the practical half of the work — the discernment that sorts true from false much as the organ sorts clear from turbid. These are historical framings offered as cultural context: graceful ways pre-modern writers connected digestion, perception, and decision, not clinical claims.

## Everyday Complaints Tradition Brings Here {#everyday-complaints}

What did practice actually reach for along this line? The recorded indications cluster around the channel’s own geography. Neck complaints lead: stiffness and pain of the head and neck, stiffness of the nape. Shoulder and blade follow: aching shoulders, backs, and elbows, difficulty raising the arm, scapular pain. The ear and face form a third cluster — tinnitus, deafness, swollen cheeks, toothache, sore throat — and a fourth gathers around the low back and the hand itself. Breast-related entries, such as insufficient lactation, appear in the older records too, attached chiefly to the little-finger points. All of this is reported in the traditional-use sense: it describes the classical shortlists, not verified outcomes.

How does that inheritance meet the present day? Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and the same reviews are candid that study quality varies across applications. A quiet evening ritual of stretching the neck, rolling the shoulders, and resting a fingertip on a favourite point costs little and, kept gentle, risks little — which is a fairer promise than relief. Anyone whose neck or shoulder trouble follows an injury, or arrives with fever, numbness, or spreading weakness, belongs with assessment first and routine second.

## Exploring the Channel Gently at Home {#gentle-self-care}

The simplest exploration asks nothing but a free hand. Rest a palm on the little-finger side of the other hand, breathe out slowly, and draw it in one unhurried line up the forearm’s back, around the point of the elbow, along the upper arm, and over the shoulder toward the shoulder blade and ear — the sweep the classical pathway takes. Repeat it a few times in each direction, letting pace follow breath rather than the clock. Pressure stays feather-light throughout; the aim is attention, not kneading.

For individual points, two forgive beginners readily. Houxi (SI3) likes a loose fist resting on a table: settle a fingertip into the crease-side hollow just below the little finger’s knuckle and simply stay, taking three or four slow breaths before releasing. Tianzong (SI11) sits mid-blade, easiest to meet with the opposite hand reached across the chest, or with a partner’s flat palm; either way a light hold beats any firm press. The neck points — tianchuang, tianrong — deserve particular respect: the great vessels pass close by, so a feather touch beside the muscle’s back border is plenty, and pushing forward into the neck is unnecessary. Skip broken or irritated skin entirely, and stop at once on sharp, electric, or radiating sensation.

A few standing invitations to caution round this out. Pregnancy, a managed health condition, or medication that affects bleeding or skin all warrant a conversation with a qualified practitioner before point work joins the week. Feverish days call for rest rather than routines. And the scapular points lie over ribs and lung territory — one more reason an honest tool there is a light flat palm, never probing thumbs.

## Who This Page Helps, and When Care Comes First {#who-this-page-helps}

This page suits several readers: desk workers whose necks and shoulders collect the day’s residue; newcomers who want one well-shaped channel to learn the meridian map by; readers arriving from the individual point pages who wish to see the line those pages sit on; and the simply curious, who enjoy watching an old medical imagination lay a river system over the human body.

Expectations stay modest by design. Tracing and gentle holding are low-cost relaxation practices some people find pleasant; they are not treatments, and this page makes no efficacy claims. Some situations belong promptly with a doctor, full stop: any injury from a fall, sudden weakness or numbness in an arm, new or rapidly changing hearing, a lump in the neck, fever together with a stiff neck, or any symptom that frightens you. Ongoing troubles — recurring neck pain, persistent ringing, a shoulder that will no longer lift — can be brought to licensed practitioners, including registered acupuncturists and traditional medicine clinicians, as complements to conventional care rather than replacements. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.
