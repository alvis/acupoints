The foot jueyin Liver channel climbs from the outer corner of the big toenail, up the inner leg and thigh to the ribs below the breast — fourteen catalogued points along the way. Meet the classical route, the wood-element associations of flow, sight, and stored blood, and gentle ways to explore the line at home.

## The Liver Channel at a Glance {#channel-at-a-glance}

In classical East Asian medicine, twelve principal channels were mapped along the limbs and torso, and the liver channel — the foot jueyin channel — holds the closing position among them. Where the lung channel was given the opening of the daily cycle, traditional writings let the round of qi finish here: the liver channel completes the circuit and hands momentum back to the lung, so that each new day begins again. In the five-phase framework it belongs to the wood element, an affiliation that ties it to spring, to shoots unfolding and sap rising, and to the qualities of suppleness and steady growth. On this site the channel is drawn with fourteen catalogued points, numbered LV1 through LV14, beginning beside the outer corner of the big toenail and finishing on the ribs below the breast.

This page is a whole-channel companion to the point pages. It traces the pathway as classical sources understood it, introduces the points and the roles tradition assigned them, and gathers self-care ideas that stay firmly within light touch. It is written for newcomers who want the map before visiting individual sites, for readers who already know taichong (LV3) and wonder about the rest of the line, and for anyone weighing traditional framings alongside modern care. It is educational material, not medical advice, and it deliberately avoids teaching any diagnostic or therapeutic technique. Where wording matters: phrases such as “traditionally used for” report what historical practice reached for — they are not statements about proven outcomes.

## The Route of the Channel: Big Toe to Rib-Side {#course}

Classical pathway descriptions draw the foot jueyin channel as an upward climb along the body’s inner landscape. The line begins at the outer corner of the big toenail, crosses the dorsum of the foot through the web between the first and second toes, and climbs the shallow valley between the two long bones of the forefoot to the ridge where those bones meet. It then passes in front of the inner ankle bone, settling into the hollow just inside the strong cord-like tendon that runs down the front of the shin — a spot framed, in the catalogue’s wording, between the spleen channel’s shangqiu (SP5) and the stomach channel’s jiexi (ST41). From there the channel ascends the inner shin, touching the flat inner face of the tibia at five cun above the tip of the ankle bone and again two cun higher, slips behind the inner bump at the top of the shin bone — where xiguan (LV7) waits, one cun behind its spleen-channel neighbour yinlingquan (SP9) — and arrives at the inner end of the crease behind the flexed knee.

Above the knee the line continues up the inner thigh, leaving yinbao (LV9) four cun above the bony prominence at the lower end of the thigh bone, between the strap-like muscle along the front-inner edge and its neighbour. It then runs to the root of the thigh, where zuwuli (LV10) and yinlian (LV11) are counted straight down from a stomach-channel landmark at the groin crease, at three cun and two cun respectively, and jimai (LV12) rests beside the pubic bone where the groin pulse can be felt, two and a half cun from the midline. The final two points turn onto the trunk: zhangmen (LV13) lies below the free end of the eleventh rib at the side of the body, and qimen (LV14) sits directly below the nipple in the sixth intercostal space, four cun from the midline. A note on units: cun are proportional measures read off the body itself, not lengths a ruler could confirm.

Classical writing also follows branches inside the body: around the lower abdomen and the reproductive region, across the ribs, linking with the stomach and the lung, and climbing through the throat toward the eyes and the crown of the head. The surface handovers have their own order. The liver channel receives the current from its exterior–interior partner, the gallbladder channel, whose line finishes at the outer corner of the fourth toe; and in the classical physiology of the daily circuit, the moment the liver channel completes its run is the moment the lung channel takes up the next round. The channels were conceived as one continuous loop rather than twelve separate threads.

## Fourteen Points Worth Meeting {#fourteen-points}

Dadun (LV1) opens the line as its jing-well point — in classical imagery, the wellhead where the channel’s current first rises to the surface. Xingjian (LV2), the ying-spring just behind the toe-web, marks the current’s first gathering. Taichong (LV3) is the channel’s most storied ground: it is doubly titled as the shu-stream point and the yuan-source point — the latter read by tradition as the place the liver’s own vitality presses closest to the surface — and its name is usually glossed as the great thoroughfare. Its recorded action list is correspondingly wide: headache, dizziness, red and painful eyes, rib-side discomfort, vomiting and abdominal complaints, and a long run of menstrual entries.

Zhongfeng (LV4) stands in front of the inner ankle as the jing-river point. Ligou (LV5), five cun up the flat inner face of the shin, is the luo-connecting point — classically read as the tie that binds this channel to its gallbladder partner. Zhongdu (LV6), two cun higher yet, is the xi-cleft, the slot tradition reserves for moments when a channel’s activity suddenly intensifies. Ququan (LV8), at the inner end of the crease behind the bent knee, closes the leg as the he-sea point; its name, usually rendered “the spring in the bend”, pictures the current pooling deep before the line turns upward.

The remaining six points climb the thigh and trunk. Yinbao (LV9) sits midway up the inner thigh; zuwuli (LV10), yinlian (LV11), and jimai (LV12) gather at the thigh’s root, where the recorded actions cluster around lower-abdominal, urinary, and reproductive themes and the setting is personal territory most home practices simply pass over. Zhangmen (LV13), below the free end of the eleventh rib, wears two classical titles at once — front-mu point of the spleen, and the eight-influential point associated with the zang organs. Qimen (LV14) completes the line as the front-mu point of the liver itself, directly below the nipple in the sixth intercostal space. Tradition treats front-mu points as the gates where an organ’s sphere presses nearest the chest and belly, which is why these two recur in classical discussions of rib-side and digestive matters.

## Flow, Stored Blood, and Sight: Traditional Associations {#flow-blood-sight}

The wood image colours everything classical writing says about the liver. Spring shoots bend without breaking; sap moves quietly and persistently; growth follows its own timing. Classical physiology gave the liver system a matching duty: to keep qi coursing freely through the body — a role later traditions summarised with a single verb, to course and discharge. When that movement stalls, the old texts speak of constraint and stagnation, and the emotion classically paired with a stilled flow is irritation: the short temper, the sighing frustration of a spring that cannot quite arrive. Many traditional summaries compress the whole job into that one image — keep it moving.

A second duty concerns storage. Tradition assigns blood to the liver’s keeping: when a person rests, blood is said to return inward to be stored, and when activity calls, it is sent outward again. Classical gynaecology folded the menstrual cycle into this picture of storing and releasing — and the catalogue echoes the same emphasis, for menstrual themes such as irregular menstruation, period pain, and absence of menstruation appear again and again on this channel’s recorded action lists. Sight, too, was entrusted to the liver: the old phrase “the liver opens into the eyes” explains why red and painful eyes stand recorded under xingjian (LV2) and taichong (LV3). Nails and sinews were read as the liver’s outward expression, which is where classical writers located the channel’s attention to knees and lower-limb strength — entries that dot the knee-area points.

The partnership with the gallbladder channel completes the frame. As an exterior–interior pair, the two were believed to work in tandem, and a familiar commonplace cast the liver as the drafter of plans and the gallbladder as the maker of decisions; traditional physicians accordingly read some complaints across both lines. These associations are presented here as cultural and historical context, attributed generally to pre-modern sources rather than quoted or verified. They explain why the liver channel surfaces in conversations about stress seasons, screen-tired eyes, and premenstrual days — as a lens, not a diagnosis. Modern perspectives on mood, vision, and circulation proceed by their own methods, and nothing here substitutes for them.

## Exploring the Line Gently {#self-care}

The simplest practice asks no anatomy lesson. Sit comfortably, rest a hand over the web between the first two toes of one foot, breathe out slowly, and draw the fingertips in a light, unhurried stroke up the inner shin and inner thigh toward the hip — the same climb the classical pathway takes. Move to the other side and repeat a few times, letting the pace follow the breath rather than the clock. Pressure stays feather-light throughout; the goal is attention, not manipulation, and the torso needs no stroking at all for the exercise to feel complete.

Two stops suit beginners especially well. Taichong (LV3) welcomes a settled fingertip in the valley between the long bones of the forefoot: apply only as much pressure as feels pleasant, hold while taking several slow breaths, then lift away gradually — the spot tolerates a patient, sustained touch kindly. Ligou (LV5), on the flat inner face of the shin five cun above the ankle’s tip, is easy to reach from a chair and responds to the same soft hold. Traditional practice reaches for these places around tense, crowded days; you can simply enjoy the pause they create, with no further agenda.

A few boundaries keep the exploration friendly. Skip broken, inflamed, or numbed skin, and decline deep prodding everywhere. The trunk points — zhangmen (LV13) and qimen (LV14) — merit a light open-palm rest at most; pressing between the ribs accomplishes nothing worth the discomfort. The three points at the thigh’s root belong to private territory, and plenty of home practices simply leave them out; professional settings handle those regions with draping and consent. Anyone pregnant, managing a health condition, or taking medication that affects bleeding should ask a qualified practitioner before adding self-pressure routines. Sharp, electric, or radiating sensation is always a signal to stop.

## What a Home Practice Can Honestly Offer {#expectations}

At minimum, a few minutes of deliberate slowing: quiet attention, longer exhalations, and time away from screens carry value even before any point is touched. As for the pressure itself, where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and no review crowns any single point a settled remedy for anything. Nothing on this page should be read as a promise of relief, and the honest pleasures of the practice — rhythm, quiet, contact — are available without one.

It helps to hold tradition and evidence in separate frames. As heritage, the liver channel is a coherent, centuries-old way of organising observation about flow, sight, storage, and the seasons — worth understanding on its own terms. As self-care, tracing and gentle holding are low-cost relaxation habits some people genuinely enjoy. As medicine, neither substitutes for assessment: a headache that will not leave, bleeding that is not normal for you, or fatigue that reshapes your weeks belong with a clinician, whatever the wellness calendar suggests. Holding all three frames at once remains the most realistic stance.

## When Symptoms Belong with a Professional {#when-to-seek-care}

Some situations call for prompt medical assessment, full stop. A sudden severe headache, changes in vision or speech, weakness on one side of the body, yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes, severe or persistent abdominal pain, a newly found breast lump, unusually heavy bleeding, or any symptom that frightens you — these deserve a clinician without waiting to try self-care first. Urgency is information, and it deserves a professional audience.

Between emergencies and everyday comfort lies the wider ground of ongoing matters — recurring tension-type headaches, stubborn irritability or low mood, menstrual irregularities, digestive complaints — where licensed practitioners, including registered acupuncturists and traditional medicine clinicians, can offer channel-informed perspectives alongside conventional care. Bring your questions and your medication list, and expect coordination with your regular doctors rather than replacement of them. And if a single point draws your curiosity, the catalogue pages for LV1 through LV14 carry the precise locations, recorded actions, and dataset cautions this overview intentionally leaves to them.
