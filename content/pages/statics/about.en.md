Acupoints.com describes itself here: why a calm reference for the 361 standardized acupoints was worth building, how each page is sourced, what the editors claim and deliberately decline to, and how to use the site safely as self-care background.

## A Calm Reference for a Loud Category {#why-this-site}

Search for almost any acupressure topic and the results split into two camps: clinic pages written to convert, and wellness posts written to excite. Between them sits very little a curious reader can trust to be measured, sourced, and calm. Acupoints.com began as the page we wanted to send friends when they asked which point someone meant — a single quiet reference where each of the 361 WHO-standardized acupoints has a stable home, a consistent identity, and prose that admits uncertainty instead of performing confidence.

The site is free to read and needs no account. Its foundations are deliberately unglamorous: a registry holds every point’s code, Chinese name, pinyin, characters, meridian, anatomical location, recorded actions, and dataset cautions, and presents them identically everywhere. The editorial layer you are reading adds prose only — context, history, and practical orientation — and never overrides those identity facts. If the two ever disagreed, the registry would win.

## How the Pages Are Built {#how-built}

Every page stands on three tiers of sourcing. The catalogue entries are compiled from an openly licensed dataset, aijiu-mpvue (MIT License), with in-house English wording that follows the nomenclature conventions of the WHO Standard Acupuncture Point Locations in the Western Pacific Region (2008). An older set of twenty editorial records is reviewed against that WHO standard and against A Manual of Acupuncture (Deadman, Al-Khafaji & Baker, second edition, 2007). The newest layer — pages like this one — is written in-house under a content policy the site publishes alongside the writing.

Standing habits follow from that structure. Each page lists its sources, and where a specific claim leans on one of them, the page says so inline. Contraindications recorded in the source data are carried onto the page prominently rather than buried, and the safety note at the foot of every article restates the universal limits of self-care. The catalogue records themselves are never edited by hand, so a correction happens once, in the record, and propagates everywhere.

## What We Claim, and What We Decline To {#claims-and-hedges}

Most assertions here wear traditional-use framing: a point is described as traditionally used for a purpose, or classically associated with a season, an emotion, or an element. Classical ideas arrive attributed generally — “classical texts describe…” — and are never dressed up as direct quotation. You will not find miracle promises, superiority rankings, percentages, or assured outcomes in these editorial pages. The tradition is interesting enough without embellishment, and readers deserve prose that respects their judgement.

Where modern research touches self-applied pressure at all, reviews suggest effects are modest and context-dependent, and the literature itself is candid about uneven study quality. That is the whole claim, and it is deliberately small. What self-acupressure reliably offers is slower breathing, a few minutes of quiet attention, and a low-cost ritual some people find pleasant — none of which competes with medical assessment, and none of which is offered as a substitute for it.

## Reading Your First Point Page {#first-point-page}

A newcomer usually arrives at a point page from a search result, so the page settles the essentials before any storytelling: the alphanumeric code, the Chinese name with its pinyin and characters, the parent meridian, and the anatomical location — all supplied by the registry and identical in both languages. Below that sit the recorded indications, any dataset cautions, and only then the editorial prose: the point’s classical roles, its neighbours along the channel, and gentle ways people explore it at home.

Orientation helps. The channels were drawn as continuous lines, and every point belongs somewhere on one. The lung channel, for instance, sweeps from zhongfu high on the chest down the inner arm to shaoshang beside the thumbnail — eleven catalogued points from end to end. Following a channel in order, rather than meeting points at random, is the easiest way to watch the tradition’s geography assemble itself — and the meridian overview pages on this site are built for exactly that kind of unhurried tour.

## Safety Before Everything {#safety-first}

Self-acupressure, as this site treats it, means fingers and palms only — never needles — and pressure that stays comfortable rather than firm. The recurring rules are few on purpose: avoid broken, inflamed, or numbed skin; stop at once if anything sharp, electric, or radiating appears; and treat pregnancy, an ongoing medical condition, or medication that affects bleeding as reasons to ask a qualified practitioner first. Dataset cautions are echoed on the relevant pages so they cannot scroll past unnoticed.

The limits deserve stating plainly too. Self-care is background comfort, not diagnosis, and a symptom that persists, worsens, or frightens you has earned professional attention whatever the internet suggests. Persistent symptoms belong with a licensed practitioner — Western medicine or East Asian medicine, whichever suits the complaint.

## Voice, Language, and Contact {#voice-and-contact}

The house voice is plain and grounded: no mystique, no exclamation marks doing the work of evidence, and Chinese terms defined on first use so a newcomer is never locked out. Every editorial module is written twice — once in English and once in Chinese, authored in Traditional characters with the Simplified edition derived from them — with matching structure, so neither language is a poor cousin. Spelling follows British conventions throughout, and dates, sources, and cautions are formatted the same way on every page.

Behind the site is a small editorial team; individual bylines will appear as contributors join. Corrections, source suggestions, and ordinary reader questions are welcome at editorial@acupoints.com — the inbox is read by the people who write the pages, and pointed criticism improves the next revision faster than praise does.
